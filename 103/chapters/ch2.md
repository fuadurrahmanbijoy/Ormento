:desc A household keeps a note of its money, and so does every business. This chapter shows how keeping a record grew into accounting, what accounting does, which branches it has, and who depends on the information that it gives.

## Salma's Notebook

> A family notices that its money disappears each month and cannot say where it went.

Salma and her husband earn Tk 40,000 a month between them. In March, Tk 9,000 was left at the end of the month, and they were pleased. In April, only Tk 3,000 was left. They looked at each other and asked the same question: where did the other Tk 6,000 go? Neither could answer. Salma remembered a school fee, a visit to a doctor and a large electricity bill, but she could not say how much each one cost. The money had left, and no trace remained.

> Salma starts a notebook, and after a month she can answer the question.

In May, Salma bought a small notebook. On the left page she wrote each amount that came in, with the date and its source. On the right page she wrote each amount that went out, with the date and its purpose. At the end of May she added each page. The right page showed that the electricity bill was Tk 5,500, almost double that of March, because the family had used a fan all night. She now knew where the money went, and she could plan a change.

> Salma's problem is also the problem of every business, but larger and shared by many people.

Think about what Salma's notebook did. It did not create money. It only recorded facts: what came in, what went out, when and why. Yet that small act gave her knowledge and control. A business has Salma's problem in a much larger form. Mitali's bakery will receive money from hundreds of customers and pay it to many suppliers and workers. Strangers will also want to read her notes: a banker, an officer of the government who checks the tax that her business must pay (section 2.3 explains tax), and a supplier. This chapter asks what her notes must contain, and for whom.

> The chapter answers the questions in order: what to record, what accounting is, which branches exist, and who reads the results.

Section 2.1 explains why records matter, starting with the household. Section 2.2 defines accounting and explains why it is called the language of business. Section 2.3 describes its branches. Section 2.4 names the people who use accounting information and shows what each one wants to know. Section 2.5 states the aims of accounting. Chapter 1 gave you the words *business*, *owner*, *customer*, *supplier*, *cash* and *on account*, and we shall use them freely.

## 2.1 Keeping Records: From the Household to the Business

> A record is a written note of something that happened, kept so that it can be checked later.

@ Record | A written note of something that happened, kept so that it can be checked later.

Salma's notebook is an example of a **record**. A record is a written note of something that happened, kept so that it can be checked later. A record has three features. It states a fact, such as "paid Tk 5,500 for electricity". It gives the date. And it is written, so that it lasts longer than a person's memory. Without the third feature, a note is only a thought.

> A household record serves four purposes: it shows where money goes, it helps planning, it settles arguments, and it reminds us of debts.

@! Go slowly here.

Why did Salma's record help her? It served four purposes. First, it showed where the money went. Second, it helped her to plan: once she saw the electricity cost, she could change her habits. Third, it settled arguments: if her husband said that the doctor's fee was Tk 1,000 and she remembered Tk 1,500, the page would decide. Fourth, it kept track of debts: if a neighbour borrowed Tk 500, the notebook remembered it when both people forgot.

> A good record answers four questions about each event: when, what, how much and with whom.

A good record answers four questions about each event. When did it happen? What happened? How much money was involved? With whom did the exchange take place? Salma's note "Tuesday: paid electricity Tk 5,500 to the power company" answers all four. A note such as "bills Tk 9,000" answers only one. If any answer is missing, a later reader may not be able to use the note. Businesses add a fifth question: was it paid in cash or on account?

> A month of Salma's notes can be read as a page of sums, which shows how a record answers her first question.

One month of Salma's notes can be read as a page of sums. In the table that follows, each row is one kind of spending, with its total for May. At the foot of the page, she compares what came in with what went out. This is the question that she could not answer in April.

:table Salma's page for May (amounts in taka)
| Item | Came in | Went out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pay from work | 40,000 | |
| Rent | | 12,000 |
| Food | | 14,000 |
| Electricity | | 5,500 |
| School | | 3,000 |
| Doctor | | 1,500 |
| Other | | 1,000 |
| Total | 40,000 | 37,000 |

> The page shows Tk 3,000 left, and the sum is checked by adding in the other direction.

*Given.* The table above. *Find.* The money left at the end of May. *Formula.* In words: money left equals money that came in minus money that went out. *Steps.* Money out: 12,000 + 14,000 = 26,000; add 5,500 to give 31,500; add 3,000 to give 34,500; add 1,500 to give 36,000; add 1,000 to give 37,000. Money left: 40,000 − 37,000 = 3,000. *Answer.* Tk 3,000 is left. *Check.* Add the money left to the money that went out: 3,000 + 37,000 = 40,000, which is the money that came in.

> A household record is small, written by one person, and read by a few.

Notice how simple Salma's task was. She was the only writer, and she wrote only a few events each day. Her only reader was her family. She did not need rules, because everyone in the house understood her notes. If she wrote "milk" on one day and "dairy" on another, nobody would be confused. The household record therefore needed few rules, because its writer and its readers were the same people.

:fig A household's page of notes beside a business's page of notes, showing how the same idea grows | fig05_household_business.svg

> The figure compares a household page with a business page and shows what is added as the number of events grows.

Read the figure from the left side to the right side. The left page is Salma's: a few lines, one writer, and simple words. The right page is a business's: many lines, several people writing, and columns that show whether an amount is cash or on account. The right page also shows who paid and who must pay. Everything on the left page is still there, but much more has been added, because more people need to understand it.

> A business needs records for the same four purposes, but on a larger scale and for more people.

A business needs records for the same four purposes. Mitali needs to know where the bakery's money goes and where it comes from. She needs to plan, for example how much flour to buy next week. She needs to settle arguments, for example with a customer who says that he paid for a cake in cash. And she needs to track debts, for example the amount that a school still owes her. The purposes are the same as Salma's; only the size has changed.

> A business differs from a household in the number of events, the number of people involved, and the strangers who read the records.

Three differences separate a business from a household. The first is volume: a bakery may record a hundred small sales in a day. The second is the number of people: several workers may handle money, so an error by one affects the whole. The third is the readers: people outside the business, who do not know the owner, may need to trust her notes. Strangers will accept a record only if it follows rules that they know. This leads to accounting.

> Writing a record also protects honest people, because it shows what really happened.

A record also protects people who are honest. If a worker is accused of taking Tk 2,000 from the till, a clear record of the day's sales and receipts may show that the money was never missing. If a supplier says that a bill was not paid, the owner's record may show the date of payment. A record is therefore not an insult to those involved. It gives each side a fair test of what really happened.

> A record is useful only if it is honest, so the people who write it must be trusted and their work must be checkable.

A record has value only if it is honest. A page can be written falsely as easily as truthfully. For this reason, businesses ask that the person who writes a record can be identified, and that another person can check it against evidence such as a receipt. Salma has no such need, because she trusts herself. But a business that holds money for many people must be able to show that its records are true, and Chapter 7 shows how a written receipt supports each entry.

> Records need not be on paper: a phone, a computer or a notebook can all hold them.

A record may be kept in many ways. Salma used a paper notebook, but a phone or a computer can hold the same notes. The material does not change the idea. What matters is that each fact is written in plain words, with a date, so that someone can check it later. Modern businesses use computers for most records, yet the principles in this book apply to every kind of record, whether on paper or on a screen.

> A short test of what a good record contains.
*Pause and check.* Salma writes only "bills Tk 9,000" in her notebook. Why is this note not a good record? What two things should she add?

> The recap states what a record is and why a business needs one.

*Before moving on.* A record is a written note of a fact, with a date, that can be checked later. Households and businesses keep records to see where money goes, to plan, to settle arguments and to remember debts. A business needs more rules, because more people write and read its records. *You should now be able to say:* "A record turns a fact into evidence."

## 2.2 What Accounting Is, and Why It Is Called the Language of Business

> Three different readers can read the same page of a business's records and understand it in the same way.

Imagine that Mitali has written a page about her bakery's year. A banker reads it, and so does a tax officer, and so does a supplier of flour. They have never met, and they have different aims. Yet all three understand that page in the same way. They agree on what is owned, what is owed, what was earned and what was spent. This shared understanding is the achievement of accounting, and it is the subject of this section.

> Accounting is the process of recording, sorting and reporting the money facts of an organisation.

@ Accounting | The process of recording, sorting and reporting the money facts of an organisation.

We now define the word. **Accounting** is the process of recording, sorting and reporting the money facts of an organisation. It has three parts. *Recording* means writing down each event, as Salma did. *Sorting* means placing like items together: all the money spent on flour in one group and all the money spent on rent in another. *Reporting* means summarising the groups into a short statement that a reader can use.

> Accounting turns many small events into a few clear reports.

Follow the three parts for the bakery. In a week, Mitali's bakery makes two hundred small sales and buys flour five times. Recording writes down all of these events. Sorting places the sales together and the flour purchases together. Reporting then states one sum for sales and one for flour. A reader who sees only two numbers does not need to read two hundred and five lines. This is how accounting turns many events into a few useful facts.

@ Accounting information | Facts about money, such as what a business owns, owes, earns and spends.

@! Go slowly here.

> Accounting information means facts about money that readers can use.

The facts that accounting produces are called **accounting information**. Accounting information means facts about money, such as what a business owns, owes, earns and spends. For example, "the bakery owns an oven" and "the bakery owes the supplier for flour" are both accounting information. Some people say *financial information*. Information is useful only if a reader can understand it and trust it. Chapter 3 shows how accounting protects trust.

> Bookkeeping is the part of accounting that records events in an orderly way.

@ Bookkeeping | The part of accounting that records transactions in an orderly way.

Many people use the words *accounting* and *bookkeeping* as if they meant the same thing. They do not. **Bookkeeping** is the part of accounting that records events in an orderly way. It is the daily work of writing each event in the right place. Accounting includes bookkeeping, and it also includes sorting, reporting and explaining what the figures mean. A bookkeeper keeps the diary of the business; an accountant uses the diary to write the year's report.

> The two jobs differ in skill and in purpose, though one person may do both in a small business.

The difference is one of scope, and not of importance. Bookkeeping must be accurate, because errors in the daily record spread into the reports. Accounting needs the same accuracy and also judgement. For example, an accountant must decide how to report a debt that a customer may never pay. In a small business, one person may do both jobs. In a large one, many bookkeepers record events, and a few accountants study the results.^1

:box IN SHORT
Bookkeeping records. Accounting records, sorts, reports and explains. Accounting information is the result that readers use.

> Following one day of a baker's sales shows the three parts of accounting in order.

Let us follow one Monday in a small bakery, to see all three parts at work. The baker makes three sales. She sells bread for Tk 300, paid in cash. She sells a cake for Tk 800, also paid in cash. And she delivers a catering order to a school for Tk 1,500, to be paid later, so this one is on account. The table that follows records the three events, one per row.

:table A baker's Monday: the three events recorded (amounts in taka)
| Event | Amount | How paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bread sold | 300 | Cash |
| Cake sold | 800 | Cash |
| Catering for a school | 1,500 | On account |

> Sorting places the cash sales together and the sale on account apart, and reporting states one total for each.

*Recording* is the table above: each event on its own row. *Sorting* places like items together. The two cash sales form one group, worth 300 + 800 = Tk 1,100. The sale on account forms another group, worth Tk 1,500. *Reporting* then states the result in a few lines: sales for the day were Tk 2,600, of which Tk 1,100 was received in cash and Tk 1,500 is still owed by the school. *Check.* 1,100 + 1,500 = 2,600, which equals 300 + 800 + 1,500. A reader of the report knows two useful facts at once: how much was sold, and how much is still to be collected.

> Accounting information is useful only when readers can understand it, when it bears on their choice, and when they trust it.

What makes accounting information good? Three qualities matter at this early stage. It must be understandable, so that a reader trained in the language can follow it. It must be relevant, so that it bears on the choice the reader must make. And it must be trustworthy, so that the reader believes it. A report that is clear but wrong is harmful. A report that is true but unreadable is useless. Chapter 3 describes the rules that protect these qualities.

> Accounting is often called the language of business, because it gives people common words for facts about money.

Accounting is often called the *language of business*. A language is a set of agreed words that lets people share ideas. If a Bangladeshi and a Chinese trader speak no language in common, they cannot discuss a sale. But if both read the same words for money facts, such as *profit* and *debt*, they can discuss the business even without a spoken language in common. Accounting provides such shared words and shared rules for the money facts of every business.

> The comparison with language is useful because it explains why accounting needs shared rules.

Why is the comparison useful? Because a language works only when everyone uses a word in the same way. If one baker uses *profit* to mean the money in the till and another uses it to mean earnings minus costs, they will misunderstand each other. Accounting fixes the meaning of such words. When a banker reads "profit Tk 127,000", she knows exactly what the figure means, however far she is from the bakery. This agreement lets strangers trust one another.

> A language must also be learned, and accounting is no exception, which is the purpose of this book.

A language must also be learned. A person who has never heard English cannot read an English page, however clear it is. In the same way, a person who has never studied accounting finds a page of figures difficult. This is not a fault in the reader. The words and rules of accounting must be taught, one at a time, and this book teaches them in order. By Chapter 12 you will read a business's reports as easily as a letter.

> The comparison also has limits: accounting says only what can be measured in money.

The comparison has limits. A spoken language can describe feelings, beauty and ideas. Accounting can describe only facts that have a money value. It can say that the bakery owns an oven worth a certain amount. It cannot say that the bakery's bread is delicious, or that its workers are kind. These facts matter, but they lie outside accounting. Chapter 3 calls this the *money measurement* rule. A reader who forgets the limit may think the reports tell the whole story, when they tell only part of it.

> A short test of recording, sorting and reporting.
*Pause and check.* Salma lists her month's spending, groups it into food, bills and school, and states a total for each group. Which parts of accounting did she perform: recording, sorting, reporting?

> The recap states what accounting is and why it is a language.

*Before moving on.* Accounting records, sorts and reports the money facts of an organisation. Bookkeeping is the recording part. Accounting information is the result. Accounting is called the language of business because it gives strangers common words and rules. *You should now be able to say:* "Accounting is a language for money facts, and it must be learned."

## 2.3 The Branches of Accounting

> Accounting has four main branches, each serving a different purpose.

A large business has many questions about money, and no single report can answer them all. For this reason, accounting has grown into several branches. Each branch gathers the same basic facts but arranges them for a different purpose. This section describes four branches and then explains a fifth activity that is related but different. We shall use the bakery to show how each one would work.

> Financial accounting reports the business's results to readers outside it.

@ Financial accounting | The branch that reports a business's results to people outside it.

The first branch is **financial accounting**. It is the branch that reports a business's results to people outside it. These people include banks, suppliers and the tax office. Financial accounting produces the statements that you will learn in Chapter 5: the income statement and the balance sheet. It follows fixed rules, so that every outside reader can understand it. Most of this book teaches financial accounting.

> Management accounting gives managers the information they need to run the business.

@ Management accounting | The branch that gives managers the information they need to run the business.

The second branch is **management accounting**. It is the branch that gives managers the information they need to run the business. Unlike financial accounting, it follows no fixed rules from outside, because it serves only the people inside the business. A baker might ask: which cake earns most for each hour of work? A report from management accounting can answer that question. It can also look forward as well as back.

> A budget is a plan of expected earnings and spending, and management accounting prepares it.

@ Budget | A plan that shows the money a business expects to earn and spend.

One tool of management accounting is the **budget**. A budget is a plan that shows the money a business expects to earn and spend. For example, Mitali might write in December that she expects to sell more bread in January and will need to buy more flour. Later she can compare the plan with what really happened. If the two differ, she asks why.

> Cost accounting measures what it costs to make a product or perform a service.

@ Cost accounting | The branch that measures what it costs to make a product or perform a service.

The third branch is **cost accounting**. It is the branch that measures what it costs to make a product or perform a service. How much does one loaf cost the bakery? The answer includes flour, sugar, gas, the baker's time and part of the oven's wear. A baker who does not know this cost may sell the loaf at a price that loses money. Cost accounting is most important for manufacturing businesses, as Chapter 1 explained.

> Tax is money that the government requires from people and businesses.

@ Tax | Money that the government requires people and businesses to pay.

Before the fourth branch, we need one more word. **Tax** is money that the government requires people and businesses to pay. The government uses taxes to build roads, run schools and pay many other costs. A business may pay tax on its profit, on its sales and on other items. The amount depends on the rules of the country.

> Tax accounting works out the tax a business must pay.

@ Tax accounting | The branch that works out the tax a business must pay.

The fourth branch is **tax accounting**. It is the branch that works out the tax a business must pay. It follows the tax law, which may differ from the rules of financial accounting. A tax accountant asks: which income is taxed, and which costs may be subtracted? Mitali's profit as shown in her reports may differ from the profit on which the law asks her to pay tax. Chapter 14 returns to this point.

Read the figure from the bottom to the top. At the base is a single set of records, where all events are written. From this base, four branches grow upward. Each branch takes the same facts and arranges them for a different purpose. Financial accounting reports to outsiders. Management accounting helps managers. > Auditing is an independent check that the accounts are fair and correct.

@ Auditing | An independent check that a business's accounts are fair and correct.

Related to these branches, but different, is **auditing**. Auditing is an independent check that a business's accounts are fair and correct. An auditor is a person outside the business who examines its records and reports whether they can be trusted. The check matters because outside readers cannot see the business's books for themselves. Auditing is not a fifth way of preparing accounts. It is a way of testing the accounts that others have prepared.

> Each branch makes its own kind of report, and the reports differ in form.

The branches also differ in the reports they make. Financial accounting makes a few formal statements at fixed times, usually once a year. Management accounting makes many informal reports, as often as the managers ask: daily, weekly or monthly. Cost accounting makes cost sheets for each product. Tax accounting makes the returns that the law requires. A bakery owner may read a daily sales note, a weekly budget check and a yearly set of statements, and each one comes from a different branch.

> Tax accounting and financial accounting may report different profits, because tax law has its own rules.

It may surprise you that two reports of the same year can show different profits. The tax law has its own rules about which costs may be subtracted and in which year. Financial accounting follows the rules of accounting. When the rules differ, the profit for tax may differ from the profit in the financial statements. Neither report is dishonest. They answer different questions, and each follows the rules of its own purpose.

> One person may work in several branches, and small businesses often use none of the specialist names.

In practice, the branches overlap. A small bakery has no separate cost department; the owner does cost accounting in her head and in a notebook. A bookkeeper may also prepare the tax return. The names tell us what kind of question is being asked, and not necessarily who answers it. This book teaches financial accounting in detail because every other branch builds on it.

> A reader should always ask which branch produced a report, because each branch follows different rules.

Why does it matter which branch produced a figure? Because the branches follow different rules and serve different readers. A profit figure prepared for the tax office may differ from one prepared for the bank. Neither is wrong; each is prepared for its own purpose. A careful reader asks first: for whom, and for what purpose, was this report made? That question prevents many misunderstandings.

> The recap lists the four branches and auditing.

*Before moving on.* Financial accounting reports to outsiders. Management accounting helps managers, and uses budgets. Cost accounting measures the cost of products. Tax accounting works out the tax due. Auditing is an independent check on accounts. *You should now be able to say:* "The branches use the same records for different purposes."

## 2.4 Who Uses Accounting Information, and for What

> Many different people read accounting information, and each wants to know something different.

@ User | A person who reads accounting information to help make a decision.

@ Decision | A choice between two or more actions, such as whether to lend money.

The people who read accounting information are called **users**. A user is a person who reads accounting information to help make a decision. A **decision** is a choice between two or more actions, such as whether to lend money. Two examples show the link. A banker reads the bakery's reports before deciding whether to lend. A supplier reads them before deciding whether to deliver flour on account. The information serves the choice.

> Users fall into two groups according to whether they work inside or outside the business.

@ Internal user | A user inside the business, such as the owner or a manager.

@ External user | A user outside the business, such as a bank or the tax office.

Users fall into two groups. An **internal user** is a user inside the business, such as the owner or a manager. An **external user** is a user outside the business, such as a bank or the tax office. The two groups need different information. Internal users need detail, to run the business from day to day. External users need a clear summary prepared by fixed rules, because they cannot see inside the business.

> The owner is the first internal user, deciding how to run the business and whether to continue.

Consider the users of the bakery one by one, starting inside. The first is Mitali, the owner. She decides what to bake, what price to charge, how much flour to buy and whether to hire another baker. She also decides how much to take out of the business for her own use and whether to expand. Her question is always the same: how is my business doing, and what should I do next?

> Employees are also internal users, because their pay and jobs depend on the business.

The bakery's staff are also users. A worker wants to know whether the bakery is healthy enough to pay wages and to keep her job. A worker may also ask for a share of a good year's profit. Some staff, such as a manager, use detailed reports to run a section. Others use only a short summary. Whether staff are called internal or external depends on the business; in this book, we count them as internal.

> Outside the business, suppliers and lenders are creditors, who want to know whether they will be paid.

@ Creditor | A person or business to whom money is owed.

Turning to outside users, we meet the creditors. A **creditor** is a person or business to whom money is owed. A supplier who delivers flour on account is a creditor, because the bakery owes the supplier. A bank that lends money is also a creditor. Both ask the same question: will the business pay me, and when? They study how much the business owns, how much it owes, and whether it earns enough.

> Investors and owners who are not managers want to know whether their money earns a good return.

@ Investor | A person who puts money into a business hoping to receive a return.

Another outside user is the **investor**. An investor is a person who puts money into a business hoping to receive a return. A friend who puts money into the bakery in exchange for a share of its profit is an investor. So is a person who buys shares in a large company. Investors ask: is the business earning enough profit, and is my money safe? In a company, the investors are its owners, so they read the reports closely.

> The tax authority is an outside user that checks whether the business has paid the right amount of tax.

The tax authority is another outside user. It asks whether the business has reported its earnings fairly, and whether it has paid the right amount of tax. It cannot visit every business each day, so it relies on the accounts. For this reason, the law of most countries requires businesses to keep records. A business that keeps poor records may have to pay more tax than it owes, or may be fined.

> Customers, and sometimes the public, also use the information to judge whether a business will last.

Customers are a further group of outside users. A customer who pays in advance for a wedding cake, or who relies on a bakery for regular supplies, wants to know that the business will survive. A school that signs a long order may check that the bakery is sound. The public also takes an interest in large businesses, because their success affects jobs, prices and the country's economy.

> A user without information must guess, and guessing leads to costly mistakes.

Think about what happens when a user has no information. A supplier who knows nothing about a bakery must either refuse credit, and lose a customer, or give credit blindly, and risk losing the goods. A bank that knows nothing must refuse to lend. A worker who does not know whether the business is healthy cannot plan for the future. Information reduces such guessing. This is why users ask for records, and why a business that cannot show them finds it harder to win trust.

> Accounting information shows the past, in money, with some estimates, so users must read it with care.

Users should also know the limits of accounting information. First, it describes what has already happened, so it cannot promise what will happen next. Second, it counts only facts with a money value. Third, some figures are estimates, such as how long an oven will last. A user who treats every figure as perfect may be misled. A careful user reads the information together with other facts, such as the state of the market and the skill of the people who run the business.

> The same facts must reach every user, even though the arrangement of the report differs.

An important point of honesty follows. The bakery's owner may be tempted to show a high profit to the bank, so that it will lend, and a low profit to the tax authority, so that she pays less tax. This is wrong, and in most countries it is a crime. Accounting may arrange the *same* facts differently for different users, but it must never report *different* facts to different users. Trust in accounting rests on this rule.

> A person can belong to more than one group of users, as the owner of a small business does.

One person may also belong to several groups. Mitali is the owner of the bakery. She also manages it, and she works in the kitchen. As owner she asks whether the business makes a profit. As manager she asks which products to make. As worker she asks whether the wages are paid. A small business often has an owner who is all three, and the owner then reads the same records in three different ways.

> The facts that different users want overlap, but the emphasis differs.

The users' needs overlap, yet the emphasis differs. A supplier cares most about debts and about speed of payment. A bank cares about earnings over many years, because it will be repaid over many years. The tax authority cares about the earnings of one year. The owner wants every kind of fact. A well-kept record holds all the facts, and reports can then be arranged for each user.

> A short test of internal and external users.
*Pause and check.* A baker is deciding whether to buy a second oven. Is she an internal or an external user? Which facts would she want?

> The recap lists the users and the two groups.

*Before moving on.* A user reads accounting information to make a decision. Internal users, such as the owner and staff, work in the business. External users, such as creditors, investors, the tax authority and customers, are outside it. *You should now be able to say:* "Different users make different decisions from the same records."

## 2.5 What Accounting Is Trying to Achieve: the Objectives of Accounting

> Accounting has four aims, and each one answers a need of the users.

Why does accounting exist at all? We can answer by drawing together what we have learned. Accounting has four main aims. They are to record, to report, to support decisions and to show accountability. Each one answers a need that we have already met. In this section, we study the four aims in order and show how each one helps the people who read accounting information.

> The first aim is to record, so that no important fact is lost.

The first aim is to record. This is the work of bookkeeping, which writes down each event so that no important fact is lost. Salma's notebook served this aim. Without a record, the other three aims cannot be met, because there is nothing to report or to use. For this reason, accurate recording is the foundation of everything in accounting.

> The second aim is to report, which means to summarise the records for readers.

The second aim is to report. Reporting means summarising the records into statements that readers can understand. Few people have time to read every event. Mitali's banker wants one page, not two hundred. The reports must be short, clear and prepared by agreed rules. Chapter 5 shows the first of these reports.

> The third aim is to support decisions, by giving users the facts they need before they choose.

The third aim is to support decisions. Every user in section 2.4 makes a choice, and the choice is better if the facts are good. A supplier who knows that a business pays late may ask for cash instead. A banker who sees steady profit may agree to lend. In each case, accounting does not make the decision; it supplies the facts, and the user decides. This is why accuracy and fairness matter so much.

> The fourth aim is accountability, which means being able to explain how you used what others trusted you with.

The fourth aim is to show accountability. Accountability means being able to explain how you used what others trusted you with. If a bank lends money, the owner must show how she used it. If investors give money to a company, its managers must show what they did with it. If members pay fees to a club, the committee must show how the fees were spent. Accounting provides the evidence for such explanations.

> The four aims depend on each other, and failing one weakens the rest.

The four aims depend on each other. If recording is careless, reports will be wrong. If reports are unclear, users will make poor decisions. And if the information is not honest, there is no real accountability. The aims therefore work as a chain, in which each link supports the next. A weakness in one link is a weakness in all. This is why accountants take great care with accuracy, with clarity and with honesty.

> A week at the bakery shows the four aims working one after another.

A week at the bakery shows the four aims in order. On Monday to Saturday, the bookkeeper records each sale, each purchase and each payment; this meets the aim to record. On Sunday, the accountant sorts the week's records and writes a short report of sales, spending and debts; this meets the aim to report. On the next Monday, Mitali reads the report and decides to buy more flour; this meets the aim to support decisions. At the end of the year, she shows the bank how she used its loan; this meets the aim of accountability.

> The aims explain why accounting follows rules, which Chapter 3 describes.

The aims also explain why accounting follows rules. A report that serves outside users must be trusted by people who cannot check it for themselves. Trust needs shared rules about what to record and how to report it. Without such rules, two accountants might produce different reports from the same facts, and users could not rely on either. Chapter 3 describes these rules and shows why they exist.

> The recap of the four aims.
*Before moving on.* Accounting aims to record events, to report them, to support users' decisions and to show accountability. Each aim relies on the one before it. *You should now be able to say:* "Accounting records facts, reports them, helps decisions and shows accountability."

*Check.* We test the answer by asking whether each reader's question matches a fact that the records hold. The bank's question needs facts about owning and owing, which the records hold. ## Summary

> The summary restates the chapter's main ideas in a few lines.

A record is a written note of a fact, kept so that it can be checked. Households and businesses keep records to see where money goes, to plan, to settle arguments and to track debts. Accounting records, sorts and reports the money facts of an organisation; bookkeeping is its recording part. Accounting is called the language of business because it gives strangers common words, but it can say only what has a money value. Its branches are financial, management, cost and tax accounting, with auditing as an independent check. Users, internal and external, read the same records to make different decisions. Accounting aims to record, to report, to support decisions and to show accountability.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five common mistakes about accounting are stated and corrected here.

*"Accounting and bookkeeping are the same."* Bookkeeping is the recording part; accounting also sorts, reports and explains. *"Accounting is only for large businesses."* Salma's notebook is a small form of the same idea, and every business needs it. *"Accounting information is only for the owner."* Banks, suppliers, the tax authority and others also depend on it. *"Accounting tells the whole truth about a business."* It reports only what can be measured in money. *"An audit is the same as accounting."* An audit is an independent check on accounts that others prepared. *"Accounting information is always exact."* Some figures, such as the life of an oven, are estimates, and the information describes only the past. *"Different users should receive different facts."* They may receive the same facts arranged in different ways, but never different facts.

## In History: Francesco Datini and His Account Books

> A merchant of the fourteenth century left behind records that still teach us how trade was kept.

Francesco Datini was a merchant who lived in Prato, a town near Florence in Italy, from about 1335 until his death in 1410. He traded across great distances and had businesses in several cities, among them Pisa, Genoa and Barcelona. A merchant with businesses in many places could not watch them all. He had to rely on written records, so that partners far away could see what had been bought, sold and owed.

> Centuries later, a surprising find proved how much he had written.

In 1870, workers altering the house where Datini had lived found sacks of papers in a disused stairwell. They held about 150,000 letters, more than 500 account books and ledgers, and hundreds of deeds of partnership and insurance policies.^2 Few merchants of the period left so complete a record. Historians have used it to study how medieval trade worked, and the Italian writer Iris Origo built her book *The Merchant of Prato* from it.

> The lesson is that records outlast their writers and can be read by strangers long afterwards.

The lesson matches this chapter. Datini wrote for readers in other cities and for the future, and strangers can still read his records six centuries later. They show a merchant's business, step by step. They also show why records matter: a record carries facts across distance and across time, to people who were not there. That is the purpose that accounting still serves, in every bakery and every bank.

^2: The figures follow the entry on Datini in *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. Counts of Datini's papers vary slightly between sources, so they are given as approximate.
