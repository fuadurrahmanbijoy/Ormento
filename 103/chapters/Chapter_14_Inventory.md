:chapter XIV | Inventory
:mood The same goods may carry different costs, yet the accounts must tell one coherent story.
:desc Inventory is more than goods sitting on a shelf. It is an asset whose assigned cost affects both the income statement and the balance sheet. This chapter follows goods from their arrival to their eventual sale, then asks how different cost-flow assumptions change the numbers reported.

> A box can be worth different amounts on paper without changing what is inside it.

Mitali Bakery has a shelf of identical cartons of bottled drinks. The cartons look alike, but they were bought at different prices. The first hundred cost Tk 200 each. Later cartons cost Tk 220 and then Tk 240. If three hundred cartons are sold, which cost belongs to the cartons sold, and which cost remains in the closing inventory? The physical goods cannot answer the accounting question by themselves. The accountant must use a consistent method for assigning cost. That choice affects cost of goods sold, gross profit, the carrying amount of inventory, and, where the tax rules use accounting figures, taxable profit. The goods have not changed. The story told by the accounts has.

> Inventory becomes important when a business holds goods across an accounting period.

A service business can often recognise its main revenue and expenses without keeping goods for resale. A merchandising business cannot. It buys goods before it sells them, and some of those goods may remain at the reporting date. The unsold goods are not treated as an expense merely because cash was paid for them. They remain an asset until the business consumes them in production or sells them. This timing is the foundation of inventory accounting.

## 14.1 What inventory is, and its kinds

> Inventory is a current asset because it is normally expected to be sold or used in the operating cycle.

@ Inventory | Goods held for sale, or materials held for making goods to sell.

**Inventory** means goods a business holds for sale or materials it holds to make goods for sale. A retailer may hold finished merchandise. A manufacturer may hold raw materials, partly completed goods, and finished goods. The classification therefore depends on what the business does with the item, not merely on what the item looks like.

> The word inventory describes an economic resource, while the accounting treatment depends on its role.

A carton of bottled water purchased by a shop for resale is **merchandise inventory**. The same carton bought by a factory for workers to drink is not merchandise inventory; it is more likely an operating supply or expense. Flour held by a bakery to make bread is a raw material. Dough partly prepared but not yet ready for sale is work in process. A packed loaf ready for customers is a finished good. The distinction matters because the cost of goods in each category travels through the accounts differently.

> Raw materials are inputs that have not yet become the finished product.

@ Raw materials | Basic materials a business will turn into products that it intends to sell.

In a manufacturing setting, raw materials enter production. Flour, sugar, eggs and packaging may be raw materials in a bakery when the accounting system is designed to trace manufacturing costs. The present running case uses a simplified treatment for the bakery’s ingredients in earlier chapters. This chapter concentrates on the bought-in merchandise at the drinks counter, where the goods are acquired for resale rather than manufacture.

> Work in process represents goods that have begun production but are not complete.

@ Work in process | Goods that are partly made and not yet ready to sell.

Work in process contains cost that belongs to incomplete production. A factory producing furniture may have timber cut and assembled into partly completed tables at the reporting date. The amount remains an asset because the business has not yet used the goods to earn the related sales revenue. Once the goods become finished and remain unsold, their classification changes to finished goods.

> Finished goods are completed products waiting for sale.

@ Finished goods | Goods that are complete and ready to sell.

A bakery’s packaged cakes prepared for customers may be finished goods. A manufacturer’s completed shirts in its warehouse are also finished goods. Their cost remains attached to inventory until sale, when the appropriate cost becomes part of cost of goods sold. This is one reason the income statement and balance sheet must be read together.

> Merchandise inventory differs from manufactured inventory because the business buys the goods in finished form.

@ Merchandise inventory | Goods bought by a business for resale without being transformed into its main product.

Mitali Bakery’s bottled drinks and packaged snacks are merchandise. The bakery does not make these goods; it buys them and resells them. Their purchase cost becomes inventory first. When the goods are sold, that cost moves to cost of goods sold. The sale price is revenue, while the assigned purchase cost is an expense.

> The classification can be understood as a journey from input to sale.

The diagram above is deliberately broad. It does not prescribe one manufacturing system for every business. Its purpose is to show the accounting question: at the reporting date, what goods remain, and what role do they play? A business that buys for resale usually has merchandise inventory. A manufacturer can have all three manufacturing categories. A service business may have little or no inventory.


### Inventory as a current asset

> Inventory is normally a current asset because the business expects to sell it or use it within its normal operating cycle.

@ Current asset | An asset expected to be realised, sold, or consumed within the normal operating cycle or within the relevant short-term period.

The classification connects inventory with Chapter 13’s classified balance sheet. Inventory is not presented merely as a number representing cash already spent. It represents an economic resource still available to produce future sales. That is why a purchase of goods does not automatically become an expense in the same period.

> The same purchase can therefore appear in different places at different times.

Suppose Mitali buys 100 cartons at Tk 200 each. The Tk 20,000 initially forms part of inventory. If all 100 cartons are sold, the Tk 20,000 becomes cost of goods sold. If only 40 are sold, the cost assigned to those 40 becomes cost of goods sold and the cost assigned to the remaining 60 stays in inventory. The timing is not a technical trick. It is the accounting expression of the difference between goods sold and goods still held.


## 14.2 Why inventory is kept, and the cost of too little or too much

> Businesses hold inventory because sales and supply do not always occur at the same moment.

A shop cannot wait for a customer to arrive before ordering every item. A restaurant cannot buy every ingredient after receiving each order. A manufacturer cannot begin production only after a customer has appeared. Inventory creates a bridge between supply and demand. It can also protect a business against delays, seasonal demand, uncertain deliveries and changes in purchasing conditions.

> Holding inventory has benefits, but it also ties up money and creates risks.

@ Holding cost | The cost of keeping inventory, including storage, insurance, handling, financing and the risk of loss.

Inventory occupies space and requires supervision. Some goods can spoil, become obsolete, be damaged, or lose value as customer preferences change. Money invested in unsold goods is not available for another use. Therefore, the question is not whether inventory is good or bad. The question is how much inventory is appropriate for the business and the nature of its demand.

> Too little inventory can create a stockout and interrupt sales.

@ Stockout | A situation in which a business has no available inventory of an item when customers want it.

A stockout can mean a lost sale, a delayed order, or a dissatisfied customer. A bakery that repeatedly runs out of a popular drink may lose the customer to another shop. In a manufacturing business, a missing raw material can stop production. The economic cost may exceed the purchase price of the missing item because the interruption affects the whole operation.

> Too much inventory creates a different set of costs.

Excess inventory may require additional storage, handling and insurance. It may also become obsolete or damaged. A product with a short shelf life can lose value before it is sold. Even when the goods remain usable, capital is committed to them. Effective inventory management therefore balances availability against the cost and risk of holding goods.

> Inventory decisions are operational decisions, but their consequences appear in accounting numbers.

A larger closing inventory generally means that less of the available product cost has been assigned to cost of goods sold for the period. A smaller closing inventory generally means that more cost has been assigned to goods sold. The accounting system therefore reflects a real operational question: how much stock remains and what does it cost?

:table Reasons for holding inventory and corresponding risks

| Reason | Benefit | Risk if excessive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meet ordinary demand | Goods are available when customers buy | Storage and capital tied up |
| Protect against delivery delays | Operations continue during interruptions | Older goods may become obsolete |
| Prepare for seasonal demand | Business can serve predictable peaks | Demand may be lower than expected |
| Buy in economical quantities | Fewer orders or better purchase terms may be possible | Large quantities increase exposure to loss |

> A useful accounting habit is to separate the physical question from the costing question.

First ask, “What goods are physically on hand?” Then ask, “What cost should be assigned to those goods?” A physical count answers the first question. A cost-flow method helps answer the second. Confusing the two causes many inventory errors. Counting 100 cartons does not, by itself, tell us whether the inventory cost is Tk 20,000, Tk 22,000, or another amount.


## 14.3 Counting and costing inventory

> Inventory accounting begins with a physical quantity, then assigns a monetary cost to that quantity.

@ Goods available for sale | Opening inventory plus purchases available for sale during the period.

If a business begins with 50 units and buys 350 more, it has 400 units available for sale before considering sales. If 300 units are sold, 100 units remain. The physical relationship is exact: goods available for sale = units sold + units in ending inventory. The cost relationship must also reconcile: cost of goods available for sale = cost of goods sold + cost of ending inventory.

:fig From goods available for sale to the two accounting results | figures/fig14_2_cost_flow.svg

> The term cost of ending inventory refers to the cost assigned to goods still on hand at the reporting date.

@ Cost of ending inventory | The amount of cost assigned to goods that remain unsold at the end of the period.

The figure appears as an asset in the balance sheet. It is not the same as the goods’ expected selling price. A carton bought for Tk 240 may remain in inventory at an assigned cost of Tk 240 under a basic cost model, even if the business intends to sell it for a higher amount. The chapter’s central problem is therefore cost assignment, not price prediction.

> Cost of goods available for sale is divided between ending inventory and cost of goods sold.

The relationship can be written as: **Cost of goods available for sale = Cost of goods sold + Cost of ending inventory.** Rearranging gives the familiar Chapter 13 expression: cost of goods sold = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory, when the system and definitions fit the periodic calculation. This identity is a powerful check because every unit of cost must have a destination.

> A physical count is essential because accounting records and physical goods can differ.

Goods may be damaged, misplaced, stolen, received without being recorded, or recorded but not yet received. The count establishes what is actually present. The accounting records then determine the appropriate cost information. A sound inventory count therefore has both a physical and an accounting dimension.


### Perpetual and periodic ideas

> A perpetual inventory system updates inventory records after purchases and sales, while a periodic system determines the closing amount at the end of a period.

@ Perpetual system | A system that updates the inventory record after every purchase and sale.

The word perpetual describes the frequency of updating, not a claim that the physical count is never needed. Even a business with detailed records may count inventory to verify the records. The methods taught in this chapter can be applied to cost assignment under the relevant inventory system, although the mechanics differ.

> The distinction matters because a method of cost flow is not the same thing as a system of recording.

FIFO, LIFO, weighted average and specific identification describe how costs are assigned. Perpetual and periodic describe how inventory information is maintained through the period. A student should not place these into one list as if they were four competing methods. They answer different questions.


## 14.4 The cost flow methods: specific identification, FIFO, LIFO, weighted average

> When identical units have different purchase costs, the accounts need a rule for deciding which costs belong to the units sold.

@ Cost flow assumption | A consistent rule used to assign available inventory costs between goods sold and goods remaining.

The physical movement of goods does not have to match the accounting flow of costs. A shop may sell the newest carton first while using FIFO for financial reporting. The cost-flow assumption is an accounting convention for assigning costs. It does not necessarily describe the physical order in which goods leave the shelf.

> Specific identification assigns the actual cost to each item when individual units can be identified reliably.

@ Specific identification | A method that assigns the actual recorded cost of each identifiable item to that item when it is sold.

This method suits goods that are distinct and individually traceable, such as unique pieces of jewellery, custom machinery, or individually identified high-value items. It is less practical for large quantities of interchangeable low-value goods because tracing every unit would cost more effort than the information is worth.

> FIFO assigns the oldest available costs to cost of goods sold first.

@ FIFO | A method that assigns the oldest inventory costs to goods sold first.

FIFO means first-in, first-out. Under this assumption, the earliest costs are treated as leaving the inventory first. The remaining inventory therefore tends to contain the more recent costs when prices have been rising. The method describes cost assignment, not necessarily the physical movement of goods.

> LIFO assigns the newest available costs to cost of goods sold first.

@ LIFO | A method that assigns the newest inventory costs to goods sold first.

LIFO means last-in, first-out. When prices rise, the newer costs are higher, so LIFO generally assigns a higher cost to goods sold and leaves a lower-cost older layer in ending inventory. The method is not permitted under every financial reporting framework, so a business must consider the standards that govern its reporting.

> Weighted average assigns an average cost per unit to the goods available for sale.

@ Weighted average cost | A method that gives units an average cost based on the total cost of goods available divided by total units available.

The method smooths the effect of different purchase prices. If 400 cartons cost Tk 89,000 in total, the average cost is Tk 222.50 per carton. If 300 cartons are sold, cost of goods sold is 300 × Tk 222.50 = Tk 66,750. The remaining 100 cartons carry Tk 22,250.

### The Mitali Bakery inventory case

> The March case uses three purchases of the same product at rising prices.

The drinks counter buys 100 cartons at Tk 200, 150 cartons at Tk 220, and 150 cartons at Tk 240. The business therefore has 400 cartons available. The total cost is Tk 20,000 + Tk 33,000 + Tk 36,000 = Tk 89,000. During March, 300 cartons are sold, leaving 100 cartons. The accounting problem is to allocate Tk 89,000 between the 300 cartons sold and the 100 cartons remaining.

:table March purchase layers before the sale

| Lot | Units | Unit cost | Total cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 100 | Tk 200 | Tk 20,000 |
| 2 | 150 | Tk 220 | Tk 33,000 |
| 3 | 150 | Tk 240 | Tk 36,000 |
| Total | 400 | — | Tk 89,000 |


### Specific identification

> Specific identification works only if the 300 cartons sold can be linked to particular purchase lots or individual units.

For example, suppose a business can prove that the 300 cartons sold consisted of all 100 cartons from the first lot, all 150 from the second lot, and 50 from the third lot. The cost of goods sold would then be Tk 20,000 + Tk 33,000 + Tk 12,000 = Tk 65,000. The remaining 100 cartons would be the remaining 100 cartons from the third lot, carrying Tk 24,000. The result happens to equal the planned FIFO result because the identified goods correspond to the oldest layers.


### FIFO

> Under FIFO, the 300 cartons sold are assigned the oldest available costs.

The first 100 cartons are assigned Tk 200 each, producing Tk 20,000. The next 150 are assigned Tk 220 each, producing Tk 33,000. The final 50 are assigned Tk 240 each, producing Tk 12,000. Total cost of goods sold is therefore Tk 65,000. The remaining 100 cartons are from the Tk 240 layer, so ending inventory is Tk 24,000. The check is exact: Tk 65,000 + Tk 24,000 = Tk 89,000.

:table FIFO calculation for 300 cartons sold

| Units sold | Cost assigned | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 100 × Tk 200 | Tk 20,000 |
| 150 | 150 × Tk 220 | Tk 33,000 |
| 50 | 50 × Tk 240 | Tk 12,000 |
| Total 300 | — | Tk 65,000 |

> The FIFO result leaves the newest cost in the closing inventory in this rising-price example.

The remaining 100 cartons are valued at Tk 240 each, giving Tk 24,000. This does not mean FIFO always produces a higher inventory figure. The direction of price movement matters. With falling prices, the relative result can reverse. The method must therefore be understood rather than memorised as “high” or “low”.


### LIFO

> Under LIFO, the 300 cartons sold are assigned the newest available costs first.

The first 150 cartons sold are assigned Tk 240 each, giving Tk 36,000. The next 150 are assigned Tk 220 each, giving Tk 33,000. Total cost of goods sold is Tk 69,000. The remaining 100 cartons are from the oldest layer at Tk 200 each, giving ending inventory of Tk 20,000. Again the total is Tk 89,000.

:table LIFO calculation for 300 cartons sold

| Units sold | Cost assigned | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 150 | 150 × Tk 240 | Tk 36,000 |
| 150 | 150 × Tk 220 | Tk 33,000 |
| Total 300 | — | Tk 69,000 |

> LIFO therefore gives a different allocation of the same total cost.

The physical count remains 300 cartons sold and 100 cartons on hand. Nothing about the goods changes. What changes is the cost attached to the sale and the cost attached to the closing asset. That difference flows into gross profit because gross profit is sales less cost of goods sold.


### Weighted average

> Weighted average begins by finding one average cost for every unit available.

Total cost is Tk 89,000 and total units are 400. Average cost per carton is Tk 89,000 ÷ 400 = Tk 222.50. Cost of goods sold is 300 × Tk 222.50 = Tk 66,750. Ending inventory is 100 × Tk 222.50 = Tk 22,250. The two amounts again total Tk 89,000.

:table Weighted-average calculation

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average cost | Tk 89,000 ÷ 400 | Tk 222.50 |
| COGS | 300 × Tk 222.50 | Tk 66,750 |
| Ending inventory | 100 × Tk 222.50 | Tk 22,250 |

> The three methods can now be compared without treating one as universally correct.

The choice depends on the nature of the goods, the reporting framework, the information system, and the consistency required by the applicable rules. Specific identification can be informative for individually traceable goods. FIFO provides a particular cost-flow pattern. LIFO provides another pattern where permitted. Weighted average reduces the effect of individual price layers.

:table March comparison under rising purchase prices

| Method | COGS | Ending inventory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specific identification in the illustrated selection | Tk 65,000 | Tk 24,000 |
| FIFO | Tk 65,000 | Tk 24,000 |
| LIFO | Tk 69,000 | Tk 20,000 |
| Weighted average | Tk 66,750 | Tk 22,250 |

> A crucial check is that every method must allocate the same Tk 89,000 total cost.

The numbers differ between cost of goods sold and ending inventory, but their sum must remain Tk 89,000. If a calculation gives Tk 65,000 of cost of goods sold and Tk 22,250 of ending inventory, the total is Tk 87,250 and the allocation is incomplete. The error is not a matter of method; it is a failure to account for all available cost.


## 14.5 Comparing the methods: effect on profit and tax

> When purchase prices rise, the method that assigns higher recent costs to goods sold produces a lower gross profit, all else equal.

Suppose sales revenue is Tk 100,000 and there are no other operating expenses for the illustration. FIFO COGS of Tk 65,000 produces gross profit of Tk 35,000. LIFO COGS of Tk 69,000 produces gross profit of Tk 31,000. Weighted average COGS of Tk 66,750 produces gross profit of Tk 33,250. The difference comes entirely from cost allocation.

:table Illustrated effect when selling price is held constant

| Method | Sales | COGS | Gross profit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FIFO | Tk 100,000 | Tk 65,000 | Tk 35,000 |
| LIFO | Tk 100,000 | Tk 69,000 | Tk 31,000 |
| Weighted average | Tk 100,000 | Tk 66,750 | Tk 33,250 |

> The same logic explains why inventory methods can affect tax comparisons, but accounting method and tax law are not the same thing.

@ Taxable profit | The profit figure on which a business is required to pay income tax under the applicable tax rules.

If taxable profit is based on an accounting profit measure, a higher cost of goods sold can reduce the profit base, while a lower cost of goods sold can increase it. But the actual tax result depends on the tax law and its permitted inventory methods. A textbook comparison should therefore show the mechanical effect without presenting it as legal advice.

> In the United States, LIFO remains part of the federal tax system under specific rules, illustrating why tax treatment must be checked rather than assumed.

The U.S. Internal Revenue Service explains that LIFO has specific adoption and use rules and that inventory valuation affects taxable income. Its guidance also states that, during rising prices, LIFO generally produces higher cost of goods sold and lower closing inventory than FIFO. These observations are used here only as an international illustration; they do not establish the rules applicable to a Bangladeshi taxpayer.^1

> For a Bangladeshi business, the applicable financial reporting and tax rules must be checked separately before selecting or documenting an inventory method.

The safe accounting principle is consistency with the governing framework. A method should not be selected merely because one year’s arithmetic appears favourable. The financial reporting framework, tax legislation, administrative rules, industry practice and the business’s information system can all matter. Where the rules are uncertain or have changed, the accountant should consult the current authoritative requirements rather than rely on an old textbook statement.

> Rising and falling prices provide a useful thought experiment because they expose the mechanism.

When prices rise, FIFO tends to leave newer, higher costs in ending inventory and assigns older, lower costs to goods sold. LIFO does the reverse where it is permitted. When prices fall, the direction of the difference changes. Weighted average remains between the relevant cost layers in a simple pooled example. Specific identification depends on which actual units are identified as sold.


### Consistency and comparability

> A method becomes meaningful when it is applied consistently and explained when a permitted change occurs.

Consistency does not mean that a business can never change a method. It means that accounting choices should not be changed casually from one period to another merely to manipulate the reported result. Consistent application improves comparison across periods. If a permitted change occurs, the relevant reporting rules determine how it is accounted for and disclosed.

> Comparability also requires the reader to understand that identical-looking profit figures can contain different inventory assumptions.

Two businesses with the same sales and purchases may report different gross profits if they use different permitted cost-flow assumptions while prices are changing. That does not automatically mean that one set of records is wrong. The reader must know the method and understand its effect.


## Worked case: Mitali Bakery in March

> The March case shows why inventory accounting is an allocation problem rather than a counting problem.

Situation: Mitali Bakery has 400 cartons available, purchased in three lots at Tk 200, Tk 220 and Tk 240. Question: what amount belongs to cost of goods sold and what amount remains in inventory after 300 cartons are sold? Reasoning: first establish total available cost, then apply the stated cost-flow assumption. Conclusion: FIFO gives COGS Tk 65,000 and ending inventory Tk 24,000; LIFO gives Tk 69,000 and Tk 20,000; weighted average gives Tk 66,750 and Tk 22,250. Check: each pair totals Tk 89,000.

> The case also demonstrates why the phrase “inventory value” needs care.

The accounting amount is a cost assigned under the chosen method. It is not automatically the market price, replacement price, or expected selling price. Other financial reporting rules may require additional measurement considerations in particular circumstances. Those rules are beyond this introductory cost-flow exercise, so the student should not treat the four methods as a complete theory of every possible inventory measurement issue.


## A second inventory calculation: falling prices

> The direction of price movement changes the comparison, so the rule must be tested rather than memorised.

Suppose a different business begins with 100 units at Tk 300 and buys 100 units at Tk 260. It sells 120 units. Under FIFO, the first 100 units sold carry Tk 300 each and the next 20 carry Tk 260 each. Cost of goods sold is therefore Tk 35,200. The remaining 80 units carry Tk 20,800. Under LIFO, the first 100 units sold carry Tk 260 each and the next 20 carry Tk 300 each, producing COGS of Tk 32,000. The remaining 80 units carry Tk 24,000. The total available cost is Tk 56,000 under either method.

> Falling prices reverse the direction of the simple FIFO–LIFO comparison.

The example shows why statements such as “FIFO always gives higher profit” are incomplete. The result depends on the direction and pattern of cost changes, as well as the quantity sold. The method is a rule for assigning costs, not a permanent ranking of reported profit. Weighted average would fall between the relevant cost layers in this two-price illustration, while specific identification would depend on which units were actually identified as sold.

### Two-stage sales

> A second sale tests whether the cost-flow rule is applied continuously rather than only once.

Return to the Mitali Bakery data. The business has 400 cartons available: 100 at Tk 200, 150 at Tk 220 and 150 at Tk 240. Suppose the first sale is 180 cartons and the second sale is 120 cartons. Under FIFO, the first sale uses 100 cartons at Tk 200 and 80 at Tk 220, giving COGS of Tk 37,600. The remaining layers are 70 cartons at Tk 220 and 150 at Tk 240. The second sale of 120 cartons therefore uses 70 at Tk 220 and 50 at Tk 240, giving Tk 27,400. Total COGS remains Tk 65,000 and ending inventory remains Tk 24,000.

> The two-stage calculation reaches the same total because the same 300 units have ultimately been sold.

This is an important check. A cost-flow method does not change because the sale is split into two invoices. If the same 300 units are sold during the same period and the same method is applied consistently, the total cost assigned to those units should agree with the one-stage calculation. The timing of the entries may differ, but the final allocation should not depend on the number of invoices.

### A weighted-average exercise with a new business

> Weighted average becomes easier when quantity and total cost are kept separate until the average is calculated.

Consider a small stationery trader. It has 60 notebooks costing Tk 80 each and buys 90 more at Tk 100 each. Goods available are 150 units. Total cost is Tk 4,800 + Tk 9,000 = Tk 13,800. The weighted-average unit cost is Tk 13,800 ÷ 150 = Tk 92. If 100 notebooks are sold, COGS is Tk 9,200 and the 50 remaining notebooks carry Tk 4,600. The check is Tk 9,200 + Tk 4,600 = Tk 13,800.

> The average is weighted because the two purchase quantities are not equal.

A simple average of Tk 80 and Tk 100 would be Tk 90, but that would ignore the quantities purchased. The business bought 90 units at Tk 100 and only 60 at Tk 80. The higher-priced group therefore has greater weight in the calculation. Weighted average is not the arithmetic mean of the prices; it is the total cost divided by total units.

### What specific identification can and cannot do

> Specific identification is strongest when the individual units are economically distinct and traceable.

Imagine a business selling three custom machines. Machine A cost Tk 400,000, Machine B Tk 550,000, and Machine C Tk 700,000. If Machine B is sold, the cost attached to that sale can be identified directly as Tk 550,000. There is no need to decide whether the oldest or newest machine is treated as sold. The actual unit is known.

> The method becomes burdensome when thousands of interchangeable units must be traced individually.

A supermarket may have thousands of identical packets of a common product. Recording the exact cost of each packet as it leaves the shop may require excessive tracking. A systematic cost-flow assumption can provide a practical alternative. The accounting method should therefore fit the nature of the inventory and the information available.

### Inventory errors and the income statement

> An inventory error can affect more than one accounting period because closing inventory becomes opening inventory later.

Suppose ending inventory is understated by Tk 5,000 while all other figures are correct. In the current period, cost of goods sold is overstated by Tk 5,000 because the formula subtracts too small a closing inventory amount. Gross profit and profit are therefore understated by Tk 5,000 before considering tax effects. In the following period, if the error is not corrected, the understated opening inventory can cause cost of goods sold to be understated and the later profit to be overstated by the same amount, subject to the accounting system and other corrections.

> The same inventory number can therefore have a current-period and a next-period consequence.

This is why inventory counts and cost calculations are part of the period-end control process. A wrong closing figure does not remain isolated on the balance sheet. It enters the income statement through cost of goods sold and then travels into the next accounting period as opening inventory. The student should therefore treat the closing inventory figure as both an asset amount and a component of profit measurement.

## Summary

> Inventory is an asset until its assigned cost becomes an expense.

Inventory represents goods held for sale or materials held for making goods for sale. Merchandise, raw materials, work in process and finished goods describe different roles.

> Physical quantity and monetary cost are separate questions.

A count establishes what is present. A cost-flow method determines how available cost is allocated between goods sold and goods remaining.

> The four methods assign cost differently.

Specific identification follows identifiable units; FIFO assigns oldest costs first; LIFO assigns newest costs first where permitted; weighted average uses an average unit cost.

> The total available cost must always reconcile.

Cost of goods sold plus ending inventory must equal the cost of goods available for sale in the basic allocation exercise.

> Price movement affects the comparison.

With rising prices, FIFO, LIFO and weighted average can produce different gross profits and ending inventory amounts because they attach different costs to the sale and the remainder.

> Tax treatment must be checked under the applicable law.

Accounting mechanics can show the direction of an effect, but the actual taxable result depends on current tax rules and permitted methods.


## Think it through

> The first question is always physical: what is on hand at the reporting date?

Imagine 600 units are available and 425 are sold. Before assigning any monetary cost, state the number of units remaining. Then identify which purchase layers exist and which method you are being asked to apply. This prevents a cost calculation from hiding a quantity error.

> The second question is allocation: which costs are assigned to the units sold?

If there are three purchase lots, write the lots in order and mark the number of units consumed under the selected method. Do not multiply until the quantity assigned to each layer is known. This method makes FIFO and LIFO almost mechanical.

> The third question is reconciliation: where did every taka of available cost go?

After calculating cost of goods sold and ending inventory, add them. The result must equal total goods-available cost in the basic exercise. If it does not, return to the layer quantities rather than changing the final answer to force agreement.


## Where people go wrong

> A common error is to treat FIFO as a physical instruction.

FIFO is a cost-flow assumption. A retailer may place new cartons at the front of a shelf and sell them first while still using FIFO for cost assignment if the accounting rules permit. The accounting method and physical movement are related concepts but are not identical.

> Another error is to confuse a method with an inventory system.

Perpetual describes continuous updating of inventory records. FIFO describes a cost-flow assumption. A question asking for FIFO is not asking whether the company keeps perpetual records unless the question says so.

> A third error is to compare profit without holding sales and other expenses constant.

Inventory methods change cost of goods sold. To isolate that effect, keep revenue and all other relevant expenses unchanged in the illustration. Otherwise, a difference in profit may have several causes and the inventory effect becomes unclear.


## In history

> The history of inventory methods shows why accounting choices can become connected with taxation.

The United States developed formal tax rules for LIFO, and the method remains subject to detailed federal requirements. Current IRS guidance describes LIFO adoption and the effect of price changes on FIFO and LIFO results. The historical point is not that one country’s tax rule governs another country’s accounting. It is that inventory costing has long had consequences beyond the warehouse ledger.^1
