:chapter XV | Special Journals
:mood When one kind of transaction appears again and again, repeating the whole journal entry can hide the pattern.
:desc A busy business may record dozens of purchases, sales and cash movements in a month. A single general journal can contain them, but repeated work can make posting slower and errors harder to locate. Special journals divide the work by transaction type while preserving the double-entry system.

> A journal can be correct and still be a poor working tool when every transaction crowds into one list.

Imagine Mitali Bakery in April. The drinks counter has begun selling to several institutional buyers on account. Suppliers send repeated invoices for merchandise. Customers pay by cash or cheque. The owner pays suppliers, pays expenses, and receives collections from customers. If every transaction is written in one general journal, the book becomes a long stream of similar entries. The accounting remains possible, but the repeated work becomes expensive. The solution is not to abandon the general journal. It is to divide repeated transactions into special journals and post them systematically.

> Special journals are an organisational development of the ordinary journal, not a different accounting system.

The same double-entry principle remains. Each transaction still has a debit and a credit, and total debits must equal total credits. What changes is where a repeated class of transaction is recorded and how the periodic totals are posted. The purpose is efficiency, clearer responsibility, and easier checking.

## 15.1 The puzzle of the crowded journal: why special journals

> A special journal is a journal designed for a recurring class of similar transactions.

@ Special journal | A journal used for one kind of repeated transaction.

A purchases journal may record merchandise bought on account. A sales journal may record merchandise sold on account. Cash transactions can be separated into cash receipts and cash disbursements. The precise set of journals depends on the size and nature of the business. A small enterprise may use fewer books; a larger enterprise may use more detailed subdivisions.

> The main advantage is repetition with structure.

When a business records twenty credit purchases, the purchases journal provides one familiar layout for all twenty. The clerk does not redesign the entry each time. The supplier, date, invoice and amount appear in predictable columns. At the end of the period, the journal can be totalled and the totals posted to the appropriate general ledger accounts.

> Special journals also support division of labour.

One employee may handle purchases, another sales, and another cash receipts. Each can work within a defined book. The arrangement can improve speed, but it also creates a control requirement: the totals from the separate journals must reconcile with the general ledger and subsidiary records. Division of work without reconciliation would merely divide the opportunity for error.

> A transaction belongs in a special journal only when it meets the journal’s rule.

A purchase of merchandise for cash does not belong in a purchases journal designed for credit purchases. A credit sale of merchandise belongs in the sales journal, while a cash sale belongs in the cash records. The transaction’s substance, not the name of the document, determines the book.

| Journal | Main use | Typical entry pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchases journal | Merchandise bought on account | Purchases/Inventory Dr; Accounts payable Cr |
| Sales journal | Merchandise sold on account | Accounts receivable Dr; Sales Cr |
| Cash receipts journal | Cash received | Cash Dr; relevant account Cr |
| Cash disbursements journal | Cash paid | Relevant account Dr; Cash Cr |
| General journal | Transactions outside the special-journal rules | Depends on transaction |


## 15.2 Subsidiary ledgers and control accounts

> Special journals become more useful when individual customer and supplier accounts are kept in subsidiary ledgers.

@ Subsidiary ledger | A ledger containing the individual accounts that support one controlling general-ledger balance.

Suppose Mitali sells to ten institutional customers on account. The general ledger does not need ten separate accounts inside the main control structure if a subsidiary accounts-receivable ledger stores each customer’s balance. The general ledger can maintain one Accounts Receivable control account representing the total. The subsidiary ledger then answers the detailed question: which customer owes what amount?

:fig Individual customer accounts support one accounts-receivable control account | figures/fig15_2_control_account.svg

> A control account is a general-ledger account whose balance should agree with the total of the related subsidiary ledger.

@ Control account | A general-ledger account showing the total represented by a related subsidiary ledger.

If Customer A owes Tk 18,000, Customer B owes Tk 27,000 and Customer C owes Tk 15,000, the subsidiary ledger total is Tk 60,000. The Accounts Receivable control account should also show Tk 60,000 after all relevant postings. The two records provide different levels of detail, but they describe the same total.

> The same principle applies to accounts payable.

Individual supplier accounts show what the business owes to each supplier. The Accounts Payable control account shows the total amount owed to all suppliers. If the control balance and subsidiary total disagree, the accounting system has a reconciliation problem. The difference must be investigated rather than ignored.

> A folio or reference column helps trace an entry from one book to another.

@ Folio | A reference column that identifies where an entry has been posted or where its supporting record can be found.

A reference may point from a special journal to a subsidiary ledger page or account code. The exact format varies between systems. Its purpose is traceability. An accountant should be able to move from a total to its supporting entries and from an individual entry back to its source.

> Control accounts therefore turn special journals into a system of linked records rather than isolated notebooks.

The purchases journal supports purchases and supplier balances. The sales journal supports sales and customer balances. Cash records support the cash and bank balances. The general ledger remains the central accounting record, while subsidiary records provide detail. This structure is one reason a business can handle a large number of transactions without placing every detail on one page.


## 15.3 The purchases journal

> The purchases journal records merchandise bought on account when the business uses a periodic purchases approach.

@ Purchases journal | A special journal for merchandise bought on account.

The key condition is that the transaction is both a purchase of merchandise and a credit purchase. A cash purchase goes into the cash records. The purchase of an office computer on account is not merchandise if the business is buying it for use; it belongs in the general journal or another designated record. The definition therefore prevents overuse of the purchases journal.

> A typical purchases-journal row records the supplier, invoice reference and amount, then supports the appropriate subsidiary and control postings.

For an introductory periodic system, a purchase of merchandise on account is recorded as Purchases debit and Accounts Payable credit. The supplier’s individual account receives a credit. At the end of the period, the purchases journal total is posted to the general ledger Purchases account and the total credit is posted to Accounts Payable control. The exact columns can be expanded for freight, tax or other classifications when the accounting system requires them.

| 18 Apr | Fresh Pack Ltd | FP-77 | Tk 15,000 |

> The three April purchases illustrate why a repeated layout saves work.

Each row describes the same class of event: merchandise acquired on account. The total of Tk 57,000 is the period’s credit-purchase amount in this illustration. The supplier accounts would receive their individual credits, while the control account receives the total credit. The purchases account receives the corresponding debit under the periodic approach.


### Purchase returns and cash discounts

> A purchase return is not another purchase and therefore should not be treated as an ordinary purchases-journal row.

When merchandise is returned to a supplier, the business reduces the amount owed or receives another agreed settlement. The exact record depends on the system, but the important conceptual point is that a return reverses part of the original purchase relationship. It must remain traceable to the supplier and the original transaction.

> A cash discount received from a supplier is different from a trade discount deducted before recording the invoice.

@ Discount received | A cash discount that the business obtains from a supplier for meeting stated payment conditions.

A trade discount normally reduces the invoice price before the accounting amount is recorded. A cash discount depends on payment timing and is recorded when the business qualifies for and takes it. Students should therefore avoid treating every reduction in an invoice as the same kind of discount.


## 15.4 The sales journal

> The sales journal records merchandise sold on account under the relevant system.

@ Sales journal | A special journal for merchandise sold on account.

The buyer becomes a debtor of the business, so Accounts Receivable is debited and Sales is credited in the basic periodic model. The customer’s individual subsidiary account receives the debit. At the end of the period, the sales total is posted to the Sales account and the total receivable amount is posted to the Accounts Receivable control account.

> A cash sale does not belong in the sales journal when the journal is defined for credit sales.

The word sales describes the revenue event, but the special-journal classification depends on how the transaction is settled. Cash sales are recorded through the cash-receipts side of the cash records. This distinction is important because the same revenue account can receive postings from more than one journal.

:table Illustrative April sales journal

| Date | Customer | Invoice | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 Apr | Rajshahi College Canteen | S-031 | Tk 16,000 |
| 12 Apr | City Hospital Cafeteria | S-032 | Tk 22,000 |
| 25 Apr | North Bank Staff Club | S-033 | Tk 12,000 |
| Total | — | — | Tk 50,000 |

> The customer ledger provides the detail behind the Tk 50,000 sales-journal total.

Rajshahi College Canteen has a debit of Tk 16,000, City Hospital Cafeteria Tk 22,000, and North Bank Staff Club Tk 12,000. The total of these individual receivables is Tk 50,000. The Accounts Receivable control account should therefore receive the same total debit from the sales journal, subject to subsequent collections and adjustments.


### Sales returns and allowances

> A sales return is recorded outside the ordinary sales-journal rule because it reduces the original sale rather than creating a new sale.

The return must be linked to the customer’s account and to the appropriate sales-return account or equivalent treatment under the chosen system. The conceptual sequence is important: sale creates the receivable and revenue; return reduces the customer’s obligation and reduces the related sales measure. The special journal should not become a place where every sales-related event is mixed together.


## 15.5 The cash book: receipts, payments and the bank column

> Cash transactions are frequent enough to deserve their own organised record.

@ Cash receipts journal | A special journal for cash received.

Cash received can come from cash sales, collections from customers, owner contributions, loans, or other sources. The cash account is debited when cash increases. The other account credited depends on the source. A structured cash-receipts journal allows repeated transactions to be grouped and totalled.

> Cash payments are likewise grouped because the same cash account appears in many different transactions.

@ Cash disbursements journal | A special journal for cash paid out.

Cash paid may settle supplier accounts, operating expenses, loans, or purchases of assets. Cash is credited when it decreases. The account debited depends on what the payment represents. The journal therefore needs columns that make common categories easy to identify without removing the underlying double-entry logic.

> A cash book can combine the receipts and payments records with a running cash and bank balance.

@ Cash book | A book that records cash received and cash paid, and often the bank balance.

The cash book can function as both a journal and a ledger for cash-related transactions. Its structure varies. A simple two-column arrangement may separate cash and bank. Additional columns can record discounts or other recurring categories. The design should match the transactions the business actually encounters.

:fig The cash book connects receipts, payments and bank movements | figures/fig15_3_cash_book.svg

> Discounts allowed and discounts received can be shown in cash records when the system includes them.

@ Discount allowed | A cash discount that the business gives to a customer.

Suppose a customer owes Tk 10,000 and pays Tk 9,800 within the discount period. The business receives Tk 9,800 and allows Tk 200 as a discount. The cash-receipts record can therefore show the cash received and the discount allowed, while the customer’s account is cleared for Tk 10,000. The exact column design depends on the cash book used.

> The bank column is particularly useful because the bank balance becomes a major control point.

Deposits into the bank increase the bank column. Cheques and other bank payments reduce it. The cash book’s bank balance is later compared with the bank statement in Chapter 16. That later reconciliation is easier because the cash book has already separated bank-related movements from other transactions.

The illustrative cash receipts total is Tk 50,000 and the illustrative disbursements total is Tk 46,500. If the opening bank balance were Tk 400,000 and all these amounts passed through the bank column, the resulting balance would be Tk 403,500. In a real cash book, cash-on-hand and bank movements may be separated, so the calculation must follow the actual columns rather than treating every receipt as a bank deposit.


## 15.6 Posting from special journals; what the general journal still records

> Special journals save work because their periodic totals can be posted systematically to the general ledger.

Suppose the April purchases journal total is Tk 57,000. Under the periodic model, the Purchases account receives a debit of Tk 57,000 and Accounts Payable control receives a credit of Tk 57,000. The individual supplier accounts receive their own credits. The general ledger therefore contains the total, while the subsidiary ledger contains the detail.

> The sales journal works by the same principle in the opposite direction.

A Tk 50,000 sales-journal total produces a debit of Tk 50,000 to Accounts Receivable control and a credit of Tk 50,000 to Sales. Individual customer accounts receive their corresponding debits. The equality of debits and credits is preserved even though the detailed rows and the general-ledger totals are stored in different places.

> The general journal remains necessary for transactions that do not fit the special journals.

Adjusting entries, correcting entries, closing entries, depreciation entries, unusual non-cash transactions, and certain transfers may remain in the general journal. A special-journal system is not designed to eliminate the general journal. It is designed to prevent repeated transactions from overwhelming it.

> Posting is therefore a controlled movement of information rather than a second recording of the same event.

The source document supports the transaction. The special journal records it in the appropriate repeated format. The subsidiary ledger receives individual detail where needed. Periodic totals move to control and general-ledger accounts. The chain should remain traceable from source to final balance.

| Book | Detail posted to | General-ledger effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchases journal | Supplier accounts | Purchases Dr; Accounts Payable control Cr |
| Sales journal | Customer accounts | Accounts Receivable control Dr; Sales Cr |
| Cash receipts journal | Relevant cash-source accounts | Cash/Bank Dr; source account Cr |
| Cash disbursements journal | Relevant payment accounts | Expense/asset/liability Dr; Cash/Bank Cr |
| General journal | Accounts affected by unusual/non-repeating entries | According to the individual entry |


## Worked case: Mitali Bakery in April

> The April case puts all four recurring books beside one another.

Situation: Mitali Bakery has begun dealing with institutional buyers and several merchandise suppliers. Question: how can the business record repeated purchases, credit sales and cash movements without writing every event in one general journal? Reasoning: apply the transaction-class rules first, then record each qualifying event in the relevant special journal, maintain individual customer and supplier detail in subsidiary ledgers, and post periodic totals to the general ledger. Conclusion: the April records can be organised into purchases, sales and cash records while the general journal remains available for transactions outside those classes. Check: the control accounts must agree with the totals of their subsidiary ledgers.

> The following April figures are fixed for this draft so that the case can be carried forward into Chapter 16.

Case file, illustrative. Credit merchandise purchases are Tk 57,000: Rahman Wholesale Tk 18,000, City Foods Tk 24,000, and Fresh Pack Ltd Tk 15,000. Credit merchandise sales are Tk 50,000: Rajshahi College Canteen Tk 16,000, City Hospital Cafeteria Tk 22,000, and North Bank Staff Club Tk 12,000. Illustrative cash receipts are Tk 50,000 and illustrative cash disbursements are Tk 46,500. The cash-book bank balance will therefore depend on which of these receipts and payments actually pass through the bank column; the examples above treat the totals as bank movements only when explicitly described as such.

> A control check is stronger than a visual impression that the books look complete.

If the supplier subsidiary accounts show credits of Tk 18,000, Tk 24,000 and Tk 15,000, their total is Tk 57,000. The Accounts Payable control account should contain the corresponding total credit from the purchases journal before subsequent settlements. If the customer subsidiary accounts show debits of Tk 16,000, Tk 22,000 and Tk 12,000, their total is Tk 50,000. The Accounts Receivable control account should contain the corresponding total debit before subsequent collections.

> The case also shows why cash records require a separate conceptual check.

Cash received from a customer reduces that customer’s receivable, while cash received from a cash sale creates revenue. Both increase cash, but they credit different accounts. Cash paid to a supplier reduces a payable, while rent paid creates an expense. Both reduce cash, but they debit different accounts. The cash book groups transactions by cash movement while its counterpart columns preserve the reason for the movement.


## A fuller April posting illustration

> A complete month makes the relationship between special journals, subsidiary ledgers and the general ledger visible.

Assume the opening Accounts Receivable balance is Tk 35,000. During April, the sales journal records Tk 50,000 of credit sales. The cash receipts journal records collections of Tk 22,000 from customers. Ignoring returns and other adjustments for the illustration, the ending Accounts Receivable control balance is Tk 35,000 + Tk 50,000 − Tk 22,000 = Tk 63,000. The subsidiary ledger must produce the same total after the individual customer postings.

> The control-account calculation is a summary, not a replacement for customer detail.

Suppose the three April credit sales are Tk 16,000, Tk 22,000 and Tk 12,000. The three customers therefore receive those individual debits. If the collections include Tk 10,000 from the first customer and Tk 12,000 from the second, their balances change individually. The control account does not show the identity of each customer; it shows the total. The subsidiary ledger supplies the identity and the control account supplies the general-ledger total.

### Accounts payable posting

> The same logic works for suppliers, but the normal balance and transaction direction are reversed.

Assume opening Accounts Payable is Tk 40,000. April credit purchases are Tk 57,000 and payments to suppliers are Tk 24,000. The ending Accounts Payable control balance is Tk 40,000 + Tk 57,000 − Tk 24,000 = Tk 73,000. The three supplier accounts must collectively produce the same Tk 73,000 balance after their individual purchases and settlements.

> Supplier detail can reveal an error that a total alone cannot explain.

If the control account shows Tk 73,000 but the subsidiary ledger totals Tk 68,000, the accountant knows that a Tk 5,000 difference exists. The next step is not to change either number without evidence. The accountant traces postings, source documents, journal totals and dates until the difference is explained. Control accounts are useful because they create a deliberate point of comparison.

### A two-column cash book illustration

> A two-column cash book separates cash on hand from the bank balance when the system requires both.

Suppose the opening balances are cash Tk 12,000 and bank Tk 400,000. During the month, cash sales of Tk 8,000 increase cash. A deposit of Tk 6,000 moves money from cash on hand to the bank; it is not new income. A cheque received from a customer for Tk 10,000 increases bank when deposited. Rent of Tk 15,000 is paid by cheque. Electricity of Tk 4,500 is paid from the bank. The cash column and bank column therefore change for different reasons, even though both represent cash resources.

> Internal transfers must not be mistaken for income or expense.

The Tk 6,000 deposit from cash to bank increases the bank column and decreases the cash column. Total cash resources do not increase. If the accountant treated the deposit as revenue, the accounts would report income that never occurred. The counterpart logic of double entry prevents that mistake when the cash book is designed and posted correctly.

### Cash discounts

> A cash discount changes the amount settled when payment conditions are met.

Suppose a customer owes Tk 10,000 and receives a 2 per cent discount for prompt payment. The discount is Tk 200, so the customer pays Tk 9,800. From the seller's perspective, the receipt is Tk 9,800 and the discount allowed is Tk 200, while the customer's receivable is cleared for the full Tk 10,000. The three amounts must be connected: Tk 9,800 + Tk 200 = Tk 10,000.

> The supplier-side case is the mirror image.

If Mitali owes a supplier Tk 10,000 and earns the same Tk 200 discount, it pays Tk 9,800. The payable is reduced by Tk 10,000, while the business records the appropriate discount received according to the system being used. The important idea is that the settlement amount and the amount originally owed are not necessarily identical.

### What remains in the general journal

> Special journals are selective, so unusual transactions continue to require individual entries.

Suppose Mitali records monthly depreciation on the oven. That entry does not belong in the purchases journal, sales journal or cash book because it is a non-cash adjusting transaction. Likewise, an adjusting entry for accrued wages, a correcting entry for a posting mistake, or a closing entry at the end of the period requires the general journal. The special-journal system therefore divides routine work without removing the general accounting cycle.

> A useful rule is to ask whether the transaction is both repetitive and covered by a defined journal.

If the answer is no, the general journal is often the appropriate place. If the answer is yes, the transaction can enter the special journal designed for that class. This decision rule is more reliable than memorising a list because different businesses can design their journals differently.

### Source documents and audit trail

> Every journal entry should have a path back to evidence.

A purchase invoice supports a credit purchase. A sales invoice supports a credit sale. A receipt or bank advice supports a cash receipt. A cheque or payment record supports a cash disbursement. The journal records the accounting interpretation of the document. The folio and reference information then help a reviewer trace the entry into the ledger or subsidiary account.

> Traceability turns bookkeeping from a list of numbers into an evidence-based record.

When an error appears in a control account, the accountant can move backwards from the total to the special journal, from the journal to the individual customer or supplier account, and from that account to the source document. The reverse direction is also possible. A source document can be followed forward until its effect reaches the financial statements. This two-way trace is an important feature of a well-organised accounting system.

## Summary

> Special journals organise repeated transactions.

They divide a crowded general journal into predictable transaction classes while preserving double-entry accounting.

> Subsidiary ledgers provide individual detail.

Customer and supplier accounts can be kept separately while control accounts show the corresponding totals in the general ledger.

> Purchases and sales journals have precise boundaries.

In the basic periodic model, they record merchandise bought or sold on account, not every transaction containing the word purchase or sale.

> Cash records group cash movement.

Cash receipts and disbursements can be recorded separately, or a cash book can combine cash and bank columns.

> Periodic totals connect special journals to the general ledger.

The totals preserve efficiency while the subsidiary ledgers preserve detail.

> The general journal remains necessary.

Adjustments, corrections, closing entries and unusual transactions still require a place for individual journal entries.


## Think it through

> Classifying the transaction before recording it prevents most special-journal mistakes.

For each transaction, ask three questions. Is it merchandise? Is it on account or for cash? Is it a recurring class for which the business maintains a special journal? The answers determine the appropriate book. This is faster and safer than memorising isolated examples.

> A control account should always invite a second question: where is its detail?

If Accounts Receivable shows Tk 60,000, the accountant should be able to obtain the subsidiary ledger and add the customer balances to the same total. A control account without supporting detail is not performing its intended control role.

> Cash can be the same amount but represent different economic events.

A Tk 10,000 receipt from a customer is a collection of an existing receivable. A Tk 10,000 cash sale is new revenue. A Tk 10,000 owner contribution increases capital. The cash column alone cannot explain the transaction. The counterpart account supplies the meaning.


## Where people go wrong

> One frequent mistake is putting every purchase into the purchases journal.

The purchases journal in this chapter is defined for merchandise bought on account. A cash purchase belongs in the cash records. A plant asset bought on account belongs in the appropriate general-journal treatment or designated asset-purchase record. The classification rule must be followed precisely.

> Another mistake is posting every subsidiary transaction separately to the control account when the system is designed for periodic totals.

The subsidiary ledger needs individual postings because management needs customer and supplier detail. The control account can receive the periodic total from the special journal. Mixing the two posting methods can double-count transactions.

> A third mistake is treating the cash book as if it removes the need for the general ledger.

The cash book records cash-related transactions efficiently, but the business still needs accounts for revenue, expenses, receivables, payables, assets and equity. The cash book is one component of the accounting system, not the whole ledger.


## In history

> The McKesson & Robbins case of 1938 illustrates why detailed records do not replace independent checking.

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission reported that fictitious inventory and accounts receivable were included in the company’s financial statements. The investigation contributed to changes in auditing practice, including stronger attention to physical inventory inspection and confirmation of receivables. The lesson for a special-journal system is narrow but important: an organised chain of records is useful only when the records can be traced and independently checked.^1
