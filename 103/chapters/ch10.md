:chapter X | Plant Assets and Depreciation
:mood How the cost of a lasting machine is shared among the years that use it.
:desc A bakery pays Tk 240,000 for an oven and a mixer, yet it charges only Tk 2,000 for them in January. This chapter explains why. It shows what a plant asset is and what it costs, why its cost is spread over its life, and how four methods do the spreading. It ends with the sale of the asset.

> A large payment in January does not have to be a large cost in January.

On 2 January 2026 Mitali Rahman paid for an oven and a mixer. The price was Tk 240,000: Tk 100,000 in cash and Tk 140,000 on account. By the end of the month the bakery had earned Tk 212,000 from its customers. Yet the income statement for January charges only Tk 2,000 for the oven and mixer. The rest of the Tk 240,000 is not a cost of January. Where is it?

> Two quick answers to the puzzle are both wrong.

Two quick answers are possible. The first says that the whole Tk 240,000 is a cost of January, because the money left the business in January. Then January's profit of Tk 127,000 would become a loss of Tk 111,000. The second answer says that the oven costs nothing in January, because Mitali still owns it. Then the profit would be Tk 129,000.

> Neither profit figure describes January truthfully.

Neither figure describes January truthfully. The oven will bake bread for about eight years, which is 96 months. It helps to earn revenue in every one of those months. A loss of Tk 111,000 would suggest that the bakery is failing, and it is not. A profit of Tk 129,000 would hide the fact that the oven is slowly consumed.

> This chapter gives the full answer that Chapter 9 gave in one line.

Chapter 9 met depreciation in one line: an adjusting entry of Tk 2,000. This chapter studies it in full. We shall learn what kind of asset an oven is and what its cost includes. We shall learn why a part of the cost is charged to each period, and how large the part should be. Last, we shall see what happens when the business sells the asset.

## 10.1 Plant assets and what they cost

> Some assets are used for years and are not meant for sale.

@ Plant asset | A long-lived physical asset used in the business, such as a machine or a building.
@ Plant | Word in use. In daily life, a living thing that grows from the ground. In accounting, the machines and buildings that a business uses.

Consider the things inside a bakery. Flour is used within days, and the bakery buys it again and again. The oven is different. The bakery does not sell it, and it will serve for years. Accountants give such things the name **plant asset**. A plant asset is a long-lived physical asset that the business uses in its work and does not plan to sell. The oven, the mixer, the delivery van, the shop counter and the building are all plant assets.^1

^1: The international standard for this group of assets is IAS 16, which uses the heading *property, plant and equipment*. Chapter 3 described how the Bangladesh framework relates to the international standards.

> Four tests decide whether an asset is a plant asset.

An asset must pass four tests to be a plant asset. It is physical, which means that a person can touch it. The business uses it to do its work. It lasts for more than one year. The business does not hold it for sale. The same object can pass or fail, depending on its use. A chair is a plant asset in the bakery's shop, where customers sit on it. In a furniture dealer's store the same chair is goods for sale, and Chapter 13 treats such goods in a different way.

> Plant assets come in kinds, and land is the one kind that is not depreciated.

Plant assets come in four main kinds: equipment, vehicles, buildings and land. The case file keeps the oven and the mixer in an account called Equipment, which is the name used in Chapter 4. Land is special. Land does not wear, and it has no fixed end to its use, so a business does not depreciate it. A building on the land does wear, and a business does depreciate the building.

> The name changes in Chapter 13, but the idea stays the same.

This chapter uses the name plant asset. From Chapter 13 the same assets will appear in the classified balance sheet under the name non-current asset, which means an asset kept for more than one year. Older books also use the name fixed asset. The three names describe one idea.

### What the cost of a plant asset includes

> The cost of an asset is everything spent to make it ready for use.

Recall that the cost concept of Chapter 3 records an asset at the amount paid to get it. For a plant asset, the amount paid is wider than the price on the invoice. The cost of a plant asset is all the spending needed to bring the asset to its place and make it ready for use. It includes the invoice price after any trade discount, the delivery, the installation, the testing and any taxes that the business cannot recover. It does not include spending after the asset is ready, such as insurance, service contracts and the training of staff.

> A tailor's cutting machine shows how the cost is built from its parts.

An example from another business shows the rule. A tailor in Dhaka, whose books are not part of the bakery's, buys a cutting machine on 12 April 2026. The table below lists each item of spending. Read the third column to see whether the item is part of the cost of the machine.

> The tailor records Tk 430,500, not the list price.

The tailor records the machine at Tk 430,500, because that is the total spent to make it ready. If the tailor pays in cash, the entry debits Equipment and credits Cash for Tk 430,500. The service contract and the training are separate payments, and each is an expense of the period. The list price of Tk 420,000 is not the cost. A cost that is too low would make the profit of later periods too high.

### Capital expenditure and revenue expenditure

> A business spends money on assets in two different ways.

@ Capital expenditure | Money spent to buy or improve an asset that will serve many years.
@ Revenue expenditure | Money spent on running costs or small repairs that serve only the current period.

A business spends money on its plant assets in two different ways. **Capital expenditure** is money spent to buy an asset, or to improve an asset, so that it serves for many years. **Revenue expenditure** is money spent on running costs or small repairs that serve only the current period. Capital expenditure is added to the cost of the asset and is later depreciated. Revenue expenditure is charged at once as an expense.

> One question separates the two kinds of spending.

To tell the two apart, ask one question: will this spending serve the business for more than the current period, by buying the asset, adding to it or lengthening its life? If the answer is yes, the spending is capital. If the answer is no, it is revenue. The figure below shows the two steps of the test.

:fig The test that separates capital expenditure from revenue expenditure. | ch10_fig1_capital_or_revenue.svg

> Examples help the reader to learn the test.

The test needs practice. The table below applies it to six kinds of spending in the bakery. Notice that a repair is revenue expenditure when it restores the asset to its usual condition, but a rebuild that lengthens the asset's life is capital expenditure.

:table Six kinds of spending in the bakery
| Spending | Kind | Reason |
| A second oven | Capital | A new asset serving many years |
| A larger fan that raises the oven's output | Capital | Adds to the asset |
| A rebuild that lengthens the oven's life by three years | Capital | Lengthens the life |
| A new seal for a worn oven door | Revenue | Restores the usual condition |
| Yearly cleaning and servicing | Revenue | Serves this year only |
| Fuel for the delivery van | Revenue | A running cost |

> A wrong choice changes profit and assets in the same period.

The choice matters because it changes the reported profit. Suppose a repair of Tk 6,000 is wrongly added to the cost of the oven. The year's expenses fall by Tk 6,000, so profit is Tk 6,000 too high, and the oven is Tk 6,000 too high on the balance sheet. The opposite mistake has the opposite effect: profit is too low and the asset is too low. The history section at the end of this chapter gives a large modern case of the first mistake.

> *Before moving on.* Cost includes everything needed to make the asset ready; capital spending serves many years, and revenue spending serves one.

*Before moving on.* The cost of a plant asset includes all the spending needed to make it ready for use. Capital expenditure is added to that cost, and revenue expenditure is an expense of the period. Land is not depreciated.

## 10.2 The puzzle of the wearing oven: why depreciation is charged

> The cost of a long-lived asset is shared among the years of its use.

@! Go slowly here.

Begin with an everyday bridge. A rickshaw puller buys a rickshaw for Tk 32,000. He expects to use it for four years, and then to sell the old frame for Tk 8,000. Over the four years the rickshaw loses Tk 24,000 of its price, which is Tk 32,000 less Tk 8,000. The rickshaw earns money every day of the four years. A careful puller therefore says that each year of work costs him Tk 6,000 for the rickshaw, because Tk 24,000 divided by four is Tk 6,000. Only after subtracting that Tk 6,000 does he call the rest of the year's earnings his profit.

> The oven is the rickshaw of the bakery.

The oven is the rickshaw of the bakery. Mitali does not spend Tk 240,000 on bread in January. She buys a machine that will help to earn revenue for years. The sensible plan is to charge each period with a fair part of the machine's cost. We now compare this plan with the two quick answers of the opening puzzle.

> Three treatments of the oven give three different profits for January.

The table below shows January's profit under three treatments. The revenue is Tk 212,000, and the other expenses are Tk 83,000: wages of Tk 40,000, rent of Tk 15,000, supplies of Tk 22,000 and utilities of Tk 6,000. Only the charge for the oven changes from one line to the next.

:table January 2026 profit under three treatments of the oven
| Treatment | Charge for oven (Tk) | Profit (Tk) |
| Charge the whole cost at once | 240,000 | −111,000 |
| Charge nothing | 0 | 129,000 |
| Charge a fair part (Chapter 9) | 2,000 | 127,000 |

> Only the third line shows the oven as a help to many months of work.

Only the third line treats the oven as what it is: a help to the work of many months. This is the matching concept of Chapter 9. Expenses are recorded in the same period as the revenue that they helped to earn. The oven helps to earn revenue for eight years, so its cost belongs to those eight years. This spreading of cost is depreciation. Chapter 9 gave it a short meaning; the box below gives the full one.

:box IN SHORT
Depreciation is the spreading of the cost of a plant asset, less its expected residual value, over the years in which the business uses it. It shares a cost among periods. It does not measure the market price of the asset, and it does not save money.

> Depreciation divides a cost; it does not value the asset.

Two misunderstandings are common, so we correct them now. The first is that depreciation measures how the market price of the oven falls. It does not. At the end of January the books show the oven at Tk 238,000. A dealer in used ovens might offer only Tk 200,000. There is no conflict. The books are not estimating what a buyer would pay. They are sharing a cost among the months of use. The going concern concept of Chapter 3 supports this, because the bakery plans to keep the oven and not to sell it.

> Depreciation moves no cash.

The second misunderstanding is that depreciation saves money to buy a new oven. It does not. The cash left the business when Mitali paid the supplier, in January and later. The adjusting entry of Tk 2,000 debits an expense and credits a contra account. No bank balance changes. If the owner wants a fund for a new oven, the owner must put real cash into a separate account, and that is a different decision.

> Two causes explain why an asset gives less service over time.

@ Wear and tear | Loss of an asset's value from ordinary use and the passing of time.
@ Obsolescence | Loss of an asset's usefulness because newer and better assets have appeared.

Why does a plant asset lose its usefulness? There are two main causes. **Wear and tear** is the loss of an asset's value from ordinary use and the passing of time. The oven's heating parts weaken with each hour of work, and metal ages even when the oven is idle. **Obsolescence** is the loss of an asset's usefulness because newer and better assets have appeared. A new oven that bakes faster and uses less gas makes the old oven less useful, although the old oven still works.

> The rule applies to every plant asset except land.

The rule applies to every plant asset that has a limited life. The standards require a business to depreciate such an asset over its useful life, and consistency, from Chapter 3, requires the business to keep the same method from one period to the next.^2 Land is the usual exception, because it has no limited life.

^2: IAS 16 requires the method to reflect the pattern in which the asset's benefits are used, and requires the business to review the method and the estimates at least at the end of each financial year. Chapter 3 introduced consistency.

> *Pause and check.* A van costs Tk 600,000. The owner says that Tk 100,000 of depreciation means a fund of Tk 100,000 for a new van. Is that correct?

*Pause and check.* A van costs Tk 600,000. After one year the owner says: "I have charged Tk 100,000 of depreciation, so I now have Tk 100,000 saved for a new van." Is the owner correct?

> The answer is no, because depreciation moves no cash.

*Answer.* The owner is not correct. The charge of Tk 100,000 reduces profit and increases accumulated depreciation, and no cash moves. The owner has Tk 100,000 less reported profit, but the bank balance is the same. A saved fund exists only if the owner places cash in a separate account.

> *You should now be able to say* why the oven is not charged in full in January.

*You should now be able to say* why the bakery charges only Tk 2,000 for the oven in January: the oven serves many months, so its cost is shared among them, and the sharing is not a valuation and not a saving of cash.

> *Before moving on.* Depreciation shares a cost among the periods of use, caused by wear and tear and by obsolescence.

*Before moving on.* Depreciation is a sharing of cost, in line with the matching concept. Wear and tear and obsolescence are its two causes. It is not a market valuation, and it moves no cash.
## 10.3 Cost, residual value and useful life

> Three facts decide the yearly charge: cost, residual value and useful life.

Before any charge can be calculated, the accountant needs three facts. The first is the cost, which Section 10.1 explained. For the oven and the mixer it is Tk 240,000, and it is certain, because it appears on the invoice. The other two facts are estimates about the future. Nobody knows today what the oven will be worth in eight years, or how long it will serve. The business must make careful estimates and record them.

> Residual value is what the business expects to receive at the end.

@ Residual value | The amount expected to be received when an asset is sold at the end of its use.

The first estimate is the **residual value**. This is the amount that the business expects to receive when it sells or scraps the asset at the end of its use. Other books call it the scrap value or the salvage value. An old oven still contains steel, and a dealer will pay something for it. The case file records the residual value of the oven and the mixer as Tk 48,000. If the business expects to receive nothing, the residual value is zero.

> Useful life is how long the business expects to use the asset.

@ Useful life | The time, in years or in units of use, for which a business expects to use an asset.

The second estimate is the **useful life**. This is the time, in years or in units of use, for which the business expects to use the asset. A unit of use can be a batch baked, a kilometre driven or an hour of machine work. The case file records eight years for the oven and the mixer. Useful life is not the same as the whole physical life. An oven that could work for twelve years has a useful life of eight years if the bakery plans to replace it then, or if newer ovens will make it obsolete by then.

> Only the part of the cost that will be used up is charged.

@ Depreciable amount | An asset's cost minus its residual value; the total to be charged as depreciation.

The two estimates lead to a third figure. The **depreciable amount** is an asset's cost minus its residual value. It is the total that the business will charge as depreciation over the useful life. In words: depreciable amount equals cost minus residual value. In symbols, D = C − R, where D is the depreciable amount, C is the cost and R is the residual value. For the oven, D = 240,000 − 48,000 = 192,000.

> The rickshaw puller gives the reason for subtracting the residual value.

We subtract the residual value because the business expects to receive it back. Return to the rickshaw of Section 10.2. The puller paid Tk 32,000 and expected Tk 8,000 at the end, so only Tk 24,000 was used up in his work. In the same way, Mitali expects to recover Tk 48,000 from the oven, so only Tk 192,000 is the cost of the years of baking.

> One picture shows the cost, the residual value and the life together.

The figure below shows the three facts in one picture. The long bar is the cost of Tk 240,000. The hatched part of the bar is the depreciable amount, and the plain part at the right is the residual value. The line under the bar divides the useful life into eight years.

:fig Cost, residual value and useful life of the oven and the mixer. | ch10_fig2_cost_residual_life.svg

> Estimates are judgements, and they need a reasonable basis.

Estimates are judgements, and two honest accountants may choose different numbers. A reasonable estimate rests on evidence: the supplier's advice, the experience of the business with similar assets, the prices of used assets, and the business's own plan for replacement. The business records its estimates in its accounting policies and reviews them at least once a year.

> *Before moving on.* Three facts decide the charge: cost, residual value and useful life.

*Before moving on.* The cost is known, and the residual value and the useful life are estimates. Cost minus residual value is the depreciable amount, which is the total to be charged over the useful life.

## 10.4 The straight-line method and its entries

> The straight-line method charges the same amount in every year.

@ Straight-line method | A method that charges the same depreciation amount in every year of use.

The easiest way to spread a cost is to charge the same amount every year. The **straight-line method** is a method that charges the same depreciation amount in every year of use. In words: annual depreciation equals cost minus residual value, divided by the useful life in years. In symbols, A = (C − R) ÷ n, where A is the annual depreciation, C is the cost, R is the residual value and n is the number of years.

> The oven's calculation gives Tk 24,000 a year and Tk 2,000 a month.

Apply the formula to the oven and the mixer. Given: C = 240,000, R = 48,000 and n = 8. Then A = (240,000 − 48,000) ÷ 8 = 192,000 ÷ 8 = 24,000. The bakery reports month by month, so it divides the yearly amount by twelve: 24,000 ÷ 12 = 2,000. This is the Tk 2,000 of adjusting entry A3 in Chapter 9. The case file counts the month of purchase as a full month, so the oven is depreciated for all of January, although Mitali bought it on 2 January.

> Book value is what remains of the cost after the depreciation so far.

@ Book value | An asset's cost minus its accumulated depreciation.

After each charge, the asset has a new figure in the books. The **book value** of an asset is its cost minus its accumulated depreciation. At 31 January 2026 the book value of the oven and the mixer is Tk 240,000 less Tk 2,000, which is Tk 238,000. This is the figure that appears on the balance sheet of Chapter 9. Book value is a figure from the books; it is not a market price.

> A schedule shows every year of the asset's life in one table.

@ Depreciation schedule | A table that shows depreciation, accumulated depreciation and book value for each year.

A **depreciation schedule** is a table that shows depreciation, accumulated depreciation and book value for each year. The schedule below covers the whole life of the oven and the mixer. Read it row by row. In each row, the depreciation expense is the same, the accumulated depreciation grows by that amount, and the book value falls by that amount. At the end of year 8 the book value equals the residual value.

> A part-year charge follows the same formula with a fraction.

When an asset is bought during a year, the first year's charge is a part of the yearly amount. The tailor's cutting machine shows how. Its cost is Tk 430,500, as Section 10.1 calculated. Suppose the residual value is Tk 30,500 and the useful life is ten years. Then the depreciable amount is Tk 400,000, and the yearly depreciation is Tk 40,000. The tailor bought the machine on 12 April 2026. Counting April as a full month, as the bakery does, the machine is used for nine months of 2026.

> The nine-month charge is nine twelfths of the yearly amount.

The charge for 2026 is 40,000 × 9 ÷ 12 = 30,000. The entry on 31 December 2026 debits Depreciation Expense and credits Accumulated Depreciation: Equipment for Tk 30,000. The book value at that date is 430,500 − 30,000 = 400,500. Each of the years 2027 to 2035 carries Tk 40,000, and the last three months of the life fall in 2036, with a charge of Tk 10,000. The total is 30,000 + 360,000 + 10,000 = 400,000, which equals the depreciable amount.

> A change of estimate affects future years only.

A business may learn that an estimate was wrong. When that happens, it does not correct the past years. It spreads the remaining book value, less the new residual value, over the remaining life. A small printing shop shows the method. It bought a press on 1 January 2026 for Tk 500,000, with a residual value of Tk 50,000 and a life of nine years, so the yearly charge was Tk 50,000. At 31 December 2028 the accumulated depreciation is Tk 150,000 and the book value is Tk 350,000.

> The new yearly charge is found from the book value and the new estimates.

Now the owner expects the press to serve for eight more years and to have a residual value of Tk 30,000. The new yearly charge is (350,000 − 30,000) ÷ 8 = 40,000. The charges of 2026, 2027 and 2028 stay as they are. Only 2029 and later years use the new figure. The business also tells the readers of its statements about the change.

> *Before moving on.* Straight-line charges (cost minus residual value) divided by the life, and the contra account shows the total charged.

*Before moving on.* The straight-line method charges (C − R) ÷ n each year. The expense account holds one period's charge, and the contra account holds the total. Book value is cost minus accumulated depreciation, and a change of estimate affects only the future.
## 10.5 Other methods: units of production, sum of the years' digits, declining balance

> The straight-line method suits assets that give even service, and others do not.

The straight-line method gives the same charge every year, which is fair when an asset gives the same service every year. Some assets do not. A machine may work many hours in one year and few in the next. A computer may give most of its service in its first years, before newer models replace it. Three other methods fit these patterns. We apply each one to the oven and the mixer, so that we can compare them. The figures in this section are a comparison schedule only. No new transaction enters the case file.

### The units-of-production method

> This method charges depreciation in proportion to use.

@ Units-of-production method | A method that charges depreciation in proportion to how much the asset is used.

The **units-of-production method** is a method that charges depreciation in proportion to how much the asset is used. In words: the rate per unit equals the depreciable amount divided by the total units expected in the asset's life, and the charge for a year equals the rate multiplied by the units used in that year. In symbols, r = D ÷ U and the charge is r × u, where U is the total units expected and u is the units used in the year.

> The oven's rate is Tk 4 for each batch.

Suppose the bakery expects the oven to bake 48,000 batches in its whole life. The rate is 192,000 ÷ 48,000 = Tk 4 for each batch. The table below assumes the number of batches baked in each year. These numbers are assumed for the comparison, and a real business would take them from its records. The charge is highest in the years of heavy use, and the total over the life is Tk 192,000.

### The sum-of-the-years'-digits method

> This method charges more in the early years, using falling fractions.

@ Sum-of-the-years'-digits method | A method that charges more depreciation in early years, using falling fractions.

The **sum-of-the-years'-digits method** is a method that charges more depreciation in the early years, using falling fractions. Write the digits of the years of life and add them. For eight years the digits are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8, and their sum is 36. A shortcut gives the sum: n × (n + 1) ÷ 2 = 8 × 9 ÷ 2 = 36. The fraction for the first year is 8/36, for the second year 7/36, and so on down to 1/36 for the last year. Each fraction is applied to the depreciable amount.

> The oven's first-year charge is eight thirty-sixths of Tk 192,000.

The first-year charge is 8/36 × 192,000 = 42,666.67, which we round to the nearest whole taka: Tk 42,667. The other years follow in the same way. Rounding each year can leave a difference of one taka in the total, so a careful accountant checks that the charges add to the depreciable amount. Here they add to exactly Tk 192,000.

### The declining-balance method

> This method charges a fixed percentage of the book value each year.

@ Declining-balance method | A method that charges a fixed percentage of the asset's book value each year.

The **declining-balance method** is a method that charges a fixed percentage of the asset's book value each year. Because the book value falls, the charge falls too. In words: the charge for a year equals the rate multiplied by the book value at the start of the year. Many businesses choose a rate equal to twice the straight-line rate. The straight-line rate for eight years is 1 ÷ 8 = 12.5%, so the doubled rate is 25%. The rate is a matter of policy, and the business states it.

> The rate is applied to book value, and the residual value sets a floor.

Two rules guard this method. First, the rate is applied to the whole book value, and the residual value is not subtracted before the calculation. Second, the book value must never fall below the residual value, so the charge is limited when the limit is reached. In the table below, the charge for 2031 would be 25% × 56,953 = 14,238, which would take the book value below Tk 48,000. The charge is therefore limited to 56,953 − 48,000 = 8,953, and later years have no charge.

:table Declining-balance schedule at 25% for the oven and the mixer (Tk)
| Year | Calculation | Depreciation | Accumulated | Book value |
| 2026 | 25% of 240,000 | 60,000 | 60,000 | 180,000 |
| 2027 | 25% of 180,000 | 45,000 | 105,000 | 135,000 |
| 2028 | 25% of 135,000 | 33,750 | 138,750 | 101,250 |
| 2029 | 25% of 101,250 | 25,313 | 164,063 | 75,937 |
| 2030 | 25% of 75,937 | 18,984 | 183,047 | 56,953 |
| 2031 | Limited to residual value | 8,953 | 192,000 | 48,000 |
| 2032 | None | 0 | 192,000 | 48,000 |
| 2033 | None | 0 | 192,000 | 48,000 |

> The four methods differ in timing, and they agree on the total.

The table below compares the four methods. Read the last row first. Every method charges a total of Tk 192,000 over the eight years, so the methods differ only in the timing of the charge. They do not change the total cost of the oven.

> The timing changes the profit of each year but not the profit of the whole life.

Timing matters because it changes the profit of each year. In the first year, declining balance charges Tk 36,000 more than straight-line, so that year's profit is Tk 36,000 lower. In the last years the position reverses, and declining balance gives the higher profit. Over the eight years the total profit is the same. The figure below shows the book value of the oven under each method. All four lines start at Tk 240,000 and reach Tk 48,000, but they take different paths.

:fig Book value of the oven and the mixer at the end of each year under four methods. | ch10_fig4_book_value_four_methods.svg

> The method should follow the pattern in which the asset gives its service.

How should a business choose? The method should follow the pattern in which the asset gives its service. Straight-line suits assets that serve evenly, such as buildings and ovens used every day. Units of production suits assets whose use varies, such as a van driven many kilometres in one year and few in another. The two methods with falling charges suit assets that give most service early or lose their usefulness quickly. Whatever the choice, consistency requires the business to apply the same method each year, and the business reviews the method regularly.

> The bakery uses the straight-line method.

The bakery uses the straight-line method for the oven and the mixer, as Chapter 9 showed. The other schedules are for comparison only. Every later chapter that mentions depreciation in the bakery's books uses Tk 2,000 a month for the oven and the mixer.

> *Before moving on.* Four methods give the same total and different timing; the method should follow the pattern of use.

*Before moving on.* Units of production follows use. Sum of the years' digits and declining balance charge more in early years. All four methods charge the same total, and consistency requires the same method from year to year.

## 10.6 Selling or scrapping a plant asset

> A plant asset leaves the business by sale, by scrapping or by loss.

@ Disposal | Selling, scrapping, or otherwise getting rid of an asset.

Sooner or later, a plant asset leaves the business. **Disposal** is the selling, scrapping or other removal of an asset. The business may sell the asset to another business, or it may scrap the asset because it has no further use. The accounting has three steps. First, record the depreciation up to the date of disposal, so that the book value is up to date. Second, remove the cost of the asset and its accumulated depreciation from the books. Third, record the cash received and the gain or loss.

> The gain or loss is the sale price compared with the book value.

@ Gain on disposal | The amount by which an asset's sale price is more than its book value.
@ Loss on disposal | The amount by which an asset's sale price is less than its book value.

The gain or loss comes from comparing the sale price with the book value. A **gain on disposal** is the amount by which the sale price is more than the book value. A **loss on disposal** is the amount by which the sale price is less than the book value. In words: gain or loss equals sale price minus book value. A positive answer is a gain, and a negative answer is a loss. The cost of the asset does not enter this comparison, because the depreciation has already charged part of the cost to earlier years.

> The tailor's machine is sold at the end of 2029.

We continue with the tailor's cutting machine. Its cost is Tk 430,500, and the yearly depreciation is Tk 40,000, with Tk 30,000 for the part-year 2026. At 31 December 2029 the accumulated depreciation is 30,000 + 40,000 + 40,000 + 40,000 = 150,000, so the book value is 430,500 − 150,000 = 280,500. The tailor sells the machine on that date. The figure below compares two possible prices with the book value.

:fig Sale price compared with book value: gain or loss on the tailor's cutting machine. | ch10_fig5_gain_or_loss.svg

> A sale above book value gives a gain.

Suppose the tailor sells the machine for Tk 300,000 in cash. The gain is 300,000 − 280,500 = 19,500. The entry debits Cash for the money received and debits Accumulated Depreciation: Equipment for the Tk 150,000 that is removed. It credits Equipment for the full cost of Tk 430,500, and it credits Gain on Disposal of Equipment for Tk 19,500. Debits and credits are each Tk 450,000.

> A sale below book value gives a loss.

Now suppose that the tailor sells the machine for Tk 250,000. The loss is 250,000 − 280,500 = −30,500, which is a loss of Tk 30,500. The loss is a debit, because it reduces profit. The debits are 250,000 + 150,000 + 30,500 = 430,500, and the credit to Equipment is Tk 430,500.

> A sale during a year needs the depreciation to be brought up to date first.

The tailor chose the year-end for these two entries. If the sale happens during a year, the first step matters. Suppose the sale is on 30 June 2030. The tailor first records six months of depreciation, 40,000 × 6 ÷ 12 = 20,000, which raises the accumulated depreciation to Tk 170,000 and lowers the book value to Tk 260,500. The gain or loss is then calculated against Tk 260,500. If the tailor scraps the machine and receives nothing, the loss equals the whole book value.

> Gains and losses on disposal are not sales revenue.

A gain or a loss on disposal is not part of the revenue from the business's main work. The tailor earns revenue from making clothes, not from selling machines. Chapter 13 shows the gain among other income, and the loss among the expenses. Two further points complete the picture. An asset that has reached its residual value but is still in use stays in the books at cost, with its accumulated depreciation, until its disposal, and no further charge is made. An exchange of an old asset for a new one is a special case that this book does not treat.

> *Before moving on.* Bring the depreciation up to date, remove cost and accumulated depreciation, and compare the sale price with book value.

*Before moving on.* A disposal has three steps. A sale price above the book value gives a gain, and a sale price below it gives a loss. Neither the gain nor the loss is revenue from the main work of the business.

## The worked case: choosing a method for the oven

> The bakery's oven is used to test the choice of method.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* *Situation.* The oven and the mixer were bought on 2 January 2026 for Tk 240,000, with a residual value of Tk 48,000 and a useful life of eight years. The owner of another bakery uses the declining-balance method at 25% a year. Mitali wants to know which method suits her bakery, and what the choice does to the profit of January.

> The question asks for a method and for the year-end figures.

:case *Question.* Which method should the bakery use, and what are the figures at 31 December 2026 under that method?

> The reasoning links the pattern of use to the method.

:case *Reasoning.* The oven bakes on every working day at a similar rate, so its service is even across the years. The case file gives no sign that a newer oven will replace it within eight years. The straight-line method matches this pattern. The depreciable amount is 240,000 − 48,000 = 192,000, and the yearly charge is 192,000 ÷ 8 = 24,000, which is Tk 2,000 a month. At 31 December 2026 the accumulated depreciation is Tk 24,000 and the book value is Tk 216,000. Under the other bakery's method the charge for January would be 60,000 ÷ 12 = 5,000, and January's profit would be 127,000 − 3,000 = 124,000.

> The conclusion keeps the method and the January profit.

:case *Conclusion.* The bakery uses the straight-line method and keeps it in every year, as consistency requires. The profit for January stays at Tk 127,000, as Chapter 9 found.

> The check ties the figures back to the cost.

:case *Check.* Accumulated depreciation plus book value is 24,000 + 216,000 = 240,000, which equals the cost. Eight years at Tk 24,000 give Tk 192,000, which equals the depreciable amount. The book value at the end of year 8 is Tk 48,000, which equals the residual value.

## Summary

> A plant asset is used for years, and its cost is shared among those years.

A plant asset is a long-lived physical asset that the business uses and does not sell. Its cost includes everything spent to make it ready for use. Capital expenditure adds to that cost, and revenue expenditure is an expense of the period.

> Depreciation shares the depreciable amount among the years of use.

Depreciation shares the depreciable amount, which is cost minus residual value, among the years of use. It does not measure market value and it moves no cash. The straight-line method charges (C − R) ÷ n each year. Units of production, sum of the years' digits and declining balance change the timing but not the total. The credit goes to accumulated depreciation, and the book value is the cost minus that total. When an asset is sold, the sale price minus the book value is a gain or a loss.

## Where people go wrong

> Depreciation is not a fall in market price.

*Mistake.* "Depreciation shows how much the oven would sell for." *Correction.* Depreciation shares the cost among the years. The book value is a figure from the books and may differ from any market price.

> Depreciation is not a savings fund.

*Mistake.* "The charge puts cash aside for a new oven." *Correction.* The entry moves no cash. A fund exists only if the owner places cash in a separate account.

> The asset account keeps its cost.

*Mistake.* "Credit the Equipment account directly for the depreciation." *Correction.* The credit goes to the contra account, so that the cost and the total charged are both visible.

> The rate in declining balance applies to book value.

*Mistake.* "Use the cost, or the depreciable amount, for every year of the declining-balance charge." *Correction.* The rate applies to the book value at the start of each year, and the book value never falls below the residual value.

> Land and repairs are common sources of error.

*Mistake.* "Depreciate land, and add every repair to the asset." *Correction.* Land has no limited life and is not depreciated. A repair that restores the usual condition is an expense of the period.

> Gain or loss comes from book value, not from cost.

*Mistake.* "The gain on a sale is the sale price minus the original cost." *Correction.* The gain or loss is the sale price minus the book value, after the depreciation is brought up to date.

## In history

> Nineteenth-century railways met the problem of wear and dividends.

In 1850 Dionysius Lardner published *Railway Economy* in London. The railways of his time owned engines, carriages and track that wore with use. Their directors had to decide how much to spend on keeping this equipment in repair and how much to pay to shareholders as dividends. Lardner discussed the wear of rolling stock and track, and the conflict between maintaining capital and paying dividends at once. A company that distributes all its earnings and makes no provision for wear shows its owners a profit that is too high. Depreciation is the accounting answer to that conflict.

> A modern case shows what happens when running costs are treated as assets.

On 25 June 2002 the American telecommunications company WorldCom announced that it had moved costs of its network lines from expense accounts to asset accounts: US$3.055 billion in 2001 and US$797 million in the first quarter of 2002. The announcement said that this treatment did not follow generally accepted accounting principles. The costs were fees paid to other companies for the use of their lines, which are running costs. Charged as expenses, they would have lowered profit at once. Treated as assets, they were spread over many years. The special investigative committee of the company's board later reported more than US$9 billion of false or unsupported entries in all. The case shows why the line between capital and revenue spending protects the truth of the profit figure.
