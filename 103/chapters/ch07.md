> What you must know before you begin.
*You will need.* From Chapter 6, all of it: the account and the T-account, the six kinds of account, debit and credit, the double-entry system, normal balances and the chart of accounts. From Chapter 4: the thirteen transactions of January.

> The puzzle: one sale must reach two pages, and nobody can see the whole story.
In Chapter 6 you learned to give each kind of item its own page, and a rule for writing amounts. Suppose that it is the evening of 10 January, and the bakery has sold baked goods for Tk 90,000 in cash. The rule says that Tk 90,000 must be written on the left side of the page for cash, and Tk 90,000 on the right side of the page for sales. That is two writings, on two pages, perhaps by two people, perhaps on two different days.

> Two dangers follow: a half-written transaction, and a page that does not say why.
Two dangers follow. The first is that one writing is made and the other is forgotten, or that the two amounts differ. The second is less obvious. A page for cash shows Tk 90,000 on its left side, but it does not say why. It records the view of one account, not the story of the event. If an owner asks what happened on 10 January, the answer is spread across many pages, because the pages are arranged by item and not by date.

> The answer is two books: the first records the event, the second gathers the amounts.
The answer is to keep two books with two different jobs. The first book records each transaction once, in date order, with both sides together and a note saying what happened. The second book holds the account pages, and the amounts are copied into it from the first. The first book answers the question: what happened, and when? The second answers the question: how much? This chapter teaches both books, and the copying between them. After the slow Chapter 6, the pace here is standard, but we go slowly again when the copying is met for the first time.

## 7.1 The Accounting Cycle in Outline

@ Accounting cycle | The set of steps that turns a business's transactions into financial statements.
> The accounting cycle is the chain of steps from a transaction to the statements.
Before we learn the two books, we need a map of the whole route. A business does not record a transaction and then stop. It records the transaction, tests the records, brings them up to date, reports them, and prepares for the next period. These steps are repeated in every period, in the same order. The set of steps that turns a business's transactions into financial statements is called the **accounting cycle**.

> The figure shows ten steps; this chapter teaches steps 2 to 4.
The figure below shows the ten steps. It is a map and not a lesson. Each box carries a chapter number that tells you where the step is taught. The double-lined boxes are the steps of this chapter. You have already met step 1: a transaction happens, and you analyse it with the rules of Chapter 6.

:fig The accounting cycle in outline. Each box names a step and the chapter that teaches it. | figures/fig07_cycle.svg

> One line for each step, with a pointer, so that nothing arrives without warning.
Here is one line for each step. Step 2: collect the paper that proves the transaction (section 7.2). Step 3: write the transaction in the first book, the journal (sections 7.2 and 7.3). Step 4: copy the amounts to the account pages, the ledger (section 7.4). Step 5: test the ledger with a trial balance (Chapter 8). Step 6: record the adjusting entries that bring the accounts up to date (Chapter 9). Step 7: test again, with an adjusted trial balance (Chapter 9). Step 8: prepare the financial statements (Chapters 5 and 12). Step 9: close the temporary accounts (Chapter 12). Step 10: test once more, with a post-closing trial balance (Chapter 12). > One transaction follows the route: the school's payment of 18 January.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Follow one transaction through the first steps. On 18 January the school pays the bakery Tk 25,000 of the Tk 40,000 that it owes (step 1). The bakery gives the school a receipt and keeps a copy (step 2). The bookkeeper writes a journal entry that debits Cash and credits Accounts Receivable by Tk 25,000, with the narration *Received cash from the school* (step 3). The bookkeeper then posts the debit to the Cash account and the credit to the Accounts Receivable account (step 4). Later steps test and report the result. The transaction has now left a trail: from the statements back to the ledger, from the ledger to the journal, and from the journal to the receipt.

> Computers hide some of the writing, but the steps stay the same.
Many businesses now keep their books in computer programs. A program hides some of the writing, but it follows the same steps in the same order.^1 A person who understands the cycle can check what a program does. A person who does not understand it can only trust the program.

^1: Some programs record a transaction and post it in one action. The journal and the ledger still exist inside the program, and the program can print each of them.

> Recap of 7.1: the cycle is a repeated chain of steps; this chapter covers the first part.
*Before moving on.* The accounting cycle is the set of steps that turns transactions into statements, repeated in every period. This chapter teaches the first part: the source document, the journal and the ledger.

## 7.2 The Journal: the Book of First Record

> Every record begins with a piece of paper that proves the event.
Every record starts from evidence. When the bakery buys flour, the supplier gives a paper that states what was sold and how much to pay. When a customer pays, the bakery gives a receipt. When the bakery pays a bill, it may write a cheque or keep a bill that is marked as paid.

@ Source document | Paper proof that a transaction happened, such as an invoice, receipt, or cheque.
> A source document is the paper proof of a transaction.
Each such paper is a **source document**: paper proof that a transaction happened, such as an invoice, a receipt or a cheque. The source document is the evidence. Keep it. Later, a lender, an auditor or a tax officer may ask: how do you know that this transaction took place? The paper gives the answer. Without it, the record is only a claim.

@ Invoice | A document that tells a customer what was sold and how much to pay.
@ Cheque | A written order to a bank to pay a stated sum to a named person.
> Two common source documents: the invoice and the cheque.
Two source documents appear often. An **invoice** is a document that tells a customer what was sold and how much to pay. The supplier of the oven sends the bakery an invoice, and the bakery sends invoices to its hotel customers. A **cheque** is a written order to a bank to pay a stated sum to a named person. When the bakery pays a supplier by cheque, the cheque and the matching invoice together prove the payment.

> The paper that would usually prove each of six January events is listed in the table.
The table lists the paper that would usually prove six of the bakery's January events. Read each row from left to right: the event, and the source document that supports it.

:table Source documents for six January events
| Event | Likely source document |
| Owner invests cash (1 January) | Bank deposit slip or the owner's signed note |
| Oven and mixer bought (2 January) | The supplier's invoice |
| Rent paid in advance (3 January) | The landlord's receipt |
| Catering billed to the school (15 January) | The bakery's copy of its invoice |

> A good source document states the date, the parties, the goods and the amount.
A good source document states the date, the two parties, what was bought or sold, and the amount. If one of these is missing, ask for a better document. A paper that cannot be checked is a weak support for an entry.

@ Journal | The book of first record, in which each transaction is written in date order. In daily life, a journal is a personal diary.
> The journal is the book where each transaction is first written, in date order.
The first book is the journal. A **journal** is the book of first record, in which each transaction is written in date order. The word may remind you of a diary, and the idea is close: the journal tells the story of the business, day by day. A transaction is written in the journal before it appears anywhere else.

> There are three reasons to write every transaction in the journal first.
There are three reasons for this rule. First, both sides of the transaction are written together, so neither side can be forgotten. Second, the transactions follow one another in date order, so anyone can follow what happened and when. Third, the journal carries a short explanation, so the reason for each entry is not lost.

> Journal pages are numbered, and a mistake is corrected by a new entry, never by erasing.
A journal is kept with care. Its pages are numbered, so that a missing page can be noticed. A mistake is not erased. It is put right by a new entry that shows what was changed and why, as Chapter 8 teaches. These habits make the journal a record that a lender, an auditor or a tax officer can trust.

@ Journal entry | The record of one transaction in the journal, showing accounts, debits, and credits.
@ Journalising | Writing a transaction in the journal as a journal entry.
> One transaction written in the journal is a journal entry.
The record of one transaction in the journal is a **journal entry**. The act of writing it is called **journalising**. So we journalise a transaction, and the result is a journal entry. The figure below shows the layout of a journal page, and the paragraphs after it explain each part.

:fig The layout of a journal page, with one entry. The letters are explained in the figure. | figures/fig07_journalpage.svg

> The page has five columns, and the debit comes first.
The page has five columns: the date, the account titles and explanation, the reference, the debit amount and the credit amount. The entry in the figure has one debit and two credits. Section 7.3 explains why such an entry is allowed. Read the entry in this order. The date is written on the first line. The account that is debited is written first, at the left edge of the second column, and its amount goes in the debit column. The accounts that are credited are written below, a little to the right, and their amounts go in the credit column. The explanation comes last.

@ Narration | A short note under a journal entry that says what the entry is for.
> The narration says in one line why the entry was made.
The last line of an entry is the **narration**: a short note under a journal entry that says what the entry is for. A good narration is brief, and it names the event and not the accounts. *Bought oven and mixer: part cash, part on account* is a good narration. *Debit Equipment, credit Cash* is not, because the entry already says that.

> The Ref. column stays empty until the amounts are posted.
The column headed Ref. holds the number of the ledger account. Mitali Bakery's account numbers are those of the chart of accounts in Chapter 6. The column is filled when the amounts are copied to the ledger (section 7.4), and the number shows that the copying was done. Before that, the column is empty.

> The method for one transaction has four steps.
We can now state the method. To journalise one transaction: first, write the date. Second, decide the accounts, and the side of each, with the rules of Chapter 6. Third, write the debits first and the credits below them, a little to the right, with the amounts. Fourth, check that the debits equal the credits, and write the narration.

> A small business with clean numbers shows three entries of one debit and one credit.
A small example shows the method with the cleanest numbers. Hasan's Tea Stall is illustrative, and it is separate from the bakery. On 1 April 2026 Hasan invests Tk 10,000 in cash. On 2 April he buys tea and sugar for Tk 1,500 in cash. On 3 April he sells tea for Tk 2,000 in cash. The table shows the three entries. The Ref. column is empty, because nothing has been posted yet.

*Before moving on.* A source document proves that a transaction happened. The journal records the transaction first, in date order, as a journal entry with its debits, its credits and a narration. You should now be able to say: *the journal is the book in which every transaction is first recorded, with both sides together.*

## 7.4 The Ledger and Posting

> The journal is in date order; the ledger is in account order.
@! Go slowly here: copying between books is new.
The journal is arranged by date. That is useful for following events, but it is not useful for answering the question: how much cash do we have? For that, we need all the cash amounts together. The second book does this job.

@ Ledger | The book that holds all of a business's accounts together.
> The ledger is the book that holds all the accounts.
The **ledger** is the book that holds all of a business's accounts together. It is the place where the account pages of Chapter 6 are kept, one page or more for each account, usually in the order of the chart of accounts. Everything about Cash is in the Cash page of the ledger, and everything about Sales Revenue is in the Sales Revenue page.

@ Posting | Copying amounts from the journal into the correct ledger accounts.
> Posting copies the amounts from the journal to the ledger.
The amounts reach the ledger by **posting**: copying amounts from the journal into the correct ledger accounts. Posting changes nothing. It does not change an amount, a side or an account. It moves each amount, exactly as written, from the book of events to the book of accounts.

> Posting has four steps for each line of a journal entry.
Posting has four steps for each line of a journal entry. First, find the ledger account named on the line. Second, write the date and the amount on the same side of the account as in the journal: a debit on the left, a credit on the right. Third, write the journal page in the Ref. column of the ledger, for example J1 for page 1 of the journal. Fourth, return to the journal and write the account number in the Ref. column there. Each book now points to the other, so that any amount can be traced in either direction.

:fig Posting the entry of 10 January: each amount is copied to the same side of its ledger account. | figures/fig07_posting.svg

> The figure shows the entry of 10 January and its two postings.
Study the figure with care. The journal entry debits Cash and credits Sales Revenue, each by Tk 90,000. The debit amount is copied to the left side of the Cash account. The credit amount is copied to the right side of the Sales Revenue account. Each ledger line carries J1, the page of the journal, and the journal lines carry 101 and 401, the numbers of the accounts. The same procedure is repeated for every line of every entry.

@ Balance | The difference between the debit total and the credit total of an account. In daily life, the money held in a bank account.
> The balance of an account is the difference between its two sides.
After posting, each account must answer the question: how much? The answer is its **balance**: the difference between the debit total and the credit total of an account. A balance is on the debit side if the debit total is greater, and on the credit side if the credit total is greater. In section 6.1 you found the difference between two sides in this way. Now it has a name.

> A ledger page can show a running balance after every posting.
Ledger pages often show a running balance, which is a new balance after every posting.^2 Look at the Cash account of the bakery. Each row shows one posting. After a debit, the balance rises by the amount. After a credit, it falls by the amount. The last row gives the balance at the end of the month.

> After posting, three checks catch most slips.
After posting, make three checks. First, every line of every journal entry has an account number written beside it, so that nothing was left out. Second, the total of the debit amounts posted equals the total of the credit amounts posted. Third, the balance of each account is on its normal side, or there is a reason why it is not. If a posting is wrong, do not erase it. Write a correcting entry in the journal and post it, so that the trail stays complete (Chapter 8).

> Pause and check: say on which side a posted amount belongs.
*Pause and check.* A journal entry debits Cash by Tk 5,000. On which side of the Cash account is the amount posted, and what number is written in the journal's Ref. column? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> Recap of 7.4: posting copies amounts to the ledger, and each account then has a balance.
*Before moving on.* The ledger holds all the accounts. Posting copies each amount from the journal to the same side of its ledger account, and the two Ref. columns record where each amount came from and where it went. The balance of an account is the difference between its two sides. You should now be able to say: *posting copies each amount, unchanged, from the journal to the same side of the right account.*

## 7.5 Using T-Accounts to Analyse Activity and Find Balances

> A T-account helps us to think, and to find a missing figure.
A full ledger page takes time to prepare. Accountants also use T-accounts as a thinking tool: a quick drawing of one account, used to find a balance or to discover a figure that is not known. This section shows both uses. The method needs one rule.

> The rule: ending balance equals opening balance plus increases minus decreases.
*Formula.* In words: the ending balance of an account equals its opening balance, plus the increases during the period, minus the decreases during the period. In symbols: ending balance = opening balance + increases − decreases. For an account with a debit balance, the increases are the debits. For an account with a credit balance, the increases are the credits.

> A first example uses the bakery's receivables and clean numbers.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* We start with the bakery's Accounts Receivable in January. It had no balance on 1 January. On 15 January the school is billed Tk 40,000, which is a debit. On 18 January the school pays Tk 25,000, which is a credit. The ending balance is 0 + Tk 40,000 − Tk 25,000 = Tk 15,000, a debit balance. The school still owes Tk 15,000.

:table T-account: Accounts Receivable, January 2026 (Mitali Bakery)
| Debit | Tk | Credit | Tk |
| 15 Jan Sales Revenue | 40,000 | 18 Jan Cash | 25,000 |
| | | 31 Jan Balance | 15,000 |
| Total | 40,000 | Total | 40,000 |

> A T-account is balanced by writing the balance on the smaller side.
To balance a T-account in this way, write the balance on the side that has the smaller total. Then the two sides are equal. The word balance on the credit side of the table does not mean a credit entry. It only brings the totals level, and it shows the debit balance that remains.

> A second example has messier numbers and a new business.
A second example has numbers that are less tidy. Karim Stationery, met in section 7.2, is illustrative and is separate from the bakery. *Given.* On 1 March, customers owed Tk 48,500. During March, Karim sold goods on account for Tk 126,300, and customers paid Tk 103,800. *Find.* The amount that customers owe on 31 March. *Steps.* Accounts Receivable has a debit balance, so the opening balance and the sales on account are increases: Tk 48,500 + Tk 126,300 = Tk 174,800. The payments are decreases: Tk 174,800 − Tk 103,800 = Tk 71,000. *Answer.* Customers owe Tk 71,000. *Check.* The two sides of the T-account below are equal, at Tk 174,800.

:table T-account: Accounts Receivable, March (Karim Stationery)
| Debit | Tk | Credit | Tk |
| 1 Mar Balance | 48,500 | Cash received | 103,800 |
| Sales on account | 126,300 | 31 Mar Balance | 71,000 |
| Total | 174,800 | Total | 174,800 |

> The same T-account finds a missing figure when the balance is known.
The T-account also finds a missing figure. Suppose that Karim knows the opening balance, Tk 48,500, the sales on account, Tk 126,300, and the closing balance, Tk 71,000, but not the cash received. The two sides must be equal. So the cash received equals the opening balance plus the sales, minus the closing balance: Tk 48,500 + Tk 126,300 − Tk 71,000 = Tk 103,800. The unknown figure is found by rearranging the same rule.

> The same method works for payables, with the sides exchanged.
The method works for liabilities, with the sides exchanged. Karim's Accounts Payable had a balance of Tk 62,400 on 1 March. He bought goods on account for Tk 95,700, and he paid suppliers Tk 81,250. The balance is a credit balance. Increases are credits: Tk 62,400 + Tk 95,700 = Tk 158,100. The payments are debits: Tk 158,100 − Tk 81,250 = Tk 76,850. Karim owes his suppliers Tk 76,850 on 31 March.

> The same rule finds a missing purchases figure for payables.
The same rule finds other missing figures. Karim knows that suppliers were owed Tk 62,400 on 1 March, that he paid them Tk 81,250, and that he owes them Tk 76,850 on 31 March. For a credit-balance account, the closing balance equals the opening balance plus the credits minus the debits. So the purchases on account equal the closing balance plus the payments minus the opening balance: Tk 76,850 + Tk 81,250 − Tk 62,400 = Tk 95,700. This agrees with the figure given above.

> Viewpoint: the same number says different things to the owner and the lender.
The same two numbers tell different stories from different seats. The owner sees Tk 71,000 of cash that is still to come from customers, and Tk 76,850 of cash that must go to suppliers. A lender may compare the two numbers to judge whether the business can meet its debts. A good ending balance, then, is more than a figure. It is a fact that someone will use to make a decision.

> Common slips in T-account work: the wrong side, a forgotten opening balance, mixed months.
*Common slips.* Three slips occur often. The first is treating a liability like an asset, and adding the debits. The second is forgetting the opening balance, which is part of the total. The third is mixing the figures of two different months. Write the opening balance first, in every T-account, and name the month at the top.

> Pause and check: find an ending balance.
*Pause and check.* A business had Accounts Receivable of Tk 20,000 on 1 June. It sold goods on account for Tk 35,000 in June, and customers paid Tk 28,000. What is the balance on 30 June? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> Recap of 7.5: ending balance equals opening plus increases minus decreases.
*Before moving on.* The ending balance of an account equals the opening balance, plus the increases, minus the decreases. For a debit-balance account the increases are debits, and for a credit-balance account they are credits. The same rule, rearranged, finds a missing figure.

## 7.6 Journal and Ledger Compared

> The two books do different jobs, and a business needs both.
The journal and the ledger hold the same amounts, but they arrange them in different ways, and they answer different questions. The table below sets them side by side. Read each row from left to right: the feature, how the journal treats it, and how the ledger treats it.

:table The journal and the ledger compared
| Feature | Journal | Ledger |
| Arranged by | Date | Account |
| Written | First | Second, by posting from the journal |
| Each transaction | Appears once, with both sides together | Appears in two accounts, one side in each |
| Explanation | A narration for each entry | A short explanation on each line |
| Main use | Following events, and tracing them to their proof | Finding amounts and balances |
| Question it answers | What happened, and when? | How much, in each account? |

> A trace from a ledger line back to the source document answers a lender's question.
Here is a trace. A lender asks why the Cash account shows a debit of Tk 12,000 on 22 January. The ledger line carries J2, so the lender turns to page 2 of the journal and finds the entry of 22 January: Cash debited, Unearned Revenue credited, with the narration *Advance for six weekly cake deliveries*. The entry points to the advance receipt, which is the source document. In three steps the question is answered, and each step can be checked.

> Why two books? Each one makes a task easy that the other makes hard.
Why not keep one book? A single book arranged by date makes it hard to find a balance. A single book arranged by account hides the story of each event, and it allows half-written transactions. Two books keep both advantages, and the Ref. columns tie them together. An auditor can start from a ledger amount and find the journal entry and then the source document, or can start from the source document and follow the trail forward. Chapter 15 shows how a very busy business divides the journal into several books to save effort.

> Recap of 7.6: the journal tells the story, and the ledger gives the totals.
*Before moving on.* The journal arranges transactions by date and records each once. The ledger arranges amounts by account and gives the balances. You should now be able to say: *the journal tells what happened and when, and the ledger tells how much.*

## Summary

> Each transaction is first recorded in the journal, in date order, with a narration.
A source document proves that a transaction happened. The transaction is first written in the journal, in date order, as a journal entry with its debits, its credits and a narration. A compound entry affects three or more accounts, and its debits still equal its credits.

> Posting copies each amount to the same side of its ledger account, and each account then has a balance.
The ledger holds all the accounts. Posting copies each amount from the journal to the same side of its ledger account, and the Ref. columns of both books record the copying. The balance of an account is the difference between its two sides, and it equals the opening balance plus the increases minus the decreases.

> The journal tells what happened and the ledger tells how much; both belong to the accounting cycle.
The journal tells what happened and when, and the ledger tells how much. Both are steps of the accounting cycle, which repeats in every period and ends in the financial statements.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: posting only one side of an entry.
*"I posted the debit, and the credit can wait."* A half-posted entry makes the ledger disagree with the journal. Post every line of an entry in the same sitting, and then write the account number against each line in the journal.

> Mistake two: posting to the opposite side.
*"The amount is right, so the side does not matter."* The side is half of the information. A debit posted to the right side of an account is read as a credit, and the balance becomes wrong by twice the amount.

> Mistake three: writing in the ledger before the journal.
*"The ledger is the important book, so I will write in it first."* The journal is the book of first record. It keeps both sides together, in date order, with a narration. Without it, the trail from the ledger to the source document is lost.

> Mistake four: reading a credit balance as money owed to the business.
*"A credit balance means that someone owes us."* A credit balance has no fixed meaning. In Accounts Payable it means that the business owes money. In Sales Revenue it means that the business has earned money. The kind of account decides the meaning.

> Mistake five: using the account names in the narration.
*"The narration should say: debit Cash, credit Sales."* The entry already says that. The narration should tell a reader what happened, in plain words, such as *Sold baked goods for cash*.

> Mistake six: forgetting the opening balance in a T-account.
*"I only need the new debits and credits."* The ending balance includes the opening balance. If it is left out, the balance is wrong by exactly that amount, and the T-account will not balance.

## In History

> Pacioli described three books: a rough record, the journal and the ledger.
Luca Pacioli, whose treatise of 1494 was met in Chapter 6, advised the merchant to keep three books. The first, the memorandum, was a rough record of events, written as they happened and in no fixed form. The second, the journal, gave each event in orderly form, with the accounts that were to be debited and credited. The third, the ledger, held the accounts, and it came with an alphabetical index so that any account could be found.

> The modern business still keeps the same chain, with source documents in the place of the memorandum.
The chain from rough record to journal to ledger is still the chain of today. The invoices, receipts and cheques of a modern business do the work of the memorandum, because they are the first evidence. The journal and the ledger kept their names and their jobs. The method has lasted because it gives each task its own book, and because the books point to each other.
