It then shows how the finished sheet supplies the statements and the entries.

> What this chapter needs from earlier chapters.

*You will need.* Chapter 8 (trial balance), Chapter 9 (adjusting entries, adjusted trial balance) and Chapter 10 (depreciation).

> On 31 January the bakery's accountant faces six adjustments.

On 31 January 2026 the books of Mitali Bakery show the unadjusted trial balance of Chapter 8, with debits and credits of Tk 792,000 each. Six adjustments must be made, and you met them in Chapter 9: rent used, supplies used, depreciation, an advance earned, wages owed and revenue earned but not billed. The accountant could journalise each one, post it to the ledger, and then prepare a new trial balance.

> The direct way has two weaknesses: late knowledge and hard corrections.

That direct way has two weaknesses. The first is that nobody knows the profit until the last adjustment is posted. The second is more serious. Suppose that the accountant posts four adjustments and then finds that the count of supplies was wrong. Four entries, which are now permanent records in the ledger, must be corrected by further entries. A permanent book is a poor place to try an idea.

> A question: can the adjustments be tried on paper first?

Is there a better way? Could the accountant try all six adjustments on a single sheet of paper, see the effect on every account, check that the totals agree, and learn the profit, all before one entry touches the ledger? The answer is yes, and the sheet has a name. This chapter explains the sheet, shows how to build it, and says what it can and cannot do.

> The five sections in order.

Section 11.1 explains why a working paper is useful. Section 11.2 describes the ten columns. Section 11.3 builds the sheet step by step, first for a small business and then for the bakery. Section 11.4 shows how the sheet supplies the statements and the entries. Section 11.5 lists the common slips and the checks that find them.

## 11.1 The puzzle of the busy accountant: why a working paper

> A market trader's rough page shows the idea.

Think first of a trader at a market. At night she counts the day's takings. She writes numbers on a rough page, adds them, crosses out a wrong figure, and adds them again. Only when the figures agree does she copy the final amounts into her record book. The rough page may be untidy, because nobody will read it. The record book must be tidy and permanent, because other people will rely on it.

> A working paper is a paper for the accountant's own use.

@ Working paper | A paper used by the accountant for working only; it is not a financial statement.
The accountant also needs a rough page. A **working paper** means a paper that the accountant uses for working only. It is not a financial statement, it is not part of the books, and it is not shown to the bank or to the tax office. Accountants use many working papers: a list of the invoices that are unpaid, a count of the supplies on hand, a calculation of the depreciation. The most important of them has its own name.

> The worksheet gathers the figures of the period end in columns.

@ Worksheet | A working paper in which the accountant prepares adjustments and statement figures in columns.
The **worksheet** means a working paper in which the accountant gathers, in columns, the trial balance, the adjustments, and the figures for the financial statements. Each account has one line, and each step of the work has its own columns. A reader can follow one account from left to right and see what happened to it.

### What the worksheet does

> The sheet does four jobs for the accountant.

The worksheet does four jobs. First, it puts on one page the figures that would otherwise lie in the trial balance, the journal and the ledger. Second, it lets the accountant test the adjustments before they are entered, so a wrong figure can be crossed out. Third, it shows the profit or the loss before any statement is prepared. Fourth, it supplies the figures for the statements, for the adjusting entries, and for the closing entries of Chapter 12.

### What the worksheet does not do

> The books do not change until the entries are made.

The worksheet has one important limit. Writing an adjustment on the worksheet does not enter it in the books. The ledger stays as it was until the accountant journalises and posts the adjusting entries. A beginner who completes a worksheet and stops has finished the rough page and has not yet touched the books. The worksheet is also not a financial statement: its columns have no formal headings of a statement, and no outside reader receives it.

### Who prepares it, and when

> The accountant prepares it at the period end, and the owner may read it.

The accountant, or a bookkeeper working for the accountant, prepares the worksheet at the end of an accounting period, after the unadjusted trial balance is agreed and before the adjusting entries are made. The owner may read the sheet, because it shows the profit early. A business that reports every month, as the bakery does, may prepare one sheet each month, or one sheet for a quarter or for a year. The worksheet is also optional. A business may prepare its statements directly from the adjusted ledger, and many small firms do. But for a beginner, and for any trial balance with many accounts, the worksheet is a strong aid to accuracy.

### The sheet seen from three seats

> The owner, the accountant and the lender see the sheet differently.

The same sheet looks different from different seats. The owner sees an early answer to the question, "Did I earn a profit this month?" The accountant sees a safe place to try the adjustments and to find slips before they reach the books. The lender, such as a bank, never sees the sheet. The bank receives the financial statements, which the accountant prepares from the sheet. A reader of accounts should therefore not ask a business for its worksheet. The right request is for its statements.

> Before moving on: the idea of a working paper.

*Before moving on.* A working paper is for the accountant's use, and the worksheet is the chief working paper. It gathers the trial balance, the adjustments and the statement figures in columns. It does not change the books, and it is not a financial statement.

## 11.2 The ten columns explained

> The sheet has an account column and five pairs of columns.

The most common form of worksheet has an account column on the left and ten number columns on the right. The ten columns form five pairs, and each pair has a debit column and a credit column.

> The ten-column worksheet holds five steps of the work.

@ Ten-column worksheet | A worksheet with five pairs of debit and credit columns.
A **ten-column worksheet** means a worksheet with five pairs of debit and credit columns. The five pairs are the trial balance, the adjustments, the adjusted trial balance, the income statement, and the balance sheet.^1 The diagram below shows the layout and the way the figures move from left to right.

:fig The ten-column worksheet: five pairs of columns and the work that each pair holds. | ch11_fig1.svg

> Pair one is the unadjusted trial balance from the ledger.

*Pair 1: trial balance.* The accountant copies the balance of every ledger account from the unadjusted trial balance of Chapter 8. A debit balance goes in the debit column and a credit balance goes in the credit column. The two columns must have equal totals. If they do not, the books contain an error, and the adjustments must wait until it is found.

> Pair two holds the adjusting entries as debits and credits.

*Pair 2: adjustments.* The accountant writes each adjusting entry across the sheet, with its debit in the debit column and its credit in the credit column. Each entry receives a small letter, (a), (b), (c) and so on, which is written beside both of its amounts. The letters let a reader find the other half of an entry. The two columns must have equal totals, because each entry has equal debits and credits. An adjustment may need an account that the trial balance does not have. Accumulated Depreciation and Wages Payable are two examples. The accountant adds such an account on a new line, with nil in the trial balance columns.

> Pair three combines pairs one and two.

*Pair 3: adjusted trial balance.* For each account, the accountant combines the unadjusted balance with the adjustments. This pair is the adjusted trial balance of Chapter 9, and its totals must be equal. Section 11.3 gives the rule for the combining.

> Pairs four and five split the adjusted balances by kind of account.

*Pairs 4 and 5: income statement and balance sheet.* The accountant now sorts every adjusted balance into one of two pairs of columns. Revenue accounts and expense accounts go to the income statement columns. Asset, liability, capital and drawings accounts go to the balance sheet columns. A balance stays on the same side, debit or credit, as in pair 3. The next table gives the sorting rule.

:table Where each kind of account goes in the last two pairs
| Kind of account | Normal balance | Columns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset (Cash, Accounts Receivable, Equipment) | Debit | Balance sheet, debit |
| Contra asset (Accumulated Depreciation) | Credit | Balance sheet, credit |
| Liability (Accounts Payable, Wages Payable) | Credit | Balance sheet, credit |
| Owner's capital | Credit | Balance sheet, credit |
| Owner's drawings | Debit | Balance sheet, debit |
| Revenue | Credit | Income statement, credit |
| Expense | Debit | Income statement, debit |

> Drawings and capital stay in the balance sheet columns until Chapter 12.

Two entries in the table cause surprise. Drawings is an equity account, and yet it goes to the balance sheet columns, not to the income statement. The reason is that drawings is not an expense: the owner's withdrawal does not earn or use revenue. The capital figure in these columns is the opening capital, before profit and drawings. Chapter 12 closes the accounts and combines all three into the closing capital. Until then the balance sheet columns hold the three items separately.

### How the lines are arranged

> Accounts keep the order of the chart; added accounts take their place in it.

The lines of the sheet follow the order of the chart of accounts, which you met in Chapter 6: assets first, then liabilities, then owner's capital and drawings, then revenue, then expenses. An account that the adjustments add takes its place in that order. Accumulated Depreciation, for instance, stands directly under Equipment, because it is deducted from Equipment. Following the chart means that a reader always knows where to look for an account, and the pairs of columns can be added in blocks of related lines.

> An account with no balance still needs a line if an adjustment uses it.

An account may have no balance in the trial balance and still need a line. Rent Expense has no balance on 31 January, because the bakery paid its rent in advance and recorded the payment as Prepaid Rent. The adjustment creates the expense. A student who lists only the accounts of the trial balance will find that the adjustment has nowhere to go.

> Pause and check: four accounts, four pairs of columns.

*Pause and check.* In which pair of columns, income statement or balance sheet, does each of these accounts belong: Prepaid Rent, Rent Expense, Owner's Drawings, Wages Payable? On which side, debit or credit, does each stand?

> Before moving on: five pairs, and where each figure comes from.

*Before moving on.* The five pairs are: trial balance, adjustments, adjusted trial balance, income statement, balance sheet. Pair 1 comes from the ledger, pair 2 from the adjusting entries, and pair 3 from combining pairs 1 and 2. Pairs 4 and 5 split pair 3 by kind of account.

## 11.3 Building the worksheet step by step

@! Go slowly here: this is the first full build.
> A full build has five steps, and each has one rule.

This section builds a worksheet in full for the first time. We take it slowly. The build has five steps, each with one simple rule. We follow the steps first on a small business with four adjustments, so that the numbers are easy, and then on the bakery.

### The five steps

> Step one: heading, accounts and trial balance.

*Step 1.* Write the heading: the name of the business, the words *Worksheet*, and the period, for example *for the month ended 31 January 2026*. List the accounts in the order of the chart of accounts. Copy the unadjusted trial balance into pair 1, and total it. Add a line for each account that the adjustments will need and that the trial balance does not hold.

> Step two: the adjustments, with letters.

*Step 2.* Write the adjusting entries in pair 2, one letter for each entry. Total the two columns. The totals must be equal.

> Step three: combine the trial balance and the adjustments.

*Step 3.* Combine pairs 1 and 2 into pair 3. The rule in words is this: when the adjustment is on the same side as the unadjusted balance, add; when it is on the opposite side, subtract. A debit balance of 30,000 with a credit adjustment of 22,000 gives a debit balance of 8,000. Total pair 3. The totals must be equal.

> Step four: sort each balance into the statement columns.

*Step 4.* Sort every line of pair 3 into pair 4 or pair 5, by the rule in the table of section 11.2. Keep each amount on the same side.

> Step five: total, and find the balancing figure.

@ Balancing figure | The amount needed to make two totals equal; here, the profit or loss.
*Step 5.* Total each of the last four columns. The two income statement columns will be unequal. The difference is the **balancing figure**, which means the amount needed to make two totals equal. Here it is the profit or the loss. If the credit total of the income statement columns is larger, the difference is a profit, and it is written in the debit column to make the two totals equal. The same amount is then written in the credit column of the balance sheet pair, because profit adds to the owner's equity. The balance sheet totals then agree. If the debit total of the income statement is larger, the difference is a loss, and it is placed the other way round.

> Recap: five steps, one rule each.

*Recap.* Copy the trial balance; write the adjustments; combine them; sort the results; total and find the balancing figure. Each pair must agree, except the last two, which agree only after the balancing figure is added.

### Following three accounts across the sheet

> One account at a time shows what each pair of columns does.

Before the full builds, follow three accounts of the bakery from the left side of the sheet to the right. This is the best way to see what each pair does. The accounts are Prepaid Rent, Sales Revenue and Wages Payable, and the table gives the stages.

:table Three accounts of the bakery followed across the worksheet (Tk)
| Account | Trial balance | Adjustment | Adjusted balance | Goes to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prepaid Rent | 45,000 debit | (a) 15,000 credit | 30,000 debit | Balance sheet, debit |
| Sales Revenue | 200,000 credit | (d) 2,000 and (f) 10,000 credit | 212,000 credit | Income statement, credit |
| Wages Payable | nil | (e) 4,000 credit | 4,000 credit | Balance sheet, credit |

> Read each row from left to right as a short story.

Read each row as a short story. Prepaid Rent started the month at Tk 45,000, because the bakery paid three months of rent on 3 January. One month of the rent has been used, so the adjustment removes Tk 15,000, and Tk 30,000 remains as an asset in the balance sheet. Sales Revenue started at Tk 200,000. The adjustments add Tk 12,000 of revenue that was earned and not yet recorded, so the revenue grows to Tk 212,000 and goes to the income statement. Wages Payable had no balance, because no wages were owed in the books; the adjustment creates a liability of Tk 4,000.

> The facts: four adjustments and the question.

*Given.* The trial balance above, and four adjustments. (a) A count shows that Tk 3,000 of supplies is on hand, so Tk 5,000 was used. (b) The prepaid rent of Tk 12,000 covered three months, and one month has passed, so Tk 4,000 is used. (c) Depreciation of the equipment is Tk 1,000 for the month. (d) Wages of Tk 2,000 are owed and not yet paid. *Find.* The completed worksheet and the profit for April.

> The formula is the rule of step three and the rule of step five.

*Formula.* Adjusted balance = unadjusted balance, plus adjustments on the same side, minus adjustments on the opposite side. Profit = total of the income statement credits − total of the income statement debits.

> The steps are shown in the figure, and we read them in order.

*Steps.* The figure below shows the finished worksheet. Follow it in order. Pair 1 copies the table above, and each side totals Tk 92,000. Five accounts are missing from the trial balance, and the accountant adds them on new lines: Accumulated Depreciation, Wages Payable, Rent Expense, Supplies Expense and Depreciation Expense. In pair 2, adjustment (a) debits Supplies Expense and credits Supplies for Tk 5,000. Adjustment (b) debits Rent Expense and credits Prepaid Rent for Tk 4,000. Adjustment (c) debits Depreciation Expense and credits Accumulated Depreciation for Tk 1,000. Adjustment (d) debits Wages Expense and credits Wages Payable for Tk 2,000. Each side of pair 2 totals Tk 12,000.

:fig Rina's tailoring shop: the completed ten-column worksheet for April 2026 (Tk). Letters (a) to (d) mark the four adjustments. | ch11_fig2.svg

> Pairs three to five follow from the rules of steps three to five.

*Steps, continued.* In pair 3, Supplies becomes 8,000 − 5,000 = 3,000 debit. Prepaid Rent becomes 12,000 − 4,000 = 8,000 debit. Wages Expense becomes 10,000 + 2,000 = 12,000 debit. The three new accounts take their adjustment amounts. Pair 3 totals Tk 95,000 on each side. Revenue and expense lines go to pair 4: debits of 12,000, 4,000, 5,000, 2,000 and 1,000, which total Tk 24,000, and a credit of Tk 35,000 for sales. Everything else goes to pair 5: debits of 71,000 and credits of 60,000. The balancing figure is 35,000 − 24,000 = Tk 11,000, a profit. It is written in the debit column of pair 4, and in the credit column of pair 5. Pair 4 then totals 35,000 on each side, and pair 5 totals 71,000 on each side.

> The answer, and three checks that the sheet is right.

*Answer.* Rina's profit for April is Tk 11,000. *Check.* There are three tests. The totals of pair 2 are equal, at 12,000. The totals of pair 3 are equal, at 95,000. And the balancing figure for the income statement, 11,000, is equal to the balancing figure for the balance sheet, 11,000. A sheet that passes all three tests is arithmetically sound.

> Recap and a test sentence for the first build.

*Recap.* The sheet takes the trial balance from left to right through the adjustments to the two statements, and the profit appears as one figure, used twice. *You should now be able to say:* the worksheet combines the trial balance with the adjustments, sorts the result into the income statement and the balance sheet, and finds the profit as the balancing figure that makes both pairs agree.

> Pause and check: two questions on the small sheet.

*Pause and check.* (1) In Rina's sheet, why does Supplies appear in the balance sheet columns, and Supplies Expense in the income statement columns? (2) If Rina's wages owed were Tk 3,000 and not Tk 2,000, which totals on the sheet would change, and what would the profit become?

### When the result is a loss

> The rule reverses when expenses are larger than revenue.

The bakery earned a profit in January, but a business may have a loss, and the rule then reverses. Suppose that the income statement columns of a small shop total debits of Tk 30,000 and credits of Tk 24,000. The debits are larger, so the shop has a loss of 30,000 − 24,000 = Tk 6,000. The loss is written in the credit column of the income statement pair, and both columns then total Tk 30,000. The same Tk 6,000 is written in the debit column of the balance sheet pair, because a loss reduces the owner's equity. The two balancing figures are again equal, and the balance sheet columns agree.

## 11.4 From the worksheet to the statements and entries

> The finished sheet supplies four things.

A finished worksheet is useful only when its figures are used. It supplies four things: the income statement, the balance sheet with the statement of owner's equity, the adjusting entries, and the closing entries. The diagram below shows where each one comes from.

:fig From the worksheet to the statements and the entries. | ch11_fig4.svg

> The income statement is read from pair 4.

*The income statement.* Pair 4 holds all the revenue and expense accounts. Its credit side shows revenue of Tk 212,000. Its debit side lists the expenses of Tk 85,000: wages Tk 40,000, supplies Tk 22,000, rent Tk 15,000, utilities Tk 6,000 and depreciation Tk 2,000. The difference is the profit of Tk 127,000. Chapter 12 sets these figures in the proper form of a statement, with a heading and a layout. The worksheet gives only the figures.

> The balance sheet and the equity statement come from pair 5.

*The balance sheet and the statement of owner's equity.* Pair 5 holds the assets, the liabilities, the capital, the drawings and the profit. The assets, less the accumulated depreciation, are 400,000 + 25,000 + 8,000 + 30,000 + 240,000 − 2,000 = Tk 701,000. The liabilities are 80,000 + 10,000 + 4,000 = Tk 94,000. Liabilities plus equity are 94,000 + 607,000 = Tk 701,000, and they equal the assets.

| (e) | Wages Expense | Wages Payable | 4,000 |

> The two statement pairs also give the closing entries.

*The closing entries.* Pairs 4 and 5 show which accounts are temporary and which are permanent. Chapter 12 uses the revenue, expense and drawings figures to write the closing entries. The worksheet saves the accountant from searching the ledger for them.

> A worksheet on paper has become a spreadsheet on a screen.

Today most accountants use spreadsheet software on a computer, and not paper. The layout is the same, with columns for the trial balance, the adjustments and the statement figures. The software adds the columns and combines the figures. The accountant must still decide which adjustments are needed and in which column each account belongs, because the software cannot decide that.

> Before moving on: what the sheet supplies and what it leaves undone.

*Before moving on.* The worksheet supplies the figures of the two statements, the list of adjusting entries and the figures for closing. It does not record the entries. The accountant must still journalise and post them.

## 11.5 Common slips and checks

> Most slips fall into five kinds.

The worksheet is mostly arithmetic, and most slips are small. Students make five kinds of slip. They forget to add a new account that an adjustment needs. They place a figure in the wrong column, for example a credit balance in a debit column. They add an adjustment when they should subtract it. They place drawings or capital in the income statement columns. And they treat the agreement of totals as proof that every figure is right.

> A slip in one cell is found by the difference between the totals.

Here is a worked slip. Suppose that in the bakery's sheet the accountant adds the prepaid rent adjustment of Tk 15,000 to the balance, and writes 60,000 for Prepaid Rent in pair 3, instead of subtracting it to give 30,000. The debit total of pair 3 becomes Tk 838,000, and the credit total stays at Tk 808,000. The difference is 838,000 − 808,000 = Tk 30,000. This difference is exactly twice the adjustment of Tk 15,000. A difference that is twice an amount is a strong sign that the amount has been placed on the wrong side, or added when it should have been subtracted. The accountant looks first for an amount of half the difference.

> A second slip hides inside the balancing figure.

A second slip is harder to find, because the usual check does not catch it. Suppose that the accountant writes the Utilities Expense of Tk 6,000 in the balance sheet debit column and not in the income statement debit column. Pair 3 is correct and balanced, so the sorting looks reasonable. But the income statement debits now total 85,000 − 6,000 = Tk 79,000, and the profit appears as 212,000 − 79,000 = Tk 133,000. The balance sheet debits grow to Tk 729,000 and the credits, with the profit, become 596,000 + 133,000 = Tk 729,000. The two balancing figures are both Tk 133,000, so they agree, and the sheet seems sound.

> Worked slip: the profit is checked against an independent calculation.

*Given.* A worksheet whose two balancing figures are both Tk 133,000, and an adjusted trial balance with revenue of Tk 212,000 and expense accounts that add to Tk 85,000. *Find.* Whether the profit is correct, and if not, which line is wrong. *Steps.* Add the expense balances of the adjusted trial balance: 40,000 + 15,000 + 22,000 + 6,000 + 2,000 = Tk 85,000. The profit should be 212,000 − 85,000 = Tk 127,000. The sheet shows Tk 133,000, which is Tk 6,000 too high. Look for an expense of Tk 6,000 and you find Utilities Expense in the wrong pair. *Answer.* The utilities line belongs in the income statement debit column. *Check.* After moving it, the income statement debits are Tk 85,000, the profit is Tk 127,000, and the balance sheet columns total Tk 723,000 on each side, as before.

> Lesson: equal balancing figures do not prove that every line is in the right pair.

The lesson is that equal balancing figures show only that the copying and the adding are sound. They do not show that every line stands in the right pair. The best test is an independent one: add the revenue and expense balances of pair 3 separately, and compare the result with the balancing figure.

> Five checks, and what a failure means.

The next table lists the checks that every worksheet should pass, and the meaning of a failure.

:table Checks on a ten-column worksheet
| Check | If it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Pair 1 totals are equal | The ledger or the trial balance has an error; stop and correct it first (Chapter 8) |
| Pair 2 totals are equal | An adjusting entry is unbalanced, or an amount is on one side only |
| Pair 3 totals are equal | An error in combining the figures of pairs 1 and 2 |
| Pair 4 and pair 5 balancing figures are equal | A figure was lost, copied wrongly or added wrongly after pair 3 |
| Net profit agrees with revenue minus expenses taken from pair 3 | A line stands in the wrong pair, or a line is missing |

> Agreement of the totals does not prove that every figure is right.

A last warning comes from Chapter 8. A worksheet whose totals agree can still be wrong. If the accountant records the supplies used as Tk 20,000 on both sides of pair 2 when the count shows Tk 22,000, every total still agrees, and the profit is wrong by Tk 2,000. Only the source documents can reveal such an error, in this case the count of supplies. The checks above test the arithmetic, and they do not test the facts.

> Before moving on: five slips and a method.

*Before moving on.* The common slips are a missing account, a wrong column, a wrong sign, a wrong statement pair, and false trust in agreeing totals. A difference between totals that equals twice an amount points to a wrong side. A line in the wrong pair is found by an independent calculation of the profit.

## Summary

> The chapter in one paragraph.

The worksheet is a working paper in which the accountant gathers the trial balance, the adjustments and the statement figures in columns. It is not a financial statement, and it does not change the books. The ten-column form has five pairs of columns: trial balance, adjustments, adjusted trial balance, income statement and balance sheet. The accountant copies the trial balance, writes the adjustments, combines them, sorts the balances by kind of account, and finds the balancing figure that is the profit or the loss.

> The use of the sheet in one paragraph.

The balancing figure appears in both statement pairs, and the two must agree. The finished sheet supplies the figures for the income statement, the balance sheet and the statement of owner's equity, the list of adjusting entries, and the closing entries. The entries must still be journalised and posted. Agreeing totals prove only the arithmetic, so the facts must be checked against the source documents.

## Where people go wrong

> Mistake one: believing that the worksheet records the adjustments.

*"The worksheet has the adjustments, so the books are adjusted."* They are not. The worksheet is a rough page. The adjusting entries must still be journalised and posted.

> Mistake two: treating the worksheet as a financial statement.

*"The worksheet is the income statement."* It is not. It holds the figures, but it has no statement form, and it is shown to no outside reader.

> Mistake three: putting drawings in the income statement.

*"Drawings is an expense, so it goes in the income statement columns."* It is not an expense. Drawings goes in the balance sheet columns.

> Mistake four: forgetting to add the new accounts.

*"The adjustment has no account, so I leave it out."* An adjustment may need an account that the trial balance does not hold. Add a new line for it.

> Mistake five: trusting equal totals.

*"The totals agree, so every figure is right."* Agreement shows that the arithmetic is sound. It does not show that the supplies count, the rent, or the wages are correct.

> Mistake six: placing profit in the wrong columns.

*"The profit goes in one column only."* The same figure goes twice: in the income statement pair, to make it agree, and in the balance sheet pair, to make that agree. A loss is placed the other way round.

## In history

> The first spreadsheet program automated a paper of columns.

Accountants prepared worksheets by hand, on large sheets of paper ruled in columns. In 1979 the first spreadsheet program for personal computers, VisiCalc, was released for the Apple II computer. It was written by Dan Bricklin, who conceived the idea as a student at the Harvard Business School in 1978, and by Bob Frankston. The program let a user enter figures in a grid of rows and columns, and the totals changed at once when a figure changed.

> The same layout now serves every business.

The grid of VisiCalc had the shape of the paper sheets of rows and columns that accountants and financial analysts used, with the difference that the arithmetic did itself. Many customers bought an Apple II computer in order to run this one program. Today's spreadsheet programs keep the same idea. A student who has understood the ten columns of this chapter will find the layout familiar on a screen.
