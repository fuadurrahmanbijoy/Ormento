:chapter XX | The Company and Its Shares
:mood A business can outgrow its founders. The company form changes who owns it, who owes its debts, and how capital is raised.
:desc This chapter explains the company as a separate legal form, the main kinds of shares, share issues, treasury shares, and the equity section.

> A partnership can begin with two people, but a growing business may need a different structure for ownership and capital.

A **company** is a form of business organisation in which the business is carried on through an incorporated body. The people who own shares are members or shareholders. The company can hold property and incur obligations in its own name.

The accounting difference is important. In a partnership, the accounts follow the partners' capital and current interests. In a company, ownership is represented through shares and the equity section is organised around share capital and other components of shareholders' equity.

> The company form separates the business from the people who own its shares.

Bangladesh's Companies Act, 1994 recognises public and private companies and provides rules concerning incorporation, shares and share capital. The exact legal requirements depend on the type of company and the current law, so accounting treatment should not be confused with legal procedure. citeturn1search3turn1search1

@ Company | An incorporated business organisation whose rights and obligations are legally separated from those of its owners.

The chapter follows Mitali Bakery as its partnership is converted into an illustrative company. The numbers are teaching figures, not a legal filing model.

## 20.1 What a company is: separate entity and limited liability

> The first question is not how many shares a company has, but who owns the business and who owes its debts.

A **separate legal entity** means that the company is treated as a legal person distinct from its shareholders. This does not mean that a company is a human being. It means that the law can recognise the company as the holder of rights and obligations.

For accounting, the idea is practical. If Mitali Bakery Limited buys an oven, the oven belongs to the company. If the company owes a supplier, the debt belongs to the company. A shareholder does not record the company's oven as personal property merely because she owns shares in the company.

> Separate entity changes the accounting boundary.

The distinction can be seen through a simple comparison.

:table Company and partnership compared
| Point | Partnership | Company |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ownership | Partners | Shareholders |
| Main ownership record | Partners' capital accounts | Share capital and other equity |
| Legal identity | Usually tied to the partners under the applicable law | Incorporated entity |
| Liability | Partners may have unlimited liability | Limited liability may apply where the company is formed with limited liability |
| Transfer of ownership | Partnership agreement and law govern the change | Shares provide a mechanism for ownership interests |
| Continuity | May depend on agreement and legal events | Generally has continuing legal existence subject to law |
| Raising ownership capital | New or existing partners | Issue of shares |

@ Separate legal entity | The company is legally distinct from the persons who own its shares.

A **limited liability** company limits the shareholders' liability according to the legal structure of the company. The shareholder's investment can fall in value, but that is different from saying that every company debt automatically becomes the shareholder's personal debt.

> Limited liability protects the boundary between company obligations and shareholder exposure, subject to law.

Suppose Mitali owns shares costing Tk 100,000 in Mitali Bakery Limited. The company later owes a supplier Tk 300,000. The supplier's claim is against the company. Mitali does not record the Tk 300,000 supplier liability in her personal books merely because she owns the shares.

This protection is one reason the company form is useful for larger or growing businesses. It also creates a need for formal records, governance and disclosure.

Bangladesh's Companies Act defines a public company as an incorporated company that is not a private company. It defines a private company through restrictions in its articles concerning share transfer, public invitations to subscribe for shares or debentures, and membership limits. citeturn1search3

@ Limited liability | A legal arrangement under which shareholders' liability for company obligations is limited according to the company's legal form.

### Private and public companies

> Private and public companies differ mainly in how ownership is organised and offered, not merely in size.

A **private company** is subject to restrictions specified by law and its constitutional documents. Under the Bangladesh Companies Act, the statutory definition includes restrictions on transfer of shares, invitations to the public to subscribe, and the number of members, subject to the Act's detailed provisions. citeturn1search3

A **public company** is not a private company under that Act. Public status therefore should not be reduced to the informal idea that the company is “large”.

@ Private company | A company that satisfies the legal conditions defining a private company, including specified restrictions on share transfer and public subscription.

@ Public company | A company that is not a private company under the applicable company law.

The accounting lesson is narrower than company law. This book needs the distinction because ownership, shares and equity are affected by the legal form. It does not attempt to teach the complete procedure for incorporation or public offering.

> Accounting records the financial consequences; company law determines the legal framework.

### Why the company form changes the accounting

A partnership's profit is allocated among partners according to the partnership arrangement. A company instead reports profit as part of the company's financial performance. When the company declares a **dividend**, a distribution may be made to shareholders according to the rights attached to their shares and the applicable law.

@ Dividend | A distribution of company profit to shareholders.

Profit that is not distributed can remain in the company as **retained earnings**.

@ Retained earnings | Accumulated profit kept in the business rather than distributed to shareholders.

The equity section therefore tells a different story from a partnership capital account. It asks: How much share capital has been issued? How much has been received above par value? How much profit has been retained? Has the company acquired its own shares under the applicable legal rules?

> The equity section explains the sources and movements of owners' claims against the company.

### Advantages and limits

The company form can support larger pools of capital, continuity and a clearer separation between ownership and the business. It can also make ownership easier to divide into shares.

But incorporation brings costs. Companies need formal records, compliance, governance and reporting. Limited liability is not a promise that investment cannot be lost. Shareholders can lose the amount they invested.

> A company is not “better” in every situation; it is a different legal and accounting structure.

## 20.2 Share capital and the kinds of share

> A share turns an ownership interest into a unit that can be recorded, divided and transferred according to the company's rules.

A **share** is one of the units into which company ownership is divided.

@ Share | One of the equal units into which a company's ownership is divided.

A **shareholder** is a person or organisation that owns shares.

@ Shareholder | A person or organisation that owns shares in a company.

The company records its share capital separately from other forms of equity. A share may have a stated **par value**, although par value is not the same thing as the price at which investors buy or sell the share.

@ Par value | The stated value assigned to a share in the company's capital structure; it is not necessarily its market price.

For example, suppose a company issues 10,000 ordinary shares with a par value of Tk 10 each. The par value of the issued shares is:

10,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 100,000

If the company receives Tk 12 per share, it receives Tk 120,000. The excess of Tk 20,000 over par value is recorded separately as share premium under the accounting framework being used.

@ Share capital | The amount attributed to shares issued by a company according to the applicable accounting and legal framework.

@ Share premium | The amount received for shares above their par value.

### Authorised and issued shares

> A company may be permitted to have more shares than it has actually issued.

**Authorised shares** are the maximum number of shares that the company's legal or constitutional framework permits it to issue, where that concept applies.

**Issued shares** are shares that the company has actually issued to shareholders.

@ Authorised shares | The maximum number of shares a company is permitted to issue under its governing framework, where applicable.

@ Issued shares | Shares that a company has actually issued.

Suppose Mitali Bakery Limited is authorised to issue 50,000 ordinary shares but initially issues only 20,000. The authorised amount and issued amount are therefore different.

The distinction prevents a common mistake: treating every share a company is permitted to issue as money already invested by shareholders.

> Authorisation is capacity; issue is an actual transaction.

### Ordinary shares

An **ordinary share** normally represents a basic ownership interest. It commonly carries voting rights and a residual claim on profits and net assets, subject to the company's terms and applicable law.

@ Ordinary share | A basic class of share that normally carries voting and residual ownership rights, subject to its terms and applicable law.

If the company earns a profit, ordinary shareholders may receive dividends. They do not normally receive a guaranteed dividend simply because they own ordinary shares.

### Preference shares

A **preference share** is a class of share with specified preferential rights, commonly concerning dividends or return of capital.

@ Preference share | A class of share whose terms give its holders specified preferential rights over ordinary shareholders.

The word “preference” does not mean that every preference share has identical rights. The terms may differ concerning cumulative dividends, voting, conversion or redemption.

> Always read the rights attached to a share class before assuming how it behaves.

### Ordinary and preference shares

:table Two broad share classes
| Feature | Ordinary share | Preference share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic ownership | Yes | Yes |
| Dividend | Usually variable and not guaranteed | Preferential according to its terms |
| Voting | Commonly present | Depends on terms and law |
| Claim on assets | Residual after prior claims | Preference according to terms |
| Main accounting question | Amount issued and related equity | Amount issued and rights attached |

This table is a learning model, not a substitute for the actual terms of a particular issue.

### Shares and market price

> Par value, issue price and market price answer three different questions.

**Issue price** is the amount the company receives when it issues a share.

**Market price** is the price at which a share may be traded between investors. A company issuing a share at Tk 15 does not mean that the share must later trade at Tk 15.

Consider one ordinary share:

- Par value = Tk 10
- Issue price = Tk 14
- Later market price = Tk 18

The company's original issue transaction is based on Tk 14. The later market price of Tk 18 is not automatically a new company transaction.

> The company records its own issue transaction; later investor-to-investor trading is a different event.

Bangladesh's Companies Act recognises shares as transferable interests subject to the company's articles and the Act. It also requires shares of a share-capital company to be identified by appropriate numbers. citeturn1search2

## 20.3 Issuing shares for cash

> When a company issues shares, cash enters the business and shareholders' equity increases.

The simplest issue occurs when shares are issued for cash at par value.

Suppose Mitali Bakery Limited issues 10,000 ordinary shares of Tk 10 each for Tk 10 per share.

Cash received:

10,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 100,000

Journal entry:

Cash Dr Tk 100,000
    Share Capital Cr Tk 100,000

The debit records the asset received. The credit records the shareholders' equity created by the issue.

> The entry follows the same double-entry logic used elsewhere: an asset comes in and equity increases.

@ Issue at par | Issuing a share for an amount equal to its par value.

### Issue above par: premium

Suppose the same 10,000 shares have a Tk 10 par value but are issued for Tk 14.

Cash received:

10,000 × Tk 14 = Tk 140,000

Par value:

10,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 100,000

Excess:

Tk 140,000 − Tk 100,000 = Tk 40,000

Journal entry:

Cash Dr Tk 140,000
    Share Capital Cr Tk 100,000
    Share Premium Cr Tk 40,000

The company received Tk 140,000, so the debit must equal Tk 140,000. The credit is divided between the amount attributed to share capital and the amount above par.

@ Issue at a premium | Issuing shares for more than their par value.

> A premium is part of equity, but it is not part of the share's par value.

### Issue below par

An issue below par is legally restricted in many systems and must not be treated as a routine accounting choice. In Bangladesh, company-law rules concerning issue of shares, minimum subscription, capital and related matters must be checked before any such transaction is attempted. The accounting treatment cannot make an unlawful issue lawful.

> Accounting records a transaction; it does not authorise the transaction.

For this introductory book, the normal worked examples use issues at par or above par.

### A full issue sequence

A company may collect money through stages rather than receiving the whole amount in one step. The exact terminology and legal procedure depend on the issue arrangement.

The accounting logic remains consistent:

1. identify the amount due;
2. record the company's claim or cash movement;
3. distinguish share capital from any amount above par;
4. record unpaid amounts separately when required by the accounting system.

> Break a complex issue into its economic events instead of trying to memorise one long entry.

### Worked case: Mitali Bakery Limited

Mitali Bakery Limited issues 20,000 ordinary shares with a par value of Tk 10 for Tk 12 each.

Step 1: calculate total cash.

20,000 × Tk 12 = Tk 240,000

Step 2: calculate share capital.

20,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 200,000

Step 3: calculate share premium.

Tk 240,000 − Tk 200,000 = Tk 40,000

Step 4: record the entry.

Cash Dr Tk 240,000
    Share Capital Cr Tk 200,000
    Share Premium Cr Tk 40,000

The transaction increases company assets by Tk 240,000 and equity by Tk 240,000.

> The three-step calculation—total proceeds, par component, excess—is safer than memorising the entry.

## 20.4 Issuing shares for land or other assets

> A company can receive an asset instead of cash, but the accounting still needs a reliable measure of what was received.

Suppose a company issues shares to acquire land. No cash enters the company. The company nevertheless receives an economic resource, so an asset is recognised.

The difficult question is measurement.

**Fair value** means a current measure of an asset or other item based on the price that could be obtained in an appropriate market transaction under the relevant accounting framework.

@ Fair value | A measurement representing the price for an asset or liability in an appropriate orderly transaction under the applicable accounting framework.

For a simple teaching case, suppose Mitali Bakery Limited acquires land by issuing 10,000 ordinary shares with a par value of Tk 10. The agreed fair value of the land is Tk 150,000 and the shares are treated as having the same fair value.

The entry is:

Land Dr Tk 150,000
    Share Capital Cr Tk 100,000
    Share Premium Cr Tk 50,000

The company receives land worth Tk 150,000 in the example. Of this, Tk 100,000 corresponds to par value and Tk 50,000 is above par.

> A non-cash issue still has the same structure: asset received, equity issued.

If the fair value of the asset and the fair value of the shares differ, the applicable accounting rules determine which measurement should be used and whether the transaction requires additional evidence.

This is why a real company cannot choose a convenient number merely to produce a preferred accounting result.

### Why evidence matters

A cash issue provides a visible amount: cash received. A non-cash issue needs evidence for the value assigned to the asset or shares.

For land, evidence may include an independent valuation, market information and transaction terms. The exact evidence depends on the circumstances and reporting framework.

> Measurement is part of accounting judgment, not decoration added after the entry.

## 20.5 Treasury shares: buying back and reselling

> Buying back the company's own shares is an equity transaction, but it is not the same as buying an investment in another company.

**Treasury shares** are a company's own shares that it has acquired and continues to hold, where the applicable accounting and company-law framework permits such treatment.

@ Treasury shares | A company's own issued shares that it has acquired and holds under the applicable legal and accounting framework.

The treatment is important because the company has not acquired an outside asset such as land, inventory or another company's shares. It has changed the composition of its own equity.

### A legal warning before the accounting

> The accounting treatment of treasury shares cannot be separated from company law.

Bangladesh's Companies Act contains restrictions on a company purchasing its own shares. Section 58 provides that a company limited by shares cannot freely purchase its own shares except through the procedures specified by the law; the provision also contains specific exceptions and rules for private companies and other situations. citeturn1search2

Therefore, the following examples are **accounting exercises only**. They assume that the transaction is legally permitted and properly authorised.

### Purchase of treasury shares

Suppose the company purchases 2,000 of its own shares for Tk 15 each.

Cost:

2,000 × Tk 15 = Tk 30,000

Under a treasury-share accounting model, the purchase is recorded as a deduction from equity:

Treasury Shares Dr Tk 30,000
    Cash Cr Tk 30,000

The important idea is not the account title alone. The company has paid cash to acquire its own shares, so its net equity is reduced under this model.

> Treasury shares are presented as a reduction of equity rather than as an ordinary operating asset.

### Reissue above cost

Suppose the company later reissues those 2,000 treasury shares for Tk 18 each.

Cash received:

2,000 × Tk 18 = Tk 36,000

Cost of treasury shares:

Tk 30,000

Difference:

Tk 6,000

A simplified entry under the teaching model is:

Cash Dr Tk 36,000
    Treasury Shares Cr Tk 30,000
    Additional Paid-in Capital—Treasury Shares Cr Tk 6,000

The difference is not ordinary sales revenue. The company is dealing in its own equity.

> A company does not earn sales revenue by selling its own shares.

### Reissue below cost

Suppose the same shares are reissued for Tk 13 each.

Cash received:

2,000 × Tk 13 = Tk 26,000

Treasury-share cost:

Tk 30,000

Difference:

Tk 4,000

The difference is treated within equity according to the applicable accounting framework, rather than as an ordinary operating loss.

> Treasury-share gains and losses are equity matters, not operating profit.

The exact presentation can depend on the reporting framework and the balance of related equity accounts. The beginner should therefore remember the principle first: treasury-share transactions affect equity.

### Common confusion

A company buying another company's shares may recognise an investment asset. A company buying its own shares is different.

The company cannot become an owner of itself in the same economic sense that an investor becomes an owner of another company. The transaction is therefore presented as a change in the company's own equity.

> Ask “whose shares?” before deciding how to classify a share purchase.

## 20.6 Reading a company's equity section

> The equity section is a compact history of how owners' claims were created, retained and changed.

A simplified equity section may contain:

- Share capital
- Share premium or another additional paid-in capital component
- Retained earnings
- Treasury shares, where applicable
- Other equity components required by the reporting framework

Suppose Mitali Bakery Limited has the following balances:

| Equity component | Tk |
| --- | ---: |
| Share capital | 200,000 |
| Share premium | 40,000 |
| Retained earnings | 75,000 |
| Less: treasury shares | (30,000) |
| Total equity | 285,000 |

The total is:

Tk 200,000 + Tk 40,000 + Tk 75,000 − Tk 30,000 = Tk 285,000

The negative treasury-share amount reduces total equity.

> Read each line as part of a story about capital, accumulated profit and equity transactions.

### Share capital

Share capital represents the amount attributed to issued shares according to the accounting and legal framework.

It is not necessarily equal to the total cash ever received from shareholders. If shares are issued above par, the excess is recorded separately as share premium or another appropriate additional paid-in capital component.

### Share premium

Share premium records amounts received above par value in the relevant issue.

For example:

20,000 shares × Tk 10 par = Tk 200,000 share capital.

If issued for Tk 12:

20,000 × Tk 2 = Tk 40,000 share premium.

The company therefore receives Tk 240,000, while the equity section separates the two components.

> Separate presentation helps readers understand how much relates to par value and how much was received above it.

### Retained earnings

Retained earnings accumulate profits that have not been distributed, after the relevant adjustments and appropriations.

Suppose opening retained earnings are Tk 50,000 and the company earns Tk 40,000. If it declares and pays Tk 15,000 in dividends, the simplified closing balance is:

Tk 50,000 + Tk 40,000 − Tk 15,000 = Tk 75,000

This example ignores other possible adjustments so that the basic movement remains visible.

> Retained earnings connect past profitability with the equity reported today.

### Dividends are not an expense

A dividend is a distribution to owners. It is not a cost of producing the company's goods or services.

This distinction matters. Wages help produce revenue and are expenses. Rent is an expense. A dividend transfers part of the owners' claim after profit has been determined.

> Expenses measure the cost of earning revenue; dividends distribute equity to owners.

### A complete equity reading

Suppose a company reports:

Share capital = Tk 500,000
Share premium = Tk 100,000
Retained earnings = Tk 220,000
Treasury shares = Tk 60,000 deduction

Total equity:

Tk 500,000 + Tk 100,000 + Tk 220,000 − Tk 60,000
= Tk 760,000

A reader can ask four questions:

1. How much ownership capital has been issued?
2. How much was received above par?
3. How much profit has been retained?
4. Has the company reduced equity through treasury-share transactions?

> Good accounting reading turns a list of balances into an explanation of how equity changed.

## Worked case: Mitali Bakery becomes a company

> The final case combines the chapter's ideas in the order a new company might encounter them.

Mitali Bakery has grown beyond its original partnership form. For teaching purposes, assume it is reorganised as Mitali Bakery Limited.

The company issues 30,000 ordinary shares with a par value of Tk 10 for Tk 12 each.

Step 1: total cash received.

30,000 × Tk 12 = Tk 360,000

Step 2: share capital.

30,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 300,000

Step 3: share premium.

Tk 360,000 − Tk 300,000 = Tk 60,000

Entry:

Cash Dr Tk 360,000
    Share Capital Cr Tk 300,000
    Share Premium Cr Tk 60,000

The company then acquires land by issuing 10,000 ordinary shares. The fair value of the land is Tk 180,000.

Entry:

Land Dr Tk 180,000
    Share Capital Cr Tk 100,000
    Share Premium Cr Tk 80,000

The company later purchases 2,000 of its own shares for Tk 15 each, assuming the transaction is legally permitted.

Treasury-share cost:

2,000 × Tk 15 = Tk 30,000

Entry:

Treasury Shares Dr Tk 30,000
    Cash Cr Tk 30,000

Assume the company then earns Tk 90,000 and distributes Tk 20,000 as dividends. For this simplified case, retained earnings increase by:

Tk 90,000 − Tk 20,000 = Tk 70,000

If there was no opening retained earnings balance, the simplified equity section would be:

| Equity component | Tk |
| --- | ---: |
| Share capital | 400,000 |
| Share premium | 140,000 |
| Retained earnings | 70,000 |
| Less: treasury shares | (30,000) |
| Total equity | 580,000 |

The calculation is:

Tk 400,000 + Tk 140,000 + Tk 70,000 − Tk 30,000 = Tk 580,000

The case shows the central movement of the chapter. A company raises capital by issuing shares, can receive assets instead of cash, can alter its equity through permitted treasury-share transactions, and retains part of its profit in the business.

> The equity section is not a collection of unrelated numbers; it is the record of the owners' financial relationship with the company.

## Summary

> A company changes the accounting picture by separating the incorporated business from its shareholders.

A company is an incorporated business organisation. A separate legal entity is distinct from its owners. Limited liability can limit shareholders' exposure according to the legal form.

Shares divide company ownership into units. Shareholders own shares. Par value is the stated value of a share, while issue price is what the company receives when it issues the share.

Authorised shares represent permitted capacity where that concept applies. Issued shares are actually issued.

Ordinary shares usually provide basic ownership rights. Preference shares carry specified preferential rights. The exact rights depend on the share terms and law.

Shares issued for cash increase assets and equity. When the issue price exceeds par value, the excess is recorded as share premium or another appropriate additional paid-in capital component.

A company may issue shares for non-cash assets. The measurement must follow the applicable accounting framework and reliable evidence.

Treasury shares are the company's own shares that it has acquired where legally and accounting-wise permitted. Their treatment is within equity, not ordinary operating revenue or expense.

The equity section can include share capital, share premium, retained earnings and treasury shares. Reading these balances explains how owners' claims have been created and changed.

## Where people go wrong

> Most errors arise when similar-looking ownership terms are treated as though they mean the same thing.

**Mistake 1: Treating par value as market value.** Par value is a stated capital amount. Market price can change.

**Mistake 2: Crediting the entire issue price to share capital.** When shares are issued above par, the excess is normally separated into share premium or another appropriate equity component.

**Mistake 3: Treating dividends as an expense.** Dividends are distributions to owners, not operating expenses.

**Mistake 4: Treating treasury shares as an ordinary investment asset.** Treasury-share transactions concern the company's own equity.

**Mistake 5: Assuming every company can freely buy back its shares.** The legal permission and procedure must be checked.

**Mistake 6: Assuming a private company is simply a small company.** Legal definitions concern specific restrictions and conditions.

**Mistake 7: Treating authorised shares as issued shares.** A permitted maximum is not the same as an actual issue.

## In history

> The early history of share ownership shows why divided ownership became such an important business idea.

The Dutch East India Company, commonly known as the VOC, was formed in 1602. Its development is widely associated with early large-scale joint-stock ownership and transferable shares. The historical importance of the VOC lies in the way capital could be assembled from many investors rather than depending only on one merchant or a small partnership.

The historical lesson is useful, but it should not be turned into a claim that one company single-handedly invented every modern feature of corporations. Corporate and partnership practices developed over a much longer period.

> The enduring idea is that ownership can be divided into financial interests that allow capital to be gathered from multiple investors.

The modern company is much more regulated than early joint-stock ventures. Today's accounting therefore combines an old economic idea—divided ownership—with formal legal and reporting systems.

## Final chapter reflection

> The central lesson of this chapter is that ownership is an accounting structure as well as a legal relationship.

A partnership records the claims of partners. A company divides ownership into shares and reports shareholders' equity through several components. Once this distinction is understood, share issues, premiums, retained earnings and treasury shares become connected parts of one system.

@ Final idea | Company accounting becomes easier when every equity balance is read as part of the ownership story.
