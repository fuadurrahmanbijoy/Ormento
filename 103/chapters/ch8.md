:chapter VIII | The Trial Balance
:mood How an accountant asks the books whether they agree with themselves
:desc A ledger holds many accounts, and every amount in it was copied by hand. This chapter teaches a short list of balances that tests whether debits still equal credits. It shows how to prepare the list, how to search for a difference, and which mistakes the test can never reveal. You will need Chapter 7: the ledger and its balances.

> After one month of work, Mitali has copied 27 amounts by hand and cannot see her own slips.

At the end of January 2026, Mitali Rahman has recorded the first month of her bakery. She has written 13 transactions in the journal. She has then copied 27 amounts from the journal into 12 ledger accounts, one amount for each line of each entry. Every copy is a chance for a small slip: a digit written in the wrong order, an amount placed on the wrong side, or a posting forgotten. No one checks her work. Before she builds a single statement, she needs to know whether her ledger can be trusted. How can she learn this without repeating all her work?

> A shopper's second addition of a bill is a small model of the accountant's test.

Think of a shopper who buys five items and receives a bill. The shopper adds the five prices again. If the new total matches the printed total, the bill is probably right. If it does not match, something is wrong, and the shopper knows to examine the items. The accountant has a similar test. It rests on one rule from Chapter 6: in every transaction, the debits equal the credits. This chapter turns that rule into a test of the whole ledger.

## 8.1 Testing the books: what a trial balance is and is not

> The trial balance lists every ledger balance in two columns and compares the totals.

@ Trial balance | A list of all ledger balances, used to test that debits equal credits.

A **trial balance** is a list of all the balances in the ledger, written in two columns: one for debit balances and one for credit balances. The accountant totals each column. If the two totals are equal, the ledger passes the test. The list is called a trial because it tries the books. It asks whether debits and credits still agree after all the postings.

> The test works because every transaction was recorded with equal debits and credits.

Why should the totals be equal? Chapter 3 gave the dual aspect idea: every transaction has two sides. Chapter 6 turned that idea into a rule: each entry has debits equal to credits. If every entry is balanced, then the sum of all debits posted to the ledger equals the sum of all credits posted. A balance is only the difference between the debits and credits of one account. So the debit balances, taken together, must equal the credit balances, taken together. When they do not, at least one rule was broken somewhere between the journal and the ledger.

@ Error | A mistake made when recording a transaction.

> An error is any recording mistake; some break the equality of the totals and some do not.
The word **error** names any such mistake. Some errors break the equality of debits and credits, and the trial balance shows them as a difference between the two totals. Other errors keep the equality and stay hidden. This second group is the reason why the chapter has a fourth section.

> A trial balance prepared before any adjusting entries is called unadjusted.

@ Unadjusted trial balance | A trial balance prepared before any adjusting entries are made.

The trial balance in this chapter is prepared at the end of the period, before the accounts are brought up to date. For that reason it is called an **unadjusted trial balance**. Chapter 9 explains the adjusting entries and then prepares a second list, the adjusted trial balance. The test is the same in both. Only the balances differ.

> An agreed trial balance proves a narrow fact; it does not prove that the books are right.

:box IN SHORT
A trial balance proves one fact: the total of the debit balances equals the total of the credit balances. It does not prove that every amount is correct, that every transaction was recorded, or that each amount lies in the right account.

:fig The trial balance has a fixed place in the cycle: after the ledger and before the adjusting entries. | ch8_fig1_cycle.svg

> The test is a working tool for the accountant, not a report for outsiders.

Who reads a trial balance? Chiefly the accountant who prepared it. A bank officer who wishes to lend money wants the financial statements of Chapter 5, not a list of balances. The owner may look at the list to see the size of each account, but it does not show profit or financial position in a form that a user can read quickly. It is a working paper in the strict sense: its purpose is to protect the quality of the statements that follow.

> Recap: what the list is and what agreement does and does not show.
*Before moving on.* A trial balance lists the balances of all ledger accounts and compares the debit total with the credit total. Equal totals are good evidence of correct arithmetic and balanced postings. They are not proof that the books are free from error.

## 8.2 Preparing a trial balance

> Preparation has four short steps: find the balances, list them, total the columns, compare.

Preparing a trial balance needs four steps. First, find the balance of each ledger account by the method of Chapter 7: total the debit side, total the credit side, and take the difference. Second, write the name of each account on its own line, in the order of the chart of accounts: assets, liabilities, owner's equity, revenue, then expenses. Third, write each balance in the debit column if it is a debit balance and in the credit column if it is a credit balance. Fourth, total the two columns and compare them.

> The heading names the business, the list, and the date; the amounts are in taka.

A trial balance has a three-line heading: the name of the business, the words *Trial Balance*, and the date on which the balances are taken, written as "At 31 January 2026". The money unit, *Tk*, is shown at the head of the columns. The date matters. A balance has meaning only on a stated day, because the next transaction changes it.

> The first worked example uses six accounts of a tea stall and round numbers.

We begin with a small example so that the method is the only new thing. A tea stall (illustrative) has six ledger accounts at 31 March 2026.

> The worked example is laid out as given, find, formula, steps, answer, check.
*Given.* The balances are: Cash Tk 36,000 debit; Equipment Tk 20,000 debit; Rent Expense Tk 8,000 debit; Accounts Payable Tk 6,000 credit; Owner's Capital Tk 40,000 credit; Sales Revenue Tk 18,000 credit.
*Find.* Whether the ledger passes the trial balance test.
*Formula.* In words: total of debit balances = total of credit balances.
*Steps.* List the accounts in chart order, place each balance in its column, and total each column. The debit total is 36,000 + 20,000 + 8,000 = Tk 64,000. The credit total is 6,000 + 40,000 + 18,000 = Tk 64,000.
*Answer.* Both totals are Tk 64,000, so the ledger passes the test.
*Check.* The assets, Tk 36,000 + Tk 20,000 = Tk 56,000, should equal the liabilities Tk 6,000, plus the capital Tk 40,000, plus the profit to date. The profit is Tk 18,000 − Tk 8,000 = Tk 10,000. The sum is 6,000 + 40,000 + 10,000 = Tk 56,000, which agrees.

> Three slips are common when listing balances: wrong column, missing account, wrong copy.

Three slips are common in this work. The first is to place a balance in the wrong column. The second is to leave an account out of the list, especially an account with a small balance. The third is to copy a figure wrongly from the ledger to the list. An account with a zero balance is not listed. An account that has a balance on its unusual side, such as an asset with a credit balance, is listed on the side where the balance lies, and its cause is investigated.

*Before moving on.* Find each balance, list the accounts in chart order, place each balance in its own column, and total. The bakery's list balances at Tk 792,000.

## 8.3 When the totals differ: finding the error

> When the totals differ, the size of the difference gives the first clue.

Sometimes the two totals are not equal. We use the tea stall again, three months later, at 30 June 2026. The accountant prepares the list and finds that the credit total is larger than the debit total.

:table Trial balance of the tea stall as first prepared, at 30 June 2026 (Tk)
| Account | Debit | Credit |
| Cash | 48,000 | |
| Equipment | 25,000 | |
| Supplies | 5,000 | |
| Accounts Payable | | 7,000 |
| Owner's Capital | | 50,000 |
| Sales Revenue | | 63,000 |
| Wages Expense | 9,000 | |
| Rent Expense | 6,000 | |
| Total | 93,000 | 120,000 |

> The accountant first checks the arithmetic, then reads the difference as a clue.

The difference is Tk 120,000 − Tk 93,000 = Tk 27,000. The accountant does not change any figure to make the totals agree. A figure may be changed only when the cause is known. The search follows a fixed order, from the cheapest check to the most costly. First, add both columns again. Second, check the arithmetic of each ledger balance. Third, read the size of the difference, as the next paragraphs show. Fourth, compare the journal with the ledger, entry by entry, and mark each posting that has been checked.

@ Transposition error | Two digits written in the wrong order, such as 54 for 45.

> Three clues: divisible by 9, equal to one amount, half equal to one balance.
The size of the difference gives three clues. A difference that can be divided by 9 with no remainder suggests a **transposition error**: two digits written in the wrong order. The reason is simple. The numbers 63 and 36 differ by 27, which is 9 times 3; any pair of swapped digits gives a difference of 9 times the gap between the digits. A difference equal to one balance, or to one amount in the journal, suggests that an amount was left out or posted to one side only. A difference whose half equals a balance suggests that the balance was placed in the wrong column, because moving an amount from one column to the other changes the difference by twice that amount.

:fig The search for a difference follows the size of the difference. | ch8_fig2_search.svg

> The first worked search finds a swapped pair of digits in a listed balance.

*Given.* The tea stall's list above: debit total Tk 93,000, credit total Tk 120,000.
*Find.* The cause of the difference.
*Formula.* Difference = larger total − smaller total. A difference divisible by 9 suggests a transposition.
*Steps.* The difference is 120,000 − 93,000 = 27,000. Then 27,000 ÷ 9 = 3,000, with no remainder. We search for a balance with the digits 3 and 6 in swapped places. The Sales Revenue is listed as 63,000. The ledger account shows a balance of Tk 36,000, so the digits were swapped when the balance was copied to the list.
*Answer.* The Sales Revenue balance should be Tk 36,000.
*Check.* The credit total becomes 7,000 + 50,000 + 36,000 = Tk 93,000, equal to the debit total. The error was in the list only, so the ledger needs no correcting entry.

> The second search starts from a difference that no balance explains.

In a second case, the same stall lists Sales Revenue correctly, but the debit column totals Tk 89,000 and the credit column Tk 93,000. The difference is Tk 4,000. It cannot be divided by 9 with no remainder, and no balance equals 4,000 or 2,000. The accountant therefore compares the journal with the ledger. The journal shows a wage payment of Tk 4,000 on 18 June, with the entry debit Wages Expense, credit Cash. The credit to Cash was posted. The debit to Wages Expense was not, so Wages Expense stands at Tk 5,000 instead of Tk 9,000. This is a one-sided posting. The accountant posts the missing debit of Tk 4,000 to the Wages Expense account, and the totals agree at Tk 93,000.

> One search may not remove the whole difference, because several errors can exist together.

A caution is needed. The routine finds one error at a time. If two errors exist, for example the swapped digits of 27,000 and the missing posting of 4,000, the difference is Tk 31,000, and no single clue explains it. Correct the errors that you can prove, calculate the new difference, and search again. Never insert a balancing figure without a reason. Section 8.4 shows the one safe way to hold a difference that cannot be found.

> Recap: the order of the search for a difference.
*Before moving on.* When the totals differ, add again, calculate the difference, and read it: divisible by 9 suggests swapped digits; equal to one amount suggests a missing posting; half equal to one balance suggests a wrong column. Then compare the journal with the ledger.

## 8.4 Errors a trial balance cannot reveal, and how to correct them

> A test of equality finds only mistakes that break the equality.

@! Go slowly here. One idea at a time; each of the six kinds has its own small example.

Imagine a shopkeeper who writes Tk 540 as Tk 450 on both copies of a bill. The two copies agree with each other, and both are wrong. A test that compares the copies will find nothing. The trial balance has the same weakness. It compares the debit total with the credit total. A mistake that adds or subtracts the same amount on both sides leaves the two totals equal, and the test shows nothing. A bakery whose list agrees may still hold several such mistakes. We study six kinds, one at a time. In each, the example is an imagined slip in the bakery's books, not a change to the case file.

@ Error of omission | A transaction left out of the books completely.

> An omission leaves out both sides of a transaction, so the totals stay equal.
The first kind is the **error of omission**. A whole transaction is missing from the books: both the debit and the credit were left out. Suppose that Mitali buys supplies of Tk 4,000 on account and the purchase is never written in the journal. The ledger has no trace of it, so the two sides remain equal. The result is that supplies and accounts payable are both too small by Tk 4,000.

@ Error of commission | A transaction recorded in the wrong account of the correct type.

> A commission puts the right amount on the right side of the wrong account of the same type.
The second kind is the **error of commission**. The amount is recorded on the right side, but in the wrong account of the correct type. Suppose that the electricity bill of Tk 6,000 is debited to Wages Expense instead of Utilities Expense. Both are expense accounts, so the debit is still a debit of Tk 6,000. The totals agree. The statements, however, show wages too high and utilities too low.

> Recap of the first two kinds.
*Recap.* An omission leaves out both sides; a commission puts the right amount on the right side of the wrong account of the same type. Neither upsets the totals.

@ Error of principle | A transaction recorded in an account of the wrong type.

> A principle error uses an account of the wrong type; here, private spending charged as an expense.
The third kind is the **error of principle**. The amount is recorded in an account of the wrong type, so a basic rule is broken. Suppose that Mitali pays Tk 5,000 for her own household electricity from the bakery's cash, and the bookkeeper debits Utilities Expense. The bill is a private matter of the owner and should be debited to Owner's Drawings (Chapter 3, business entity concept). An expense account was used where an equity account was needed. The debit and the credit are still equal. The bakery's profit is Tk 5,000 too low.

@ Error of original entry | A wrong amount written in both the debit and the credit.

> An original-entry error writes the same wrong amount on both sides.
The fourth kind is the **error of original entry**. The wrong amount is written in the journal, and the same wrong amount is posted to both accounts. Suppose that a cash sale of Tk 45,000 is written as Tk 54,000. Cash is debited Tk 54,000 and Sales Revenue is credited Tk 54,000. The sides are equal, but both accounts are Tk 9,000 too high. This slip is a transposition of digits, as in Section 8.3. The difference is that the digits were swapped before posting, so both sides carry the same wrong figure and nothing shows in the totals.

> Recap of the third and fourth kinds.
*Recap.* A wrong principle or a wrong original amount each keeps debits equal to credits, so the totals cannot warn.

@ Compensating errors | Two or more mistakes that cancel each other out, so the trial balance still agrees.

> Compensating errors cancel each other, so the totals agree by chance.
The fifth kind is **compensating errors**. Two or more mistakes cancel each other out. Suppose that the Sales Revenue account is added wrongly and shows Tk 210,000 instead of Tk 200,000. A second slip adds Tk 10,000 too much to the Wages Expense balance, which becomes Tk 46,000. The credit side is too high by Tk 10,000 and the debit side is too high by Tk 10,000. The totals are both Tk 802,000 and they agree. Compensating errors are the result of chance, but a careful reader should know that chance does occur.

@ Error of reversal | A debit and a credit written on the wrong sides, so that the opposite of the transaction is recorded.

> A reversal records the opposite of what happened, with equal totals.
The sixth kind is the **error of reversal**. The correct accounts are used and the correct amount is written, but the debit and the credit are on the wrong sides. Suppose that the Tk 25,000 received from the school on 18 January were recorded as a debit to Accounts Receivable and a credit to Cash. The totals agree. The entry, however, records the opposite of what happened: Cash is Tk 50,000 too low and Accounts Receivable is Tk 50,000 too high.

:fig The trial balance reveals errors that break the equality and misses those that keep it. | ch8_fig3_caught.svg

> Hidden errors are found by other checks, not by the trial balance.

How are these errors found? Not by the trial balance. They are found by other checks: comparing the ledger with source documents, checking customer statements, counting cash and supplies, comparing the cash book with the bank statement (Chapter 16), and the questions of an auditor. The trial balance is one safeguard among several, and it is a useful one because it is quick and cheap.

> A mistake is never erased; a new entry corrects it and leaves the record readable.

@ Correcting entry | A journal entry that puts right a mistake that was recorded earlier.

Once a mistake is found, the books must be corrected. The rule is that nothing is erased or covered. A **correcting entry** is written in the journal on the date of discovery, with a narration that states what is being corrected. The old entry stays visible; the new entry changes the balances to the correct figures. In this way anyone who reads the books later can follow the history.

> Each kind of error has its own correcting entry; the method is to compare what was done with what should have been done.

To write the entry, compare two things: what was recorded, and what should have been recorded. Then record the difference. The entries for the six imagined slips are shown below. Each is explained in a line, and the check is that after the entry the accounts show the correct balances.

:table Correcting entries for the six kinds of error (Tk)
| Error | Correcting entry | Reason |
| Omission: supplies Tk 4,000 bought on account, not recorded | Dr Supplies 4,000; Cr Accounts Payable 4,000 | Record the transaction that was left out. |
| Commission: electricity Tk 6,000 debited to Wages Expense | Dr Utilities Expense 6,000; Cr Wages Expense 6,000 | Move the amount to the right expense account. |
| Principle: owner's household bill Tk 5,000 debited to Utilities Expense | Dr Owner's Drawings 5,000; Cr Utilities Expense 5,000 | Move the amount to the right type of account. |
| Original entry: sale Tk 45,000 recorded as Tk 54,000 | Dr Sales Revenue 9,000; Cr Cash 9,000 | Remove the excess from both accounts. |
| Compensating: Sales Revenue Tk 10,000 too high; Wages Expense Tk 10,000 too high | Dr Sales Revenue 10,000; Cr Wages Expense 10,000 | Reduce each balance by its excess. |
| Reversal: receipt Tk 25,000 entered on wrong sides | Dr Cash 50,000; Cr Accounts Receivable 50,000 | Cancel the wrong entry (25,000) and record the right one (25,000). |

> The reversal needs twice the amount because the wrong entry must be cancelled and the right one recorded.

The last entry deserves a second look. The wrong entry debited Accounts Receivable and credited Cash by Tk 25,000. To cancel it, we credit Accounts Receivable and debit Cash by Tk 25,000. To record the true transaction, we again debit Cash and credit Accounts Receivable by Tk 25,000. The two steps together are one entry of Tk 50,000. After it, Cash has risen by Tk 50,000 and Accounts Receivable has fallen by Tk 50,000, which puts both balances where they belong.

@ Suspense account | A temporary account that holds a difference until its cause is found.

> A suspense account holds an unexplained difference for a short time.
Sometimes the search fails, and the statements cannot wait. The accountant may then open a **suspense account**. This is a temporary account that holds the difference so that the trial balance agrees while the search continues. Take the tea stall of Section 8.3, with the debit column short by Tk 4,000. The accountant lists a suspense account with a debit balance of Tk 4,000 and the totals agree at Tk 93,000. Later, the missing posting is found. The entry is: debit Wages Expense Tk 4,000 and credit Suspense Tk 4,000. The suspense account then has no balance.

> A suspense account is a waiting room, not a home; its balance must be cleared.

A suspense account is a convenience and also a risk. It does not mean that the books are correct; it means that the books contain an unexplained difference. A balance should stay in it only as long as the search lasts. A balance that remains for months invites the suspicion that someone is using the account to hide something. For this reason, accountants and auditors give a suspense balance particular attention.

> The section ends with one sentence that the reader should be able to say.

*You should now be able to say:* "An agreed trial balance shows that debits equal credits; it does not show that the books are free from error, because six kinds of error leave the totals equal."

## 8.5 Trial balance and balance sheet compared

> The two lists look alike but differ in purpose, content, and standing.

@! Do not mix them: one is a test, the other a statement.

Beginners often confuse the trial balance with the balance sheet. Both list accounts with amounts, and both have two sides that agree in total. They differ in four ways, shown in the table below.

:table Trial balance and balance sheet compared
| Feature | Trial balance | Balance sheet |
| Purpose | Tests that debits equal credits | Shows financial position to users |
| Accounts listed | All ledger accounts with balances, including revenue, expenses, and drawings | Assets, liabilities, and owner's equity only |
| Standing | Working paper | Financial statement |
| Reader | The accountant | Owner, bank, tax authority, and others |

> The bakery's two figures differ because the balance sheet leaves out revenue, expenses, and drawings.

The bakery shows the difference. The trial balance totals Tk 792,000 on each side. The balance sheet of Chapter 5 shows total assets of Tk 730,000 and liabilities of Tk 92,000 plus owner's equity of Tk 638,000. The gap of Tk 62,000 on the debit side is the expenses of Tk 42,000 and the drawings of Tk 20,000, which the trial balance lists separately. On the credit side, the revenue of Tk 200,000 is likewise listed alone and is part of equity in the balance sheet. After Chapter 9 corrects the figures, the equality of the balance sheet will again be tested.

> Recap: test versus statement.
*Before moving on.* The trial balance tests the ledger and lists every account. The balance sheet reports position and lists only assets, liabilities, and equity.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in one paragraph.

A trial balance is a list of ledger balances in a debit column and a credit column. Its totals agree when every entry had equal debits and credits and the posting was complete. A difference is searched by checking arithmetic, reading the size of the difference, and comparing the journal with the ledger. Six kinds of error leave the totals equal: omission, commission, principle, original entry, compensating errors, and reversal. A correcting entry puts them right without erasing the record. A suspense account holds a difference temporarily. The trial balance is a working paper, not a financial statement.

## Where people go wrong

> Four common misunderstandings, each stated and corrected.

> Agreement does not prove the books correct.
*"The trial balance agrees, so the books are correct."* The agreement shows only that debits equal credits. Six kinds of error leave the totals equal, so the books may still contain mistakes.

> A figure is changed only when its cause is proved.
*"I can force the totals to agree by adjusting a figure."* A figure may be changed only when the cause is known and proved from the records. An unexplained balancing figure hides the problem and misleads every reader of the statements.

> Two errors can combine into a difference that matches no single amount.
*"A difference of Tk 4,000 means one posting of Tk 4,000 is wrong."* The difference is the total of all the errors that break equality. Two errors can combine to give a figure that matches no single amount, so correct what is proved and search again.

> The trial balance is a working paper, not a statement.
*"The trial balance is a financial statement."* It is a working paper. It lists revenue, expenses, and drawings, and it is not prepared for outside users.
