:chapter XVII | Accounting for Organisations That Do Not Seek Profit
:mood An organisation can receive money without earning profit, and spend money without making a loss.
:desc Clubs, associations and charities need accounting records even when profit is not their purpose. This chapter separates cash movements from income and expenditure, then shows how subscriptions, special receipts and accumulated funds are treated.

> The purpose of an organisation determines what its accounting statements need to explain.

A neighbourhood bakers' association collects subscriptions from members, receives donations, pays for meetings and training, and keeps equipment for its activities. It does not exist to distribute profit to an owner. Yet its members still need to know how much cash was received, how much was paid, what income belonged to the period, what expenses belonged to the period, and what resources remain at the end.

> Not seeking profit does not mean keeping no accounts.

@ Non-trading organisation | An organisation formed to serve members or a social, cultural, educational, charitable or similar purpose rather than to distribute profit to owners.

A **non-trading organisation** may be a club, association, society or charity. Some receive membership subscriptions. Others depend heavily on donations or grants. Many have assets, liabilities and expenses just like businesses. The central difference is the purpose and the way the resulting surplus is used.

## 17.1 Organisations that serve members and causes

> A surplus in a club is not the same idea as profit distributed to an owner.

A business measures performance partly by the profit available to its owner or shareholders. A club may instead aim to provide sports, education, cultural activities or services to members. If its income exceeds its expenses, it may have a surplus. The surplus normally strengthens the organisation's resources and supports future activities rather than becoming a personal return to a proprietor.

@ **Surplus** | The excess of income over expenditure for a non-trading organisation during an accounting period.

@ **Deficit** | The excess of expenditure over income for a non-trading organisation during an accounting period.

The words surplus and deficit describe the period result without implying ownership by an individual. A surplus increases the organisation's accumulated fund, subject to the treatment of special items. A deficit reduces it.

> The accounting equation still works even when there is no owner.

An association can have cash, equipment, receivables and other assets. It can also owe suppliers or have subscriptions received in advance. The difference between its assets and liabilities represents the accumulated fund and related reserves. The equation remains: assets = liabilities + the organisation's residual fund.

> The first practical question is what kind of record is being prepared.

A receipts and payments account answers a cash question. An income and expenditure account answers an accrual question. A balance sheet, or statement of financial position, shows what the organisation owns and owes at the reporting date. Confusing these purposes is the source of many errors.

## 17.2 The receipts and payments account

> A receipts and payments account summarises actual cash and bank movements during the period.

@ **Receipts and payments account** | A summary of cash and bank receipts and payments during a period, regardless of whether they belong to that period's income or expenditure.

The receipts and payments account resembles a classified summary of the cash book. It begins with opening cash and bank balances, lists receipts, lists payments, and ends with closing cash and bank balances. It normally includes capital as well as revenue cash movements. It can therefore contain the purchase of equipment even though equipment is not an expense of the period.

> Cash timing matters in this account, not accrual timing.

Suppose an association receives Tk 90,000 from members during the year. The receipts and payments account records Tk 90,000 as a receipt. It does not ask whether some subscriptions relate to the previous year or the next year. That question belongs to the income and expenditure account.

> A capital receipt can appear in receipts and payments without becoming period income.

A donation specifically given to buy equipment may be a capital receipt. The cash account records the receipt because cash was received. The income and expenditure account may exclude it from ordinary income because its nature is capital. The balance sheet then reflects the related resource and the appropriate fund treatment.

> The receipts and payments account tells us where cash moved, but not the period's true surplus.

The example above deliberately includes equipment and a starting balance. A reader who treats every receipt as income and every payment as expense would misstate the organisation's performance. The next account applies accrual reasoning.

## 17.3 The income and expenditure account

> The income and expenditure account measures the period's income and expenses on an accrual basis.

@ **Income and expenditure account** | A period statement that recognises income earned and expenses incurred for the period, whether or not cash has been received or paid.

The logic is familiar from Chapter 9. Income belongs to the period when it is earned or becomes due under the accounting basis used. Expense belongs to the period when the resource is consumed or the obligation is incurred. Cash timing may differ. The statement therefore needs adjustments for arrears and advances.

> Subscriptions are a common example because members can owe money or pay ahead.

@ **Subscription** | A recurring amount paid by a member to support an organisation's activities.

Suppose subscriptions received during the year are Tk 90,000. At the beginning, members owed Tk 4,000 and Tk 5,000 had been received in advance. At the end, members owe Tk 7,000 and Tk 8,000 has been received in advance. The subscription income for the period is:

Tk 90,000 + Tk 7,000 − Tk 4,000 − Tk 5,000 + Tk 8,000 = Tk 96,000.

> The adjustment removes cash that belongs elsewhere and includes income belonging to the current period.

The opening arrears are added to current receipts because they relate to the current period's income. Closing arrears are also added because members still owe amounts earned in the period. Opening advance is removed because it belongs to an earlier or future period. Closing advance is included in the liability or deducted from current receipts according to the chosen presentation. The same principle applies to many accrual adjustments.

> Entrance fees need a policy based on their nature and the organisation's rules.

@ **Entrance fee** | A payment made when a person joins an organisation.

An entrance fee may be treated as revenue income when it is recurring and relates to ordinary membership services. In some organisations it is treated as a capital receipt because it is non-recurring and strengthens the organisation's permanent fund. The accounting treatment should follow the organisation's established policy and the nature of the receipt rather than an automatic rule.

> Donations also require attention to purpose.

A general donation may be income available for ordinary activities. A donation restricted for a building or equipment may be treated as capital or as a restricted fund, depending on the organisation's accounting framework. The important lesson is to identify the condition attached to the receipt before classifying it.

## 17.4 Subscriptions, entrance fees and special items

> Accrual adjustments turn a cash summary into a period measure.

Consider the neighbourhood bakers' association. During 2026 it received Tk 90,000 in subscriptions. Members owed Tk 4,000 at the beginning and Tk 7,000 at the end. Subscription advances were Tk 5,000 at the beginning and Tk 8,000 at the end. The income recognised is Tk 96,000. The cash figure and the income figure differ because the periods differ.

> Expenses require the same treatment.

Suppose salaries paid are Tk 48,000, with Tk 3,000 payable at the beginning and Tk 5,000 payable at the end. Salary expense is Tk 50,000. The opening payable is removed from the current period's obligation, while the closing payable is added because it belongs to the current period even though it remains unpaid.

> Prepaid expenses reverse the direction of the adjustment.

If rent paid includes an amount for the next period, that future portion is not current expenditure. It is recorded as a prepaid expense, which is an asset until the related period arrives. This is the same accrual idea used earlier in the book.

> A non-trading organisation can therefore use familiar accounting logic without having a profit-seeking owner.

The language changes from profit to surplus, and from owner's equity to accumulated fund, but the underlying discipline remains. Cash movements must be separated from period income and expenditure, and assets and liabilities must still be reported.

## 17.5 The balance sheet and the accumulated fund

> The accumulated fund represents the organisation's net resources built up over time.

@ **Accumulated fund** | The residual interest in a non-trading organisation after liabilities are deducted from its assets, subject to the organisation's fund structure.

For a simple organisation with one unrestricted fund, the accumulated fund can be thought of as opening accumulated fund plus surplus, plus appropriate capital receipts, less any capital deductions. It is not a bank balance. An organisation can have a large accumulated fund while holding little cash, because the fund may be represented by equipment, buildings, receivables and other assets.

> The balance sheet connects the period result to the organisation's resources.

For the illustrative association, the opening accumulated fund is Tk 65,000. During the year, the organisation has a surplus of Tk 14,000. It also receives Tk 15,000 as a donation for capital purposes and treats Tk 4,000 of an entrance fee as capital. The closing accumulated fund becomes Tk 98,000.

> The balance sheet is a test of whether the accounting story closes.

Assets of Tk 108,000 equal liabilities of Tk 14,000 plus accumulated fund of Tk 94,000. The figure is not produced by guessing. It is the residual after the organisation's assets and liabilities are classified and the period's result and capital items are carried into the fund.

## 17.6 Comparing the accounts of different organisations

> Different forms of organisation can use the same accounting foundations while reporting different purposes.

A sole trader reports the result available to the owner. A partnership reports the result available for sharing among partners under the partnership agreement. A company reports profit and changes in shareholders' equity. A non-trading organisation reports a surplus or deficit and changes in its accumulated or other funds. All still account for assets, liabilities, income, expenses and cash.

:table Four organisational forms and the main accounting emphasis

| Organisation | Main purpose | Period result | Residual interest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sole trader | Earn income for one owner | Profit or loss | Owner's equity |
| Partnership | Joint business activity | Profit or loss | Partners' capital/current accounts |
| Company | Business owned through shares | Profit or loss | Shareholders' equity |
| Non-trading organisation | Serve members or a cause | Surplus or deficit | Accumulated or relevant fund |

> The comparison should not erase important differences in legal form or reporting requirements.

The table is a conceptual bridge, not a substitute for the detailed accounting rules that apply to each organisation. Chapters 18 to 20 develop partnerships and companies. The present chapter focuses on the accounting logic needed for a club or association.

:case Situation: The neighbourhood bakers' association begins the year with cash Tk 29,000, equipment Tk 40,000, subscription arrears Tk 4,000 and no prepaid rent. It has Tk 5,000 of subscription advances and Tk 3,000 of salaries payable. During the year it receives Tk 90,000 subscriptions, Tk 8,000 entrance fees, Tk 15,000 capital donation and Tk 12,000 other receipts. It pays Tk 48,000 salaries, Tk 24,000 rent, Tk 10,000 training and sports, Tk 5,000 utilities, Tk 5,000 supplies and Tk 20,000 for equipment. Closing subscription arrears are Tk 7,000; closing subscription advances are Tk 8,000; closing salary payable is Tk 6,000; closing prepaid rent is Tk 3,000. Of the entrance fees, Tk 4,000 is treated as capital.
Question: What is the subscription income, salary expense, surplus and closing accumulated fund?
Reasoning: Subscription income is Tk 90,000: Tk 90,000 received + Tk 7,000 closing arrears − Tk 4,000 opening arrears + Tk 5,000 opening advance − Tk 8,000 closing advance. Salary expense is Tk 51,000 because Tk 48,000 paid − Tk 3,000 opening payable + Tk 6,000 closing payable = Tk 51,000. Other income is Tk 12,000 and the revenue portion of entrance fees is Tk 4,000. The capital donation and Tk 4,000 capital portion of entrance fees are excluded from ordinary income. Rent expense is Tk 21,000 because Tk 24,000 was paid and Tk 3,000 remains prepaid. Current expenses are Tk 51,000 salaries + Tk 21,000 rent + Tk 10,000 training and sports + Tk 5,000 utilities + Tk 5,000 supplies = Tk 92,000. Current income is Tk 106,000, giving a surplus of Tk 14,000.
Conclusion: Opening accumulated fund Tk 65,000 + surplus Tk 14,000 + capital donation Tk 15,000 + capital entrance fee Tk 4,000 = Tk 98,000.
Check: Total cash available is Tk 154,000 and total payments are Tk 112,000, leaving cash and bank of Tk 42,000. Closing assets are Tk 42,000 cash + Tk 60,000 equipment + Tk 7,000 subscription arrears + Tk 3,000 prepaid rent = Tk 112,000. Closing liabilities are Tk 8,000 subscription advance + Tk 6,000 salary payable = Tk 14,000. The closing accumulated fund is therefore Tk 98,000, so the case balances.

@! Go slowly here.

> A case is not finished merely because its income calculation is correct; the balance sheet must also close.

The association case illustrates an important discipline. A receipts-and-payments summary can support the income calculation, but the final statement must be checked against every opening balance, closing adjustment, capital item and cash movement. Here the closing assets of Tk 112,000 equal liabilities of Tk 14,000 plus the accumulated fund of Tk 98,000. The case can therefore be carried forward without an unexplained difference.

## Summary

> Non-trading accounting changes the purpose and vocabulary, but it does not abandon accounting discipline.

A receipts and payments account records cash and bank movements. An income and expenditure account measures income and expenses belonging to the period. Subscriptions require adjustments for arrears and advances. Entrance fees and donations must be classified according to their nature and conditions. The balance sheet reports assets, liabilities and the accumulated fund. A surplus increases the fund, while a deficit reduces it, subject to capital and restricted-fund treatments.

## Think it through

> The key distinction is between cash movement and period performance.

Why can a club receive Tk 100,000 in cash but recognise less than Tk 100,000 as current income?

Why can the purchase of equipment appear in receipts and payments but not appear as an expense of the whole amount in income and expenditure?

A member owes a subscription at year-end. Why can the organisation recognise income even though cash has not been received?

Why is a surplus not automatically an amount that can be distributed to members?

## Where people go wrong

> Most mistakes arise when cash and accrual ideas are mixed.

**Calling every receipt income.** A capital donation or advance subscription may not be current income.

**Calling every payment an expense.** Equipment purchased is an asset, not automatically a period expense.

**Ignoring arrears and advances.** Cash received is not the same as income earned for the period.

**Treating accumulated fund as cash.** The fund is a residual interest, not a bank account.

**Forgetting the purpose of a special receipt.** Donations and entrance fees may have conditions or capital characteristics that change their treatment.
