## 9.1 The puzzle of rent paid in advance: cash basis and accrual basis

> A bus pass paid for three months raises the same question as the bakery's rent.

@! Go slowly here: this is the first idea of the chapter, and the rest depends on it.

A student pays Tk 3,000 on 1 January for a bus pass that lasts three months. How much did the student spend on travel in January? One answer says Tk 3,000, because that is the money that left the purse in January. A second answer says Tk 1,000, because only one month of the pass has been used. Both answers are correct statements of a different fact. The first is about cash. The second is about use. A business meets the same choice every day.

> On 3 January Mitali pays three months of rent at once, and January's cost is unclear.

Chapter 4 recorded the same event in the bakery. On 3 January 2026, Mitali Rahman paid Tk 45,000 to the landlord for the rent of January, February and March. The payment left her bank on one day. The shop, however, will be used over three months. The question is this: what is the rent cost of January? Is it Tk 45,000, the sum that was paid, or Tk 15,000, one third of it?

@ Cash basis | Recording revenue and expenses only when cash is received or paid.

> The cash basis records an item on the day the cash moves.

The first answer follows the **cash basis**. Under this method, an expense is recorded on the day the cash is paid, and revenue is recorded on the day the cash is received. The method is easy to use. Many households keep their money records this way.

@ Accrual basis | Recording revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, whatever the date of cash.

> The accrual basis records an item in the period to which it belongs.

The second answer follows the **accrual basis**. Under this method, revenue is recorded in the period in which it is earned, and an expense is recorded in the period in which it is used up, whatever the date of the cash. Under the accrual basis, the rent of Tk 45,000 is divided into three equal parts. Each month receives Tk 15,000.

> The same Tk 135,000 of profit is divided very differently between the three months.

To see the effect, we need an assumption. Suppose that in each of January, February and March the bakery earns a profit of Tk 60,000 before any rent is charged. The figure is only an illustration; it is not part of the case file. The table shows the monthly profit under each basis.

:table Monthly profit before and after charging rent, under two bases (Tk, illustration)
| Month | Cash basis | Accrual basis |
| January | 15,000 | 45,000 |
| February | 60,000 | 45,000 |
| March | 60,000 | 45,000 |
| Total | 135,000 | 135,000 |

> The two bases give the same total but different months, and only one is a fair comparison.

Both bases give the same total of Tk 135,000 over the three months. The months, however, look very different. Under the cash basis, January looks poor and February and March look rich, although the shop worked in the same way in all three months. A bank officer who read the January figure might refuse a loan; an owner might decide to close the shop. Under the accrual basis the three months are equal, which agrees with what happened.

:fig Rent paid once on 3 January is used over three months. | ch9_fig1_rent.svg

> The recap states the idea in words before any entry is shown.

*Recap.* Cash moves on one day, but a benefit such as shop space is used over many days. Accrual accounting follows the use, not the cash. To record the use, the accountant makes an entry at the end of each period. The rest of the chapter explains these entries.

> The test sentence for the first idea of the chapter.
*You should now be able to say:* "The cash basis records an item when the cash moves; the accrual basis records it in the period it belongs to."

## 9.2 Why adjustments are needed: revenue recognition and matching

> Three reasons make the ledger out of date on the last day of a period.

Why is the unadjusted trial balance of Chapter 8 not ready for the statements? There are three reasons. First, cash often moves before or after the period to which the cash belongs, as the rent shows. Second, some changes happen with no transaction and no source document: the days pass, the supplies are used, and the oven becomes older. Third, the statements of Chapter 5 must show the profit of one period and the position at one date. A list that ignores these changes cannot do so.

@ Adjusting entry | A journal entry made at period end to bring accounts up to date.

> An adjusting entry records an effect of time or use, at the end of the period.

An **adjusting entry** is a journal entry made at the end of a period to bring accounts up to date. It follows the rules of Chapter 7 in every respect: a date, accounts, equal debits and credits, and a narration. It differs in one way. It is made because time has passed, and not because a transaction took place. An adjusting entry normally does not use the Cash account, since the cash was recorded, or will be recorded, as a transaction. It affects one account of the balance sheet and one account of the income statement.

@ Revenue recognition | The rule on when revenue counts as earned and is recorded.

> Revenue recognition says when revenue counts as earned.

Two rules direct the adjustments. The first is **revenue recognition**. It states that revenue is recorded when the business has done what it promised, such as delivering the bread or finishing the catering, and not when the cash arrives. A bakery that delivers cakes on 30 January has earned the revenue in January, even if the customer pays in February.

@ Matching concept | The rule that expenses are recorded in the same period as the revenue they helped earn.

> The matching concept puts each cost in the period of the revenue it helped to earn.

The second rule is the **matching concept**. It states that an expense is recorded in the same period as the revenue it helped to earn. The rent of January helped to earn the revenue of January, so it is charged to January. Together, the two rules explain why the accrual basis gives a fair picture of each period.^1

^1: The accounting standards of Chapter 3 ask companies to prepare their statements on the accrual basis. A small business may keep its day-to-day records on the cash basis, but its yearly statements must still show the accrual figures.

> The four kinds of adjustment depend on which comes first, the cash or the recording.

@ Deferral | An adjustment that spreads a cash payment or receipt made earlier across later periods.

@ Accrual | An adjustment that records an item before any cash has moved.

Adjustments fall into two groups, named by the order of events. In a **deferral**, the cash moved first, and the recording of the expense or revenue is deferred, that is, delayed, to later periods. In an **accrual**, the revenue or expense is recorded first, and the cash will move later. Each group has an expense form and a revenue form, so there are four kinds in all. Sections 9.3 and 9.4 teach them in turn.

:fig Four kinds of adjustment, named by which comes first: the cash or the recording. | ch9_fig2_four.svg

> Recap: adjusting entries and the two rules behind them.
*Before moving on.* Adjusting entries bring the ledger to the accrual basis. Two rules direct them: revenue is recorded when earned, and expenses are matched with the revenue they helped to earn.

## 9.3 Deferrals: prepaid expenses and unearned revenue

> A deferral begins with cash and ends with an expense or revenue in later periods.

@! Go slowly here: the same two accounts appear in every deferral, and the direction matters.

In a deferral the cash has already moved. Two kinds exist. In the first, the business has paid for something that it will use later. In the second, the business has been paid for something that it will supply later. We study each with a small example of clean numbers before we return to the bakery.

@ Prepaid expense | An expense paid in advance; an asset until the service is used.

> A prepaid expense is a payment for a benefit that has not yet been used.

A **prepaid expense** is an expense paid in advance. Until the benefit is used, it is an asset: the business owns a right to future service. As the benefit is used, the asset falls and the expense grows. A tea stall pays Tk 12,000 on 1 January for twelve months of insurance. On 31 March, three months have passed. The monthly cost is 12,000 ÷ 12 = Tk 1,000, and the cost used is 3 × 1,000 = Tk 3,000. The adjusting entry is: debit Insurance Expense Tk 3,000, credit Prepaid Insurance Tk 3,000. Prepaid Insurance then shows Tk 9,000, which is the nine months still to run.

> The bakery's rent follows the same steps: a payment, a monthly cost, then a smaller asset.

:case Case file, illustrative. On 3 January 2026, Mitali Bakery paid Tk 45,000 for three months of rent and debited Prepaid Rent. At 31 January, one month has been used. Rent for one month is 45,000 ÷ 3 = Tk 15,000. Adjusting entry A1: debit Rent Expense Tk 15,000 and credit Prepaid Rent Tk 15,000. Prepaid Rent now shows Tk 30,000, the rent for February and March, and Rent Expense shows Tk 15,000 for January.

@ Unearned revenue | Cash received before the goods or service is provided; a liability until earned.

> Unearned revenue is a promise to supply something in return for cash already received.

The second kind is **unearned revenue**. A business sometimes receives cash before it supplies the goods or service. Until it supplies them, it owes the customer a duty, so the amount is a liability, not revenue. As the business does its work, the liability falls and revenue grows. The tea stall receives Tk 9,000 on 1 January from a college canteen for three months of tea service. On 31 January, one third of the service has been given. The revenue earned is 9,000 ÷ 3 = Tk 3,000. The entry is: debit Unearned Revenue Tk 3,000, credit Sales Revenue Tk 3,000. Unearned Revenue then shows Tk 6,000.

> The bakery's advance receipt becomes revenue as cakes are delivered.
:case Case file, illustrative. On 22 January 2026, Mitali Bakery received Tk 12,000 in advance for six weekly cake deliveries at Tk 2,000 each, starting on 29 January. The receipt was credited to Unearned Revenue. On 29 January the first cake was delivered, so Tk 2,000 is earned. Adjusting entry A4: debit Unearned Revenue Tk 2,000 and credit Sales Revenue Tk 2,000. Unearned Revenue now shows Tk 10,000, which is five deliveries of Tk 2,000 each. The bakery still owes five cakes.

> The two deferrals are mirror images: one is an asset that shrinks, the other a liability that shrinks.

Notice the mirror. The prepaid expense is an asset that falls as the benefit is used. The unearned revenue is a liability that falls as the duty is done. In both cases the original cash entry stays as it was, and the adjusting entry moves the used part to the income statement.

> Recap: the two deferrals and their entries.
*Before moving on.* A deferral starts with cash. Prepaid expense: debit the expense, credit the asset. Unearned revenue: debit the liability, credit the revenue.

> The test sentence for deferrals.
*You should now be able to say:* "In a deferral the cash comes first, and each period receives its share of the expense or the revenue."

## 9.4 Accruals: accrued expenses and accrued revenue

> An accrual begins with use or service and ends with cash in a later period.

@! Go slowly here: in an accrual there is no earlier cash entry to adjust.

In an accrual, nothing has yet been recorded. The expense has been used, or the revenue has been earned, but no cash has moved and no entry exists. The adjusting entry creates the first record. Again there are two kinds.

@ Accrued expense | An expense used up but not yet paid or recorded.

> An accrued expense is a cost already used that the business has not yet paid.

An **accrued expense** is an expense that has been used up but has not been paid or recorded. A phone bill is the everyday example: the calls were made in January, but the bill arrives in February. Until it is paid, the business owes the amount, so a liability is recorded. A tea stall pays its helper Tk 600 a day. The last payday was 28 January. The days of 29, 30 and 31 January are unpaid, so the cost is 3 × 600 = Tk 1,800. The entry is: debit Wages Expense Tk 1,800, credit Wages Payable Tk 1,800.

> The bakery owes wages for three unpaid days at the end of January.
:case Case file, illustrative. Mitali Bakery paid wages of Tk 36,000 on 28 January. The wages for 29 to 31 January, Tk 4,000, are unpaid at 31 January. Adjusting entry A5: debit Wages Expense Tk 4,000 and credit Wages Payable Tk 4,000. The wages of January are therefore Tk 36,000 + Tk 4,000 = Tk 40,000, and the bakery owes Tk 4,000 to its staff.

@ Accrued revenue | Revenue earned but not yet received or recorded.

> Accrued revenue is a service already given that the customer has not yet been billed for.

The second kind is **accrued revenue**. The business has earned the revenue, but it has not received the cash and has not yet billed the customer. The business owns a claim against the customer, so an asset is recorded. The tea stall delivers tea to an office on 30 and 31 January for Tk 2,500, to be billed in February. The entry is: debit Accounts Receivable Tk 2,500, credit Sales Revenue Tk 2,500.

> The hotel catering is January revenue although it is not yet billed.
:case Case file, illustrative. On 30 January 2026, Mitali Bakery delivered catering to a hotel, worth Tk 10,000, and has not yet billed it. Adjusting entry A6: debit Accounts Receivable Tk 10,000 and credit Sales Revenue Tk 10,000. The revenue belongs to January. The cash will arrive in a later month.

> The same event, seen from the hotel's side, is an accrued expense.

The viewpoint of the other party shows the symmetry. The hotel has used the catering and owes Tk 10,000. In its own books the same event is an accrued expense. One person's accrued revenue is the other person's accrued expense, and one person's unearned revenue is the other person's prepaid expense.

> Recap: the two accruals and their entries.
*Before moving on.* An accrual starts with use or service. Accrued expense: debit the expense, credit a liability. Accrued revenue: debit a receivable, credit the revenue.

> The test sentence for accruals.
*You should now be able to say:* "In an accrual the recording comes first, and the cash follows in a later period."

## 9.5 Depreciation and supplies used: estimates at period end

> Some adjustments rest on a count or on an estimate rather than on a known price.

Two more adjustments belong to this chapter. They resemble the prepaid expense, because an asset is bought first and is used later. The difference is that no contract states how much has been used. The accountant counts or estimates.

> A count of supplies shows what is left, and the difference is the amount used.

Mitali bought supplies of Tk 30,000 for cash on 5 January and recorded them as an asset. At the end of the month she counts what is left in the store and finds Tk 8,000. Supplies of 30,000 − 8,000 = Tk 22,000 must have been used. Adjusting entry A2: debit Supplies Expense Tk 22,000 and credit Supplies Tk 22,000. The asset now shows the Tk 8,000 that she counted.

@ Depreciation | The spreading of an asset's cost over the years in which the asset is used.

> Depreciation spreads the cost of a long-lived asset over the years of its use.

The second adjustment concerns the oven and mixer. Long-lived assets such as equipment are used over many years. **Depreciation** is the spreading of an asset's cost over the years in which the asset is used. Mitali estimates that the equipment, which cost Tk 240,000, will serve for 8 years and will then be worth about Tk 48,000. The amount to spread is 240,000 − 48,000 = Tk 192,000. Divided by 8 years, this gives Tk 24,000 a year, or Tk 2,000 a month. Chapter 10 explains how such estimates are made and tested; here the figures are given.

@ Accumulated depreciation | The total depreciation charged on an asset since it was bought.

@ Contra account | An account that is deducted from a related account in the statements.

> Accumulated depreciation is a contra account: it is deducted from the asset it belongs to.

Adjusting entry A3 is: debit Depreciation Expense Tk 2,000 and credit Accumulated Depreciation: Equipment Tk 2,000. The credit does not reduce the Equipment account. The cost of Tk 240,000 stays in that account as a record of what was paid. The credit goes to **accumulated depreciation**, the total depreciation charged since the asset was bought. It is a **contra account**: a balance that is deducted from a related account in the statements. Equipment, less accumulated depreciation, shows 240,000 − 2,000 = Tk 238,000.

> Recap: supplies used and depreciation.
*Before moving on.* A count gives the supplies used; an estimate gives depreciation. Both debit an expense and credit an asset or a contra account.

> The adjusted figures feed the statements, and a second set of statements replaces the first.

The adjusted figures, not the unadjusted ones, go into the financial statements. The income statement will show profit of Tk 127,000. The balance sheet will show total assets of Tk 701,000, liabilities of Tk 94,000 and owner's equity of Tk 607,000, because 500,000 + 127,000 − 20,000 = 607,000. These figures replace the unadjusted statements of Chapter 5, which were correct only for the unadjusted books. Chapter 11 gathers all adjustments in one working paper, and Chapter 12 explains the reversing entries that some accountants make on the first day of the next period.

> A last example uses uneven numbers and part-month periods.

We finish the chapter with a harder example. A print shop (illustrative) closes its books on 31 December 2026. The first adjustment involves a part-month period.

> The worked example follows the order given, find, formula, steps, answer, check.
*Given.* On 16 October the shop paid Tk 27,000 for six months of insurance, to 15 April. The cost was debited to Prepaid Insurance.
*Find.* The insurance expense for the year and the prepaid balance on 31 December.
*Formula.* Expense = monthly cost × months used. Monthly cost = amount paid ÷ months insured.
*Steps.* The monthly cost is 27,000 ÷ 6 = Tk 4,500. From 16 October to 31 December is half of October, plus November and December: 2.5 months. The expense is 2.5 × 4,500 = Tk 11,250. The balance is 27,000 − 11,250 = Tk 15,750.
*Answer.* Debit Insurance Expense Tk 11,250 and credit Prepaid Insurance Tk 11,250. Prepaid Insurance shows Tk 15,750.
*Check.* The unused period is 3.5 months, and 3.5 × 4,500 = Tk 15,750, which agrees.

:table Four adjustments of a print shop at 31 December 2026 (Tk)
| Kind | Basis | Debit | Credit |
| Prepaid expense | Insurance: 2.5 months of 4,500 | Insurance Expense 11,250 | Prepaid Insurance 11,250 |
| Unearned revenue | Advance of 23,100 for seven months from 1 November; two months earned, 23,100 ÷ 7 × 2 | Unearned Revenue 6,600 | Sales Revenue 6,600 |
| Accrued expense | Wages of 1,400 a day, four days unpaid | Wages Expense 5,600 | Wages Payable 5,600 |
| Accrued revenue | Printing finished on 29 to 31 December, not yet billed | Accounts Receivable 9,450 | Sales Revenue 9,450 |

> Four slips are common in adjusting entries.

Four slips are common. The first is to adjust the wrong direction, for example to credit the expense in a deferral. The second is to count the months wrongly, especially when a period begins in the middle of a month. The third is to forget one half of the entry. The fourth is to adjust an item that has no balance to adjust. Before each entry, ask two questions: which account holds the figure now, and what should it hold at the end of the period?

> Recap: journalise, post, test again, then build the statements.
*Before moving on.* Journalise each adjustment, post it, prepare the adjusted trial balance, and use its figures for the statements. The test of equal totals is repeated.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in one paragraph.

A ledger must be brought up to date before the statements are made, because cash and use do not move together. The accrual basis records revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are used. Four kinds of adjustment exist: prepaid expenses and unearned revenue, where cash comes first, and accrued expenses and accrued revenue, where the recording comes first. Supplies used and depreciation are adjustments based on a count and an estimate. Each adjusting entry has one balance sheet account and one income statement account, and it does not use Cash. After posting, the adjusted trial balance tests the books again. For Mitali Bakery, the six adjustments reduce the profit of January from Tk 158,000 to Tk 127,000.

## Think it through

> A fresh business has six adjustments to prepare.

Rupa Laundry (illustrative) closes its books on 31 December 2026. The accountant has the following information. First, Tk 36,000 was paid on 1 September for twelve months of insurance and was debited to Prepaid Insurance. Second, the Supplies account shows Tk 18,000 of supplies bought during the year, with none at the start of the year; a count shows Tk 5,500 left. Third, wages of Tk 900 a day are unpaid for three days. Fourth, on 1 October the laundry received Tk 24,000 from a hostel for eight months of service and credited Unearned Revenue. Fifth, laundry done for a clinic on 29 to 31 December, worth Tk 6,300, has not been billed. Sixth, the depreciation of the equipment for the year is Tk 30,000. Write the six adjusting entries with narrations. Then state, in one figure, the effect of all six on profit.

## Where people go wrong

> Five common misunderstandings, each stated and corrected.

*"An adjusting entry moves cash."* It does not. The cash was recorded, or will be recorded, as a transaction. An adjusting entry records the effect of time or use on accounts that already exist, or on a new account for an amount owed or owing.

> Deferral and accrual are opposite in the order of cash and recording.
*"A deferral and an accrual are the same thing."* They are opposites in time. In a deferral the cash comes first, and in an accrual the cash comes last. If you ask which came first, the cash or the recording, you will classify correctly.

> The accrual basis adds to the cash record; it does not replace it.
*"The accrual basis ignores cash."* It does not. Cash is still recorded on the day it moves. The accrual basis adds a second record, the effect of time, so that each period shows its own revenue and cost.

> Only the used or earned part of the balance is adjusted.
*"The whole amount in the account is adjusted."* Only the part that has been used or earned is moved. Prepaid Rent keeps Tk 30,000 after A1, because two months are still to come.

> Depreciation spreads cost; it does not measure market value.
*"Depreciation records the fall in the market value of the asset."* It does not. Depreciation spreads the cost over the years of use; Chapter 10 explains this difference.

## In history

> A company that recorded revenue too early was charged with fraud.

The revenue recognition rule is tested when managers wish to show higher revenue than the business has earned. In 2001, the United States Securities and Exchange Commission sued former top officers of Sunbeam Corporation, an American maker of household appliances. The Commission alleged that, in 1997, the officers had recognised revenue on sales that did not meet the accounting rules, including bill-and-hold sales, in which goods were kept for the customer and not yet delivered. It stated that at least $60 million of the reported earnings of $189 million before tax in 1997 came from accounting fraud. The case settled in 2002. The lesson for this chapter is plain: revenue belongs to the period in which it is earned, and not to the period in which a manager would like to see it.

## Sources

> The matching concept has a classic source.

The idea of matching costs with revenues was developed in W. A. Paton and A. C. Littleton, *An Introduction to Corporate Accounting Standards* (American Accounting Association, 1940). The book argued that accounting should assign costs to the periods that receive their benefit.
