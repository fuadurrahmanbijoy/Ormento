## A Tea Seller's Tin Box

> A tea seller's single day raises three questions that this whole chapter will answer.

Every morning at six, a man named Jamal pushes a wooden cart to a corner near a bus stand in Dhaka. On the cart are a kettle, a gas cylinder, forty glass cups and a tin box. This morning the box holds Tk 400, and all of it is his own money. He walks to the market and spends Tk 300 on tea leaves, milk and sugar. He spends Tk 100 on gas. The tin box is now empty.

> By evening the box holds Tk 900, and the question is how much better off Jamal is.

During the day, bus passengers and shop workers buy 60 cups of tea at Tk 15 each. That evening Jamal tips the box onto a cloth and counts Tk 900. He smiles, and then he stops. He remembers that the box held Tk 400 when the day began, and that the tea leaves, the milk, the sugar and the gas are all used up. Is he Tk 900 better off tonight? Is he better off by some other amount? And what would the answer be if rain had kept the buyers away, so that only 20 cups were sold?

> Three questions arise: what the stall is for, how to judge the day, and who answers if the day goes badly.

Jamal's day raises three questions. The first is about purpose: why does a stall like his exist at all, and who needs it? The second is about measurement: how can he tell whether the day went well or badly? The third is about responsibility: if the day goes very badly and he owes money that he cannot pay, who must find that money? Would the answer change if Jamal had a partner, or if fifty people owned the stall together?

> The chapter answers these questions in order, and the answers explain why anyone keeps records.

This chapter answers these questions one at a time. Section 1.1 explains what a business is for and how its day is measured. Section 1.2 shows the three kinds of business. Section 1.3 explains what happens when payment comes later. Section 1.4 shows who can own a business. Section 1.5 describes organisations that do not aim to earn a gain. By the end, you will see why the owner of even the smallest stall needs a record of what happened each day. Chapter 2 then shows how that record becomes accounting.

## 1.1 What a Business Is For

> Nobody can make everything that they need, so people depend on one another.

Begin with your own week. You eat rice and vegetables, travel by bus or rickshaw, charge a phone and perhaps visit a doctor. You made almost none of these things yourself. Someone else grew the rice, someone built the bus, and someone trained for years to become a doctor. No person can do all of this work alone. For that reason, people have always depended on one another, and the way they do so is the starting point of every business.

> When each person does one task well and exchanges the result, everyone has more.

Why do people not make everything for themselves? Imagine a village where every family grows its own rice, weaves its own cloth and builds its own boat. Each family would be poor at some of these tasks, and it would lose time changing from one task to the next. Now suppose that one family weaves, another grows rice and a third builds boats. Each becomes skilled and works faster. The families then exchange what they make, and every family ends with more than before. A business is the organised form of this idea.

> A need can be met by exchange, where each side gives something and receives something.

A need is something a person must have or strongly wants, such as food, clothing, transport or medical care. Most needs are met by *exchange*: one person gives something and receives something in return. A farmer gives rice and receives money. A passenger gives money and receives a bus ride. Each side gains something that it values more than what it gave. If either side expected to lose, the exchange would not take place.

> What is exchanged falls into two groups: goods and services.
@ Goods | Physical things that a business sells, such as bread or chairs.

What is exchanged falls into two groups. The first group is **goods**, which means physical things that you can touch and carry, such as bread, chairs, shirts or phones. A loaf of bread is a good, and so is a bag of rice.

> A service is work done for someone that leaves no object.
@ Service | Work done for a customer that is not a physical thing, such as repair or delivery.

The second group is **service**, which means work done for someone that is not a physical thing. A barber cuts hair, a driver carries passengers, and a doctor gives advice. After a haircut, you have no object to carry home, only the result of the work. Many exchanges combine both groups. A tailor sells cloth, which is a good, and stitches it, which is a service.

> A business is an organisation that sells goods or services to earn money.
@! Go slowly here.
@ Business | An organisation that sells goods or services to earn money.

Now we can name the idea of this chapter. A **business** is an organisation that sells goods or services in order to earn money. Jamal's tea stall is a business, because he sells tea, which is a good, to people who want it. A large hospital, a garment factory and a corner shop are also businesses. They differ greatly in size, but each one makes exchanges with other people in order to earn money.

> Every business has people on three sides: the buyer, the seller to it, and the person who owns it.

Three kinds of person appear around every business, and this book uses a single word for each one. We define them in turn, beginning with the person who buys what the business sells.

> A customer is the person or business that buys.
@ Customer | A person or business that buys goods or services.

A **customer** is a person or business that buys goods or services. The bus passengers who buy Jamal's tea are his customers. Some people say *client*, especially for a service. This book uses *customer* for every case.

> A supplier sells to the business, and may also be a customer.
@ Supplier | A person or business that sells goods or services to another business.

A **supplier** is a person or business that sells goods or services to another business. The market trader who sells Jamal his milk is a supplier. Notice that the same trader is a customer of the dairy farmer. A person or business can be a supplier in one exchange and a customer in the next.

> The owner takes the risk and receives what is left.
@ Owner | The person who owns a business, takes its risks and receives its profit.

An **owner** is the person who owns a business, takes its risks and receives what is left over when the work is done. Jamal owns his stall. Nobody else shares in its results. Later in this chapter we shall see that a business can have one owner, several owners, or thousands.

> The figure shows the business at the centre of three kinds of exchange.

:fig The business and the people it exchanges with: suppliers, customers and its owner | fig01_exchange.svg

> The same business looks different from the customer's seat, the supplier's seat and the owner's seat.

Read the figure from left to right. Goods and services flow from the supplier to the business, and money flows back to the supplier. Then goods and services flow from the business to the customer, and money flows back to the business. The owner stands above, giving the business its first money and receiving its profit. Each person sees the business differently. The customer asks, "Is the price fair?" The supplier asks, "Will I be paid?" The owner asks, "Is anything left for me?" A good business must answer all three questions.

> Before the money is counted, the business must pay its suppliers and other bills.

Return to the cart. Jamal did not receive his Tk 900 for nothing. Before he sold a single cup, he spent money on leaves, milk, sugar and gas. These amounts are called costs: the money spent to run the business. A business can earn money only after it has spent some. This is why Jamal's box, which held Tk 900 in the evening, does not show how well he did.

> Profit is earnings minus costs, so Jamal's profit is Tk 500.
@ Profit | The money left when a business takes its costs away from what it earned.

The answer lies in the idea of **profit**, which means the money left when a business takes its costs away from what it earned. Some people say *net income* or *earnings*; this book uses *profit*. Jamal earned Tk 900 from 60 cups. He spent Tk 300 on materials and Tk 100 on gas, a total of Tk 400. His profit is Tk 900 minus Tk 400, which is Tk 500.

> If costs are larger than earnings, the result is a loss.
@ Loss | The amount by which a business's costs are more than what it earned.

Suppose that the rain kept customers away and only 20 cups were sold. Jamal would earn 20 times Tk 15, which is Tk 300. His costs are still Tk 400, because he bought the leaves, milk, sugar and gas before he knew how many cups he would sell. Now Tk 300 minus Tk 400 gives a negative number. A **loss** means the amount by which a business's costs are more than what it earned. Here the loss is Tk 100.

:box IN SHORT
Profit is what a business earns minus what it spends to earn it. When the sum is negative, the result is a loss. Jamal's good day gave a profit of Tk 500, and his rainy day gave a loss of Tk 100.

> A business is judged by its profit, not by the cash that happens to be in the box.

Compare the two measures. The box held Tk 900 on the good evening, and the profit was Tk 500. The two numbers differ because Tk 400 of the box's contents was Jamal's own money, which he had put in that morning. Profit measures only what the day added. This difference between *money in the box* and *profit* is the most important idea in the chapter, because the whole of accounting exists to measure profit correctly.

> Profit is also the reward for risk, because a business may lose money as well as earn it.

Why should the owner receive the profit? The reason is risk. Before the first cup is sold, Jamal has already spent his own money. If nobody comes, he loses it. The customers pay their money only when they receive goods, and the suppliers are paid on their agreed date, but the owner has no promise of anything. Profit is the reward for accepting this risk, and for the work of organising the business. A business that never earns a profit cannot reward its owner, and it will not last.

> A customer pays more than the materials cost because the business adds something the customer values.

Why will a customer pay Tk 15 for a cup of tea when the leaves, milk, sugar and gas cost Jamal less than that? The reason is that Jamal adds something. He boils the tea, mixes it, pours it into a clean cup and stands at a place where people are thirsty. The customer values these things, and pays for them. In the same way, a phone seller adds convenience, and a barber adds skill. A business that adds nothing that anyone values will find no customers, and its owner will make a loss.

> Earnings are not the same as profit, and profit is not the same as cash.

Three words are easily mixed up, so keep them apart. *Earnings* are the money a business gains from its sales. *Profit* is what remains after the costs are taken from earnings. *Cash* is the money actually in the box or the bank. On Jamal's good day, earnings were Tk 900, profit was Tk 500 and cash in the box at night was Tk 900, because his own morning Tk 400 had been spent and then replaced by sales. In a later chapter, we shall find days when these three numbers all differ.

> The same method of measurement works for a business of any size, with the same three steps.

The method never changes with size. First, add up what the business earned. Second, add up what it spent to earn it. Third, take the second total away from the first. A shop with a hundred staff does the same three steps as Jamal. Only the numbers, and the number of items to add, become larger.

> The simple sum leaves some things out, which later chapters will add.

Our simple sum has limits. Jamal's own work is worth something, yet we did not subtract it. His kettle and cups wear out over time, yet we did not count that loss either. These items are real, and accounting has rules to deal with them. Chapters 9 and 10 explain how. For now, remember that the sum in this section is a first step.

### A Worked Example: Jamal's Week

> A week of sales shows the profit method repeated six times and then added.

One day teaches the method, and a week tests it. In the table that follows, each row is one day. The columns show the cups sold, the money earned, the money spent, and the result, which is the profit or the loss. We read each row in the same way: earned minus spent. After the last row, we add the column of results to find the profit for the week.

:table Jamal's tea stall, Monday to Saturday (amounts in taka)
| Day | Cups sold | Earned | Spent | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | 60 | 900 | 400 | 500 |
| Tuesday | 60 | 900 | 400 | 500 |
| Wednesday | 50 | 750 | 400 | 350 |
| Thursday | 60 | 900 | 400 | 500 |
| Friday (rain) | 20 | 300 | 400 | -100 |
| Saturday | 70 | 1,050 | 400 | 650 |

> The example is set out in the standard order: given, find, formula, steps, answer and check.

*Given.* Each cup sells for Tk 15, and the daily spending is Tk 400. The cups sold each day are in the table. *Find.* The profit for the week. *Formula.* In words: profit equals total earned minus total spent. In symbols: P = E − S, where P is profit, E is the total earned and S is the total spent. *Steps.* The cups sold are 60 + 60 + 50 + 60 + 20 + 70 = 320. The total earned is 320 × Tk 15 = Tk 4,800. The total spent is 6 × Tk 400 = Tk 2,400. So P = Tk 4,800 − Tk 2,400.

> The answer is checked by adding the daily results, which must give the same total.

*Answer.* Jamal's profit for the week is Tk 2,400. *Check.* Add the daily results instead: 500 + 500 + 350 + 500 − 100 + 650. The first two give 1,000, the third gives 1,350, the fourth gives 1,850, Friday's loss reduces it to 1,750, and Saturday gives 2,400. Both methods agree. Notice that one bad day did not turn the week into a loss, because the good days were larger. A common slip is to forget the minus sign on a loss day, which gives a total that is too high by Tk 200.

### Why a Business Needs a Record

> Memory is enough for one day, but not for a month.

Jamal can remember one day. He knows what he bought, what he sold and what is in the box. But suppose he keeps the stall for a whole month. He now has thirty days of buying and selling to remember. Was Tuesday's profit better than Friday's? Did the price of milk rise during the month? Did he pay the gas seller twice or not at all? No memory can answer such questions with certainty.

> A written record answers questions that memory cannot, and others can check it.

A *record* is a written note of something that happened, kept so that it can be checked later. Chapter 2 defines it properly. Here we need only the idea. If Jamal writes down each day's purchases and sales, he can add them at the end of the month and learn his profit. He can compare weeks. He can show the milk trader what he paid. If he ever wants a loan, he can show a bank officer what the stall earns. Writing turns memory into evidence.

> Every later chapter of this book shows one way of keeping and using such a record.

This need for a record is the reason for the rest of the book. As businesses grow, their records must grow too. Mitali's bakery will need to record cash, debts, the oven, the supplies, the wages and the rent. Chapter 2 explains how such records became the subject of accounting. The remaining chapters teach you how to keep them, one idea at a time.

> A short test of profit and loss.
*Pause and check.* A baker spends Tk 700 on flour and gas, and sells loaves for Tk 1,000. What is the profit? What would the result be if she had sold loaves for Tk 600?

> The section closes with a short recap and a test of what you have learned.

*Before moving on.* Exchange meets needs. A business sells goods or services to customers, buys from suppliers, and belongs to an owner. Profit is earnings minus costs, and a loss is the reverse. *You should now be able to say:* "A business earns profit when what it earns is more than what it spends."

## 1.2 Three Kinds of Business: Service, Merchandising, Manufacturing

> Businesses can be sorted by what they do with the things they sell.

Walk along any busy street in Dhaka and you will pass a clinic, a shop selling mobile phones, a bakery and a garment factory. All four are businesses, yet each one does something different. Sorting them into kinds helps us to see what each one earns and what each one spends. It also prepares us for later chapters, where the records of each kind differ.

> The first kind sells work, not objects.

@ Service business | A business that earns money by doing work for customers, such as a clinic.

A **service business** earns money by doing work for its customers. A clinic, a barber, a driving school and a private tutor are service businesses. The customer leaves with a result, such as better health or a new skill, and not with a new object. A service business spends money mainly on people's time and on the place where the work is done.

> The second kind buys finished goods and sells them again.

@ Merchandising business | A business that buys finished goods and sells them again, such as a shop.

A **merchandising business** buys finished goods and sells them again. The shop selling mobile phones is an example. The owner does not make a single phone. She buys phones from a supplier, keeps them in the shop, and sells each one at a higher price. Her earnings are the selling prices, and her main cost is the price paid to the supplier. Some people say *trading business* for the same idea.

> The third kind makes goods from materials.

@ Manufacturing business | A business that makes goods from materials and then sells them, such as a bakery.

A **manufacturing business** makes goods from materials and then sells them. A garment factory turns cloth and thread into shirts. A bakery turns flour, sugar and water into bread. The business buys materials, spends money on labour and machines to change them, and sells the new product. Its records are the richest of the three kinds, because the cost of the product has several parts.

> A tea stall is a small manufacturing business, which shows that size does not decide the kind.

Size does not decide the kind. Return to Jamal. He buys tea leaves, milk and sugar, and he turns them into cups of tea by boiling and mixing. He changes materials into a new product, so his stall is a very small manufacturing business. A large supermarket that never changes the goods it sells is a merchandising business. A tiny repair stall is a service business. Always ask what the business does with what it sells.

> A small sum for each kind shows what each earns and what each spends.

Let us give each kind a small sum. A private tutor charges Tk 500 an hour and teaches 20 hours in a month. She earns Tk 10,000. She spends Tk 1,500 on bus fares and printed pages, so her profit is Tk 8,500. A phone seller buys a phone for Tk 12,000 and sells it for Tk 14,000. The difference is Tk 2,000, from which she must still pay rent and wages. A baker spends Tk 20 on flour, sugar and gas for each loaf and sells it for Tk 35, leaving Tk 15 before other costs. Notice that only the manufacturer's Tk 20 includes the cost of changing materials into goods.

> The next table sets the three kinds of business side by side.

The table that follows has one row for each kind of business. For each row, it states what the business does, what it sells, and an example. Read it one row at a time, and compare the rows.

Read the table one row at a time. The row for service says that the business does work and sells that work. Each row ends with an example you can picture.

> One product often passes through all three kinds of business before it reaches you.

A single shirt can pass through all three kinds. A garment factory makes it from cloth, so the factory is a manufacturing business. A wholesaler, which is a merchandising business that buys large numbers of goods and sells them to shops, buys it from the factory. A shop buys it from the wholesaler and sells it to you, another merchandising business. A tailor who shortens the sleeves for a fee is a service business. The shirt, therefore, links four businesses, each one a customer of the one before it.

> Many real businesses mix two kinds, so the best question is what most of the money comes from.

Real businesses often mix two kinds. A pharmacy sells medicines that it bought finished, which is merchandising, and it also gives advice, which is service. A restaurant makes meals from materials and also gives table service. When a business mixes kinds, we describe it by the kind that brings in most of its earnings, and we note the other kind when it matters. This book does the same.

> A customer sees only the product, but the owner sees how it was made.

The customer and the owner see a business from different seats. A customer who buys a loaf sees bread on a shelf and a price on a label. The owner sees flour, an oven, workers' wages and the gas bill. She must know the cost of each part, because she sets the price from them. This is why manufacturing businesses keep the most detailed records. Chapter 14 returns to the goods that a merchandising or manufacturing business holds, and Chapter 13 shows how a merchandising business records them.

The figure shows the three kinds as three paths. Each starts with a supplier on the left and ends with a customer on the right. In the service path, the business adds only its work. In the merchandising path, the goods arrive finished and leave unchanged. In the manufacturing path, the goods arrive as materials and leave as a new product. From now on, this book follows one business, a bakery in Dhaka. Its owner, Mitali Rahman, makes and sells bread, cakes and catering orders. Why does the kind matter? The kind tells you what the business earns and what it must spend. A service business has few materials to buy. A merchandising business must always hold goods to sell. ## 1.3 Paying Now and Paying Later: Cash and 'On Account'

> People can pay at once or later, and the choice changes what a business must record.

Think about the last time you bought a snack and paid at once. Now think about a neighbour who buys groceries and says, "I shall pay you at the end of the month." Both buyers receive their goods on the same day, yet the second buyer still owes money after leaving the shop. This difference may look small, but it changes how a business must keep its records.

> Cash is money that can be used at once.
@ Cash | Money in coins, notes or a bank account that can be used at once.

@! Go slowly here.

The first way is payment in **cash**, which means money in coins, notes, or a bank account that can be used at once. Jamal receives cash when a passenger hands him Tk 15. In a bank account, the money counts as cash because the owner can spend it. When a customer pays in cash, the exchange is complete on the day: the goods leave and the money arrives.

> Buying on account means paying later.
@ On account | Bought or sold now, with payment to be made later.

The second way is to buy or sell **on account**, which means that the goods or services are given now and payment is made later. Some people say *on credit*; this book uses *on account*. When the milk trader says to Jamal, "Take the milk today and pay me on Friday," Jamal has bought milk on account. The milk has changed hands, but no money has.

> A debt is money owed to someone else.
@ Debt | Money that a person or business must repay to someone else.

When something is bought on account, a **debt** is created. A debt is money that a person or business must repay to someone else. On the day Jamal takes the milk, he owes the trader. The trader is owed that same amount. Both sides have one debt, and they see it from opposite sides: one owes, the other is owed.

> Selling on account does not mean that the seller distrusts or trusts the buyer without limit.

Buying on account is a normal habit of trade, and it does not mean that the buyer is poor. Large and respected businesses buy on account every day. The seller does, however, take a risk. If the buyer never pays, the seller has given away goods for nothing. A careful seller therefore sells on account only to buyers it knows, and it records each debt so that it can ask for payment on the due date. This risk is one reason why records of debts are important.

> The same event is a debt from one side and a claim from the other side.

Look again at the example, because the two sides are the key. Jamal owes Tk 200 to the milk trader. The milk trader is owed Tk 200 by Jamal. In Chapter 4, the amount that Jamal owes will be called *accounts payable*, and the amount that the trader is owed will be called *accounts receivable*. For now, remember these plain pictures: "money we owe" and "money owed to us".

:fig The same sale made for cash and on account, shown on a line of days | fig03_cash_account.svg

> The figure shows that a sale on account has a gap between delivery and payment.

Read the figure from left to right. On day 1 the goods are delivered. In the upper line, a cash sale, the money arrives on day 1 as well, so no gap appears. In the lower line, a sale on account, the money arrives on day 30. During those 29 days between, a debt exists. The size of this gap is what every business must watch.

> Payments by bank or by mobile phone also count as cash, because the money can be used at once.

Notice that cash does not mean only coins and notes. A customer who pays by bank transfer or by a mobile phone payment service also pays in cash in this book, because the money reaches the seller's account and can be spent at once. What matters is whether the payment arrives on the day of the exchange. If it does, we call it cash. If it is promised for a later day, we call it *on account*.

> Sales on account help customers but make the seller wait for money.

Why would a business sell on account? Because customers may prefer it, and a customer who is allowed to pay later may buy more. A school ordering cakes for a festival may need a month to pay. If the seller refuses, the school may buy from another seller. Selling on account therefore helps to win customers. Yet it makes the seller wait. In the meantime, the seller must still pay staff and suppliers.

> Buying on account helps the buyer but creates a debt that must be paid.

Buying on account has the same two sides. It helps the buyer, who receives goods before paying and can sell them first. It also creates a debt that must be paid, usually by a fixed date. A business that cannot pay its debts on time may lose its suppliers' trust. For this reason, a careful owner always knows how much the business owes and when each debt is due.

> Writing every debt down is the first reason a business needs records.

Here we reach the first reason why businesses keep records. A cash sale is complete on the day, and memory may be enough. But a sale on account leaves a debt that lasts for weeks. Jamal may have ten customers who owe him a little. If he forgets one, he loses money. If he forgets what he owes the milk trader, he risks an argument. A written record protects both sides.

> A small example with sums shows how a debt grows from day to day.

Here is a small example. Jamal takes milk on account from the trader on five days, Monday to Friday, at Tk 200 each day. On Monday evening he owes Tk 200. On Tuesday he owes Tk 400, and on each later day the amount grows by Tk 200. On Friday he owes Tk 1,000. When he pays the whole amount on Friday, the debt falls to nothing. At each moment, the written record would show how much he owed, and nobody could argue about it.

> A sale on account counts as a sale when the goods are delivered, not when the cash arrives.

One more point prevents a common error. A sale on account is still a sale. The bakery that delivers cakes to the school on 15 January has made a sale on 15 January, even though the school pays in February. What changes is only the form in which the seller holds the reward: not cash yet, but a promise of cash. Chapter 9 returns to this idea in detail.

> A customer may also pay before receiving goods, which is a third way that this book notes.

A third pattern exists. A customer may pay first and receive the goods later. A family may pay Tk 500 as a deposit when they order a wedding cake for next month. The baker holds the money, but she still owes the cake. She owes goods, not money. Chapter 9 explains how a business records such payments in advance. The three patterns are therefore: pay at once, pay later, and pay first.

> A short test of cash, on account and debt.
*Pause and check.* On Monday a florist hands over flowers and the buyer says, "I shall pay on Friday." Is this a cash sale? Who owes money, and to whom?

> The section closes with a recap of cash, on account and debt.

*Before moving on.* Cash is money that can be used at once. On account means goods or services are given now and paid for later. A debt is money one side owes and the other side is owed. *You should now be able to say:* "When I buy on account, I owe a debt until I pay."

## 1.4 Who Owns the Business: Sole Trader, Partnership, Company

> The law offers three main ways to own a business, and each way answers the question of who is responsible.

Return to the third question from Jamal's day. If a business owes more than it can pay, who must find the money? The answer depends on how the business is owned. In most countries, including Bangladesh, three forms of ownership are common. We shall meet each one in turn and then compare them. Chapters 17 to 20 develop them in detail, so here we need only the outline.

> The sole trader has one owner who answers for all debts.
@ Sole trader | A business owned and run by one person, who is personally responsible for its debts.

The first form is the **sole trader**. A sole trader is a business owned and run by one person, who is personally responsible for its debts. Some people say *sole proprietor*. Jamal's stall and Mitali's bakery are sole traders. The name does not mean that the owner works alone; Mitali may employ twenty bakers. It means that she is the only owner.

> A sole trader keeps all the profit but also bears all the losses.

The sole trader has three clear advantages. She can start with little formality, she makes every decision herself, and she keeps all the profit. But there are costs. If the bakery owes more than it holds, those who are owed money may ask Mitali to pay from her own savings, because in law she and her bakery are one. Her responsibility has no upper limit, and her only source of money is her own savings and what she can borrow.

> A partnership has two or more owners who share profit.
@ Partnership | A business owned by two or more people who agree to share its profit.

The second form is the **partnership**. A partnership is a business owned by two or more people who agree to share its profit. Imagine that Jamal and his cousin join to run three stalls. One can cook better and the other can sell better. Together they bring more money and more skill than either could alone. They agree how to divide profit and, ideally, they write the agreement down.

> A partnership pools money and skills, but partners answer for each other's decisions.

The strength of a partnership is that it pools money and skills. Its weakness is shared responsibility. If one partner makes a bad promise to a supplier, all partners may have to pay. Partners may also disagree about decisions or about how to share profit. For this reason, careful partners write an agreement before they begin. Chapter 18 explains what that agreement contains and how profit is shared.

> A company is separate from its owners in law.
@ Company | A business that the law treats as separate from its owners, who hold shares.

The third form is the **company**. A company is a business that the law treats as separate from its owners, who hold shares. Some people say *corporation*. A share is one of the equal parts into which ownership is divided; Chapter 20 explains shares fully. Because the law sees the company as a separate person, the company, not its owners, owes the debts.

@! Go slowly here.

> A company can raise money from many people because each owner risks only the money paid for shares.

This separation has an important effect. Suppose fifty people each pay Tk 10,000 for shares in a bakery company. If the company fails, each person may lose the Tk 10,000 but nothing more; those who are owed money cannot demand more from the shareholders' savings. Because the risk is limited, many people are willing to put in money, and a company can gather large sums. Chapter 20 calls this *limited liability* and explains it carefully.

> The size of a business is a different question from its form of ownership.

Many people think that *company* only means a large business. That is not correct. A company can be tiny, with two owners and a single shop. A partnership can be large, with hundreds of partners. A sole trader can employ many workers. Size describes how big a business is; form describes who owns it and who answers for its debts. Keep the two ideas apart.

> A business also differs by how long it lasts and how much paperwork it needs.

The forms also differ in two practical ways. The first is permanence. A sole trader's business is tied to one person; if she stops, the business stops. A partnership may end when a partner leaves, unless the partners have agreed otherwise. A company continues even when its shareholders change, because the company is separate from them. The second is paperwork. The law of Bangladesh asks a company to register and to keep proper records, and it asks more of a company than of a sole trader.^2

> Each form also gives the owners' share of the business a different name in the records.

The forms matter to accounting for one more reason. In each form, the owners' claim on the business has a different name. For a sole trader this book says *owner's equity*, for a partnership *partners' capital*, and for a company *shareholders' equity*. The ideas are similar, but the records differ. Chapter 4 explains owner's equity, Chapter 18 explains partners' capital, and Chapter 20 explains shareholders' equity.

> The same bakery would look different under each form, though the ovens and the bread would not change.

Imagine Mitali's bakery under each form. As a sole trader, she decides everything and holds every risk. If she took a partner, such as a baker who brings skill, the two would decide together and share profit. If she changed the business into a company, the bakery would own its ovens and owe its debts in its own name, and Mitali would hold shares. In all three cases the bread is the same. What changes is who owns the business and who answers for its debts. Chapters 18 to 20 will follow the bakery through these changes.

> Four questions help an owner to choose a form.

How can an owner choose in practice? Four questions help. First, how much money is needed, and who can supply it? A large sum may call for many owners. Second, how much risk is the owner willing to hold personally? If the answer is very little, a company limits the risk. Third, who will make decisions? A sole trader decides alone, and partners must agree. Fourth, how much paperwork can the owner manage? A sole trader has least, and a company has most. No answer is right for every owner, and the owners may later change their choice.

> The right form depends on what the owners want: simplicity, shared skill, or the ability to raise large sums.

How should an owner choose? Consider three people. Sultana wants to open a small tailoring shop with her savings and decide everything herself; the sole trader suits her. Two brothers want to combine different skills and divide profit, and a partnership suits them. A group of fifty neighbours wants to invest in a food business without risking their homes; a company suits them. Notice that each choice trades something for something else.

*Pause and check.* Which of the three forms makes the owners personally responsible for the debts? Which form places the debts on the business itself?

> The section closes with a recap of the three forms.

*Before moving on.* A sole trader has one owner who answers for all debts. A partnership has two or more owners who share profit and answer for debts. A company is a separate person in law, owned by shareholders who risk only their shares. *You should now be able to say:* "The form of ownership decides who must pay the debts."

## 1.5 Organisations That Do Not Seek Profit

> Some organisations exist to serve members or a cause, not to earn profit.

Not every organisation tries to make a profit. A village football club exists so that its members can play. A charity may feed hungry families. A neighbourhood association may maintain a shared lane. These groups all deal with money, yet they exist for a different purpose from a business. Their money is a tool for their cause, not the goal.

> A non-trading organisation exists for its members or cause.
@ Non-trading organisation | An organisation that does not exist to earn profit, such as a club or charity.

We call such a group a **non-trading organisation**, which means an organisation that does not exist to earn profit, such as a club or charity. Some people say *non-profit organisation*. Such an organisation may still sell things, for example tickets to a match, but selling is not its purpose. If it ends a year with more money than it spent, that amount is not a profit to share among members. It stays to support the cause.

> These organisations still need records, because members and donors want to know how money was used.

Non-trading organisations need records for the same reasons as businesses. Members who pay fees want to know how the money was used. People who give donations want to be sure that the gifts reach the cause. The law may also require records. Chapter 17 shows how clubs and charities keep their accounts, and why the words *surplus* and *deficit* replace *profit* and *loss* there.

> Such organisations are paid for by their members, by donors and sometimes by sales.

How does a non-trading organisation obtain its money? Often it charges its members a regular fee. It may receive gifts from people who support the cause. It may also earn something from small sales, such as tickets or food at a meeting. The money is spent on the cause: the football, the meals for hungry families, or the repair of a shared lane. A school canteen run by parents is a useful mixed case. It sells food, which looks like trade, but any money left goes back to the school.

> The recap of this short section.
*Before moving on.* A non-trading organisation serves members or a cause, and it keeps records so that those who give money can see how it was used. *You should now be able to say:* "A club earns money, but its purpose is not profit."

* * *

> A non-trading organisation may also deal with the government, members and donors, each of whom needs a report.

Such an organisation answers to several groups. Members want to know that their fees were used fairly. Donors want to know that gifts reached the cause. The government may ask for reports if the organisation has special legal standing. Because so many people trust it, an organisation of this kind often needs more careful records than a small business. Chapter 17 will show that its accounts use different names, but the ideas are the same as those of this book.

> Whenever other people give money to an organisation, they have a right to see the record.

A last point links this section to the rest of the chapter. Whenever many people give money to an organisation, whether as fees or as gifts, they trust it. A record is how that trust is kept. This is true of a stall, a company and a club. Records are therefore not a matter for profit-seeking businesses alone.

## Worked Case: Four Businesses, One Idea

> The worked case applies the whole chapter to four businesses of rising difficulty.

This worked case applies the chapter to four businesses, moving from a familiar case to a new setting and then to a combined case.

> The case states four businesses and the questions to ask of each.

*Situation.* First, Jamal's tea stall, which we know well. Second, Mitali Rahman's bakery. Third, a small garment factory near Dhaka, owned by three sisters, which sells shirts to a buyer in another country and pays for cloth a month after it arrives. Fourth, the bakery again, after it adds a counter that sells bottled drinks bought from a supplier. *Question.* For each business: what kind is it, how do its customers pay, and what form of ownership does it have?

> The case file records the bakery's kind, owner and start date.
:case Case file, illustrative. Business: a bakery in Dhaka that makes and sells bread, cakes and catering orders. Owner: Mitali Rahman, who owns and runs it as a sole trader. Start date: 1 January 2026. Kind of business: manufacturing. No figures are recorded yet.

> Each business is judged in turn, starting with the familiar stall.

*Reasoning.* The tea stall changes materials into tea, so it is a manufacturing business. Its customers pay cash on the spot, and Jamal is a sole trader. The bakery also changes materials into goods, so it too is manufacturing. Most customers pay in cash at the counter, but a school may buy catering on account. Mitali is the only owner, so the form is sole trader.

> The garment factory is a new setting that combines manufacturing, on account dealing and shared ownership.

The garment factory changes cloth and thread into shirts, so it is a manufacturing business. It buys cloth on account, which creates a debt to the cloth supplier, and it sells shirts to its buyer, who may also pay later. Three sisters own it. If they run it together and share profit, it is a partnership. If they have formed a company, the law treats the factory as a separate person. The facts given do not settle the form, so we must say what we would need to know.

> The last case mixes two kinds of business, so it is described by the kind that earns most.

The bakery with a drinks counter is a mixed case. It still makes bread and cakes, which is manufacturing. The counter buys finished bottles and resells them, which is merchandising. Cash sales over the counter are paid at once, while the school's order creates a debt owed to the bakery. Because baking brings in most of the money, we call the business manufacturing and note the merchandising part.

> The case shows that kind, payment and ownership are three separate questions.

The four answers also show that the questions are independent. A business of any kind may be owned in any form, and may take cash or sell on account. The bakery's kind does not tell us its owner's form, and its owner's form does not tell us how its customers pay. Whenever you meet a new business, ask the three questions one by one, and do not let the answer to one decide the others.

> The garment factory case shows that missing facts must be named, not guessed.

Notice what we did with the garment factory. The facts did not say whether the sisters formed a partnership or a company, so we did not guess. We said what extra fact would settle the answer: whether the law treats the factory as a separate person, and whether the sisters answer personally for its debts. A good answer in accounting names its missing facts. Guessing can lead to a wrong record, and a wrong record misleads every person who reads it.

> The conclusion and check confirm that each answer follows from the definitions.

*Conclusion.* The tea stall: manufacturing, cash, sole trader. The bakery: manufacturing, cash and on account, sole trader. The garment factory: manufacturing, on account both ways, form unknown until the owners tell us. The bakery with drinks: manufacturing with a merchandising part, cash and on account, sole trader. *Check.* Each answer uses one definition from this chapter, and each shows that kind, payment and ownership are three separate questions.

## Summary

> The summary restates the chapter's main ideas in a few lines.

A business sells goods or services to earn money, so it deals with customers, suppliers and an owner. Profit is what is left when costs are taken from earnings, and a loss is the opposite. Businesses are service, merchandising or manufacturing, and many mix kinds. Payment may be in cash or on account; the second leaves a debt. Ownership may be sole trader, partnership or company, and the form decides who answers for debts. Non-trading organisations serve a cause. All need records.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five common mistakes about this chapter are stated and corrected here.

*"The money in the box is the profit."* The box also holds the owner's own money. Profit is earnings minus costs. *"A company is a large business."* A company is a form of ownership, and it can be tiny. *"A sale on account is not yet a sale."* The sale is made on delivery, and only the money is delayed. *"A bakery is a shop, so it is merchandising."* A bakery makes its goods, so it is manufacturing. *"A non-trading organisation handles no money."* It handles money, but its aim is its cause, not profit.

* * *

> Two more mistakes concern the owner's own work and the purpose of the business.

*"If the owner works in the business, the work costs nothing."* The owner's work has value, even when no wage is paid, and later chapters explain how accounting treats it. *"A business exists only to earn money for its owner."* Profit rewards the owner, but a business also meets customers' needs and gives work to staff and suppliers. A business that ignores its customers will soon lose its profit.

## In History: The Muslin of Dhaka

> Dhaka's muslin trade shows weavers, middlemen and buyers working together long before modern shops.

For several centuries, the cotton cloth called muslin made Dhaka famous. When Dhaka became the Mughal capital of Bengal, its muslin received the patronage of emperors and nobles and attracted buyers from distant lands.^1 The cloth was made by weavers, but it reached its buyers through merchants and through agents known as *dalals* and *paikars*, as well as through the buyers' own officials. Here is the whole chain of this chapter: those who make, those who sell onward, and those who pay.

> The numbers of the trade show how large it was and how it ended.

Estimates record that in 1747 the cotton goods exported from Dhaka, chiefly fine muslin, were valued at 28 and a half lakh rupees, where one lakh is 100,000. After 1757, the industry declined. By the end of the eighteenth century, exports had fallen to about half the 1747 figure, and by the middle of the nineteenth century to less than ten lakh rupees. Historians name the loss of patronage and, above all, to the cheap cloth that machines in England could produce.

> The muslin trade also shows how many parties each needed a record of what they owed.

Notice also how many parties the muslin trade involved. A weaver might sell to an agent, who sold to a merchant, who sold to a buyer in a distant market. At every link, one party owed money to another, or was owed it. The trade could work only if each party could trust the others to pay. Trust of this kind is easier when there is a written record that both sides accept.

> The lesson is that a business, however famous, cannot ignore its customers and its costs.

The lesson fits this chapter. A business lives by exchange with customers, and it fails when the customers go or when a cheaper maker takes them. Weavers, middlemen and buyers each needed to know what they owed and what was owed to them. Every trader who dealt across such a chain was a business owner who needed a record of debts and earnings. Chapter 2 explains how those records grew into accounting.

^2: The rules for registering a company and keeping its records are set by Bangladesh law; Chapter 20 names them.

^1: This account follows the entry on muslin in Banglapedia, the national encyclopedia of Bangladesh.
