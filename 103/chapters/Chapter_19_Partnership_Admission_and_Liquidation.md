## When the partnership changes

> A partnership can change without every change affecting the firm’s assets.

> A new partner can enter in more than one way, and the accounting depends on the route.

Nasrin Akter may buy part of an existing partner’s interest. In that case, the payment may pass directly between the individuals and the firm’s net assets do not change. Alternatively, Nasrin may invest cash directly into the partnership. Then the firm receives a new asset and records a new capital claim.

@ admission of a partner | The process by which a person becomes a partner in an existing partnership.

> The distinction is important because accounting follows the economic event.

A private purchase of an existing partner’s interest is mainly a transfer between capital accounts. A direct investment into the firm increases the partnership’s resources and the new partner’s capital.

> At the other end is liquidation.

When a firm is liquidated, the business stops as a going concern and its assets are converted into cash or otherwise settled. Liabilities are paid according to the applicable rules, and the remaining amount is distributed among partners after gains and losses are allocated.

@ liquidation | The process of ending a partnership’s business, realising its assets, settling obligations, and distributing the remaining resources.

* * *

## 19.1 Why partnerships change

> Partnership changes reflect changes in people, money, purpose, or the continued ability to operate together.

> A partnership may change because a new person is needed.

A firm may require more capital, another skill, or a new manager. Admission gives the partnership a way to change ownership while continuing the business.

> A partnership may also change because an existing partner leaves.

Retirement can occur by agreement, under the partnership deed, or under the conditions provided by law. Bangladesh’s Partnership Act states that a person may be introduced as a partner only with the consent of all existing partners, subject to the Act and the partnership contract.^1

@ retirement | The withdrawal of an existing partner from a partnership while the remaining business may continue.

> Admission and retirement change the relationship among the partners.

They may require a new or revised partnership agreement, updated capital accounts, and careful treatment of the departing or incoming partner’s interest.

> Liquidation is more fundamental.

It does not merely change who owns the firm. It ends the firm’s business relationship and requires the assets and liabilities to be settled. Bangladesh’s Partnership Act defines dissolution of the firm as dissolution of partnership between all the partners.^2

^1: The Partnership Act, 1932, section 31, Bangladesh Laws.

^2: The Partnership Act, 1932, section 39, Bangladesh Laws.

## 19.2 Admitting a partner who buys an existing partner’s interest

> A private purchase changes capital ownership without necessarily changing the partnership’s total net assets.

> Suppose Nasrin buys one-third of Rafiq’s capital interest for Tk 120,000.

The payment is made by Nasrin directly to Rafiq. The partnership itself receives no cash. Economically, Nasrin has bought part of Rafiq’s ownership claim rather than invested new resources in the firm.

@ purchase of an interest | A transaction in which an incoming partner acquires all or part of an existing partner’s ownership interest directly from that partner.

> The partnership records the transfer between capital accounts.

If Rafiq’s capital account is reduced by Tk 120,000 and Nasrin’s capital account is credited by Tk 120,000, the total partners’ capital remains unchanged. The personal payment from Nasrin to Rafiq is not a cash receipt of the partnership.

> This is a common place for students to make an unnecessary cash entry.

They see Nasrin paying Tk 120,000 and debit the partnership’s Cash account. That is incorrect when the payment was made directly to Rafiq. The firm has not received the cash.

> The new partnership agreement must also state the new profit-sharing arrangement.

The transfer of capital does not automatically answer how future profit will be divided. That is a matter for the agreement and applicable law.

> In the simplest book treatment, the transfer is recorded as a capital-to-capital entry.

Debit Rafiq, Capital Tk 120,000. Credit Nasrin, Capital Tk 120,000. The total capital remains the same.

## 19.3 Admitting a partner who invests in the firm

> Direct investment changes both the partnership’s resources and its ownership claims.

> Now suppose Nasrin contributes Tk 150,000 directly to the partnership for a new capital interest.

The firm receives cash, so the partnership’s assets increase. Nasrin receives a capital credit under the terms of the new agreement.

@ investment in the firm | A contribution of cash or other assets by an incoming partner directly to the partnership.

> The basic entry is debit Cash and credit Nasrin, Capital.

If Nasrin contributes Tk 150,000, debit Cash Tk 150,000 and credit Nasrin, Capital Tk 150,000. Total partnership capital increases by Tk 150,000.

> The amount invested and the capital credit need not always be treated as a universal one-to-one rule.

Advanced partnership accounting may recognise bonuses or goodwill in some circumstances. This introductory book does not develop goodwill or bonus calculations as full topics, so the examples here use direct admission at the stated book value.

> The agreement should be rewritten or updated when the partnership changes.

It should state the new partner’s capital, profit-sharing ratio, responsibilities, and any special arrangements. The accounting records should follow that agreement.

## 19.4 What liquidation means, and why partnerships end

> Liquidation changes the accounting task from measuring ongoing profit to settling the firm’s remaining claims.

> A liquidating partnership sells or otherwise realises its assets and uses the resulting resources to settle obligations.

The process is different from an ordinary year-end closing. In ordinary operations, assets remain available for future business. In liquidation, assets are converted into cash or settled because the business is ending.

@ realisation | The process of converting partnership assets into cash or determining their settlement value during liquidation.

> A firm may be dissolved by agreement or under circumstances recognised by law.

The Bangladesh Partnership Act provides for dissolution by agreement and also recognises compulsory and contingent forms of dissolution.^3 The exact legal route matters in practice, but the accounting sequence remains focused on realisation, liabilities, and final partner claims.

> The first accounting question is what the assets actually produce.

If inventory with a book value of Tk 80,000 is sold for Tk 70,000, the firm has a realisation loss of Tk 10,000. That loss is allocated to the partners according to the agreed loss-sharing ratio.

> The second question is what must be paid.

Outside liabilities are settled before the remaining resources are distributed to partners. The partnership cannot distribute cash to owners while ignoring valid firm liabilities.

> The final question is whether every partner’s capital can be settled.

If the firm has enough cash after paying liabilities, capital balances can be paid. If a partner’s capital becomes deficient, the deficiency must be dealt with according to the partnership agreement and applicable law.

^3: The Partnership Act, 1932, sections 40–44, Bangladesh Laws.

## 19.5 The steps of liquidation: realise assets, pay debts, share the cash

> Liquidation is safest when every movement of value is recorded in a fixed sequence.

> Step one is to transfer the relevant non-cash assets to a realisation account.

Assets such as inventory, equipment, and receivables are moved into the liquidation process. Cash is normally kept separate because it is already in liquid form.

@ gain or loss on realisation | The difference between the book values of assets and liabilities involved in liquidation and the amounts actually realised or settled.

> Step two is to record the proceeds from assets realised.

If equipment with a book value of Tk 200,000 is sold for Tk 170,000, the realisation process records a Tk 30,000 loss. If receivables of Tk 100,000 produce Tk 92,000, the difference is another Tk 8,000 loss.

> Step three is to record liabilities paid.

Suppose creditors of Tk 120,000 are settled for Tk 118,000. The Tk 2,000 difference is a gain in the realisation process if the liability is legally settled at that amount.

> Step four is to transfer the net realisation gain or loss to the partners.

A gain increases the partners’ capital accounts. A loss decreases them. The sharing ratio used is the ratio applicable to the partnership at the time of liquidation, unless the agreement provides otherwise.

> Step five is to distribute the remaining cash.

After external liabilities and liquidation costs have been settled, the remaining cash is distributed according to the partners’ final capital claims, subject to any deficiency and legal requirements.

> The order matters because each step changes the amount available for the next step.

If cash is distributed before all assets are realised and liabilities are considered, the partners may receive too much and the final accounts may become impossible to settle.

### A complete liquidation case

> A small liquidation schedule shows how asset losses flow into the partners’ final cash claims.

> Situation: Rahman & Karim have cash Tk 80,000, other assets with book value Tk 420,000, and outside liabilities of Tk 180,000. Their capital balances are Rahman Tk 180,000 and Karim Tk 140,000, with a 3:2 profit-and-loss ratio.

The capital balances total Tk 320,000. The accounting equation is satisfied because assets total Tk 500,000 and liabilities plus capital total Tk 500,000.

> Question: What happens if the other assets are realised for Tk 390,000 and liabilities are settled for Tk 175,000?

The non-cash assets produce Tk 30,000 less than book value. The liabilities are settled Tk 5,000 below their recorded amount.

> Reasoning: calculate the net realisation result.

The asset loss is Tk 30,000 and the liability settlement gain is Tk 5,000. The net realisation loss is Tk 25,000.

> Reasoning: allocate the loss 3:2.

Rahman bears 3/5 of Tk 25,000, or Tk 15,000. Karim bears 2/5, or Tk 10,000. Their adjusted capital balances become Tk 165,000 and Tk 130,000.

> Reasoning: calculate the final cash.

Opening cash is Tk 80,000. Asset proceeds add Tk 390,000, giving Tk 470,000. Paying liabilities of Tk 175,000 leaves Tk 295,000. That equals the adjusted capital balances of Tk 165,000 plus Tk 130,000.

> Conclusion: the remaining Tk 295,000 is distributed as Tk 165,000 to Rahman and Tk 130,000 to Karim.

The distribution exactly settles the adjusted capital balances.

> Check: final cash and final capital must agree.

If the cash available does not equal the partners’ final capital claims after all liabilities and liquidation results are recorded, an item has been omitted or calculated incorrectly.

## 19.6 Gains, losses and the capital deficiency: the cash distribution schedule

> A capital deficiency appears when a partner’s final capital claim falls below zero after liquidation losses.

> A capital deficiency can arise when a partner’s share of liquidation losses exceeds the partner’s capital balance.

For example, if a partner has Tk 20,000 capital but is allocated Tk 35,000 of realisation losses, the partner’s capital becomes negative Tk 15,000. The accounting record now shows a deficiency rather than a payable capital balance.

@ capital deficiency | A negative capital balance arising when a partner’s allocated losses exceed the partner’s available capital claim.

> The cash distribution schedule helps prevent premature payments.

It tracks how much cash is available, which liabilities remain, which partners can safely receive cash, and how losses affect the final capital balances. The schedule is especially useful when assets are realised in stages.

> A staged liquidation requires caution.

Suppose a firm has Tk 300,000 cash today but expects another Tk 200,000 from asset sales. It cannot treat the entire expected amount as current cash. Distribution should follow the actual cash available and the agreed priorities.

> A partner with a deficiency may need further treatment under the partnership agreement and applicable law.

The accounting textbook can demonstrate the mechanics, but the legal recovery of a deficiency depends on the governing rules and facts. This chapter therefore focuses on the accounting schedule rather than giving legal advice.

## Worked case: Nasrin joins the bakery

> A private purchase and a direct investment can produce very different journal entries even when the cash amount is identical.

> Situation: On 1 January 2027, Nasrin Akter is admitted to the partnership by purchasing part of Rafiq’s interest for Tk 120,000 directly from Rafiq.

Assume Rafiq transfers a capital interest of Tk 120,000 to Nasrin and receives the payment personally. The partnership itself receives no cash.

> Question: What entry belongs in the partnership books?

Only the capital ownership recorded by the partnership changes. The private payment between Nasrin and Rafiq is not a partnership cash transaction.

> Reasoning: transfer the capital balance.

Debit Rafiq, Capital Tk 120,000. Credit Nasrin, Capital Tk 120,000. Total partnership capital is unchanged.

> Conclusion: the firm records a capital-to-capital transfer.

The new partner enters the ownership structure, but the firm does not receive the Tk 120,000.

> Check: compare this with a direct investment.

If Nasrin instead invested Tk 120,000 into the firm, the entry would debit Cash Tk 120,000 and credit Nasrin, Capital Tk 120,000. In that case, the firm’s assets and total capital would both increase.

## Summary

> Partnership changes require the accountant to identify who paid whom and whether the business itself received resources.

> Admission by purchase of an existing interest is a transfer between partners.

Admission by direct investment is a transaction with the firm. Liquidation is different again because the firm is ending and its assets, liabilities, and partner claims must be settled.

> The safest liquidation sequence is systematic.

Realise assets, record gains and losses, settle liabilities, determine final capital balances, and distribute remaining cash. A distribution schedule makes the changing cash position visible and reduces the risk of paying out too much too early.

## Where people go wrong

> Most errors arise from confusing personal transactions with firm transactions or distributing cash too early.

- Do not debit partnership cash when an incoming partner pays an existing partner personally.
- Do not treat admission by direct investment as a mere transfer between old and new capital accounts.
- Do not distribute cash before considering outstanding liabilities.
- Do not forget to allocate realisation gains or losses among partners.
- Do not assume a negative capital balance disappears without further treatment.
- Do not confuse dissolution of one partnership relationship with the legal and accounting process of complete firm liquidation.

## In history

> Hewlett-Packard provides a documented example of a partnership that later became a corporation.

Hewlett and Packard officially started their partnership in 1939. Hewlett-Packard’s own historical material records the partnership’s early work and later incorporation as a company in 1947.^4 The example helps separate two ideas: a partnership can change form, while the accounting problem at each stage still depends on the legal and economic event being recorded.
