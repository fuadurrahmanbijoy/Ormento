This chapter shows how the final statements are prepared from the adjusted figures, how revenue and expense accounts are brought to zero, and how a single test confirms that the books are ready for the next period. It ends with the whole accounting cycle, from the first transaction to the last entry.

## The Puzzle of the Second Month

> On 1 February the ledger still shows January's figures, and two of them mislead.

On the morning of 1 February 2026, Mitali Rahman opens her ledger. The Sales Revenue account shows Tk 212,000, the revenue of January. That evening she records a sale of Tk 5,000, and the account shows Tk 217,000. She now asks a simple question: how much did the bakery earn in February? The ledger cannot answer. She must remember the January figure and subtract it every time she looks.

> The owner's capital account shows Tk 500,000, but the balance sheet says Tk 607,000.

A second difficulty is hidden in the Owner's Capital account. It still shows Tk 500,000, the amount she invested on 1 January. Yet the balance sheet of 31 January says that her equity is Tk 607,000. The profit of Tk 127,000 and her drawings of Tk 20,000 appear in the statements. They do not yet appear in the account that is meant to hold her equity.

> The answer is to close the books: end the period tidily and begin the next one clean.

Both difficulties have one cure. At the end of each period the accountant **closes** the revenue and expense accounts, which means that their balances are moved to the owner's capital account and the accounts return to zero. This chapter teaches that cure in six steps. We first prepare the final statements (12.1). We then ask which accounts must be closed and why (12.2), perform the closing entries (12.3), test the result (12.4), and learn an optional entry for the new period (12.5). Last, we view the whole accounting cycle as one picture (12.6).

## 12.1 The Financial Statements from the Worksheet

> The worksheet holds all the figures; the statements present them to readers.

At the end of Chapter 11 the worksheet for January held every figure that the statements need. Its income statement columns show revenue and expenses. Its balance sheet columns show assets, liabilities, the owner's capital and drawings. The worksheet is a working paper, and nobody outside the accountant's office reads it. The statements are the formal reports that the owner, the bank and the tax authority read.

> Prepare the statements in a fixed order, because each one feeds the next.

The order is fixed by the way the statements connect (Chapter 5). The income statement comes first, because it gives the net profit. The statement of owner's equity comes second, because it needs the net profit to find the closing equity. The balance sheet comes third, because it needs the closing equity. Each statement carries a heading of three lines: the name of the business, the name of the statement, and the period or date.

> The income statement lists revenue, then each expense, and gives the profit as the difference.

We begin with the income statement. Revenue is Tk 212,000. Expenses are the five amounts in the adjusted trial balance: wages, rent, supplies used, utilities and depreciation. Net profit is revenue minus expenses.

## 12.2 Temporary and Permanent Accounts: Why Books Are Closed

> A taxi has a trip meter that is reset for each journey and an odometer that is never reset.

Consider a taxi. It has a trip meter and an odometer. The trip meter measures one journey and is set to zero before the next journey. The odometer measures the whole life of the car and is never set to zero. Accounts behave in the same two ways. Some accounts measure one period only. Others measure what the business holds at a moment and must keep their balance from one period to the next.

@ Temporary account | An account that measures one period only and is brought to zero at the period end.
@ Permanent account | An account that keeps its balance from one period into the next.

> Revenue, expense and drawings accounts are temporary; asset, liability and capital accounts are permanent.

An account that measures one period only is a **temporary account**. Revenue accounts, expense accounts and the drawings account are temporary. An account that keeps its balance into the next period is a **permanent account**. Asset accounts, liability accounts and the owner's capital account are permanent.^1 The Income Summary account, which we meet in the next section, is temporary: it exists for a moment during closing and always ends at zero.

^1: Some books call temporary accounts *nominal accounts* and permanent accounts *real accounts*. This book uses *temporary* and *permanent* throughout.

:table The six kinds of account and their type
| Kind of account | Example | Type |
| Asset | Cash, Equipment | Permanent |
| Liability | Accounts Payable | Permanent |
| Owner's capital | Owner's Capital | Permanent |
| Drawings | Owner's Drawings | Temporary |
| Revenue | Sales Revenue | Temporary |
| Expense | Rent Expense | Temporary |

> The table shows each kind of account and its type; the contra account follows its parent.

The table needs two comments. First, the drawings account belongs to equity, yet it is temporary, because drawings are measured for one period. Second, a contra account takes the type of the account it reduces. Accumulated Depreciation: Equipment reduces an asset, so it is permanent and is never closed. It must keep growing for as long as the equipment is used.

> The income statement covers one period, so its accounts must start each period at zero.

Why must temporary accounts be closed? The income statement reports the revenue and expenses of one period. If the revenue account still held January's amount in February, the February income statement would show January and February together. Closing returns each temporary account to zero, and the new period begins with a clean record. This is the answer to the first half of the puzzle.

> Closing also moves profit and drawings into capital, which answers the second half of the puzzle.

Closing also repairs the capital account. During the period, profit and drawings are held in temporary accounts, so the Owner's Capital account does not show the owner's true equity. Closing moves the net profit and the drawings into Owner's Capital. After closing, the account shows Tk 607,000, and the balance sheet agrees with the ledger.

> Before moving on: temporary accounts measure one period and are closed; permanent accounts continue.

*Before moving on.* Temporary accounts (revenue, expenses, drawings) measure one period and are closed. Permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, capital) continue into the next period. You should now be able to say which kind of account the trip meter resembles, and why.

## 12.3 Closing Entries Step by Step

> Closing entries are journal entries; each brings a temporary account to zero.

**Closing entries** are journal entries made at the end of a period. Each closing entry brings a temporary account to zero by entering its balance on the opposite side. They are journalised and posted in the same way as every other entry (Chapter 7). The rule is short: to close a credit balance, debit the account; to close a debit balance, credit the account.

@ Closing entries | Journal entries at period end that bring temporary accounts to zero.
@ Income summary | A temporary account that gathers revenue and expenses before the profit moves to capital.

> The income summary account collects revenue and expenses, so that the profit appears in one place.

We could move every revenue and expense balance directly to Owner's Capital. We use a middle account instead, called the **income summary**. It is a temporary account that gathers all revenue and all expenses. Its balance, after both have been moved, is the net profit or loss. It gives the accountant a place to see the result, and a check against the income statement.^2

^2: In some countries this account is called the *profit and loss account*. The income statement is the report; the Income Summary is the ledger account that proves it.

> Closing takes four steps, shown in Fig. 1: revenue, expenses, the income summary, and drawings.

There are four steps. Step 1 closes the revenue accounts to the Income Summary. Step 2 closes the expense accounts to the Income Summary. Step 3 closes the Income Summary to the Owner's Capital. Step 4 closes the drawings account to the Owner's Capital. Drawings do not pass through the Income Summary, because drawings are not an expense. They are a withdrawal by the owner.

:fig The four closing entries for January 2026 | fig12_closing.svg

> The first example uses the bakery's January figures, laid out in the standard order.

**Given.** The adjusted balances of the temporary accounts at 31 January 2026: Sales Revenue Tk 212,000 (credit); Wages Expense 40,000, Rent Expense 15,000, Supplies Expense 22,000, Utilities Expense 6,000, Depreciation Expense 2,000 (all debit); Owner's Drawings 20,000 (debit); Owner's Capital 500,000 (credit).

**Find.** The four closing entries, and the balance of Owner's Capital after closing.

**Formula.** To close an account, enter its balance on the opposite side. Net profit = revenue − expenses.

> Steps 1 and 2 move revenue and expenses into the income summary.

**Steps.** Step 1: the revenue account has a credit balance, so we debit it and credit the Income Summary. Step 2: each expense account has a debit balance, so we credit each one, and debit the Income Summary once for the total.

| Date | Account | Debit | Credit |

| | Supplies Expense | | 22,000 |

| 31 Jan | Owner's Capital | 20,000 | |

> After step 2 the income summary shows the profit as a credit balance; step 3 moves it to capital.

After step 2 the Income Summary has a credit of Tk 212,000 and a debit of Tk 85,000. The difference is a credit balance of Tk 127,000. This equals the net profit on the income statement. Step 3 debits the Income Summary for Tk 127,000, which brings it to zero, and credits Owner's Capital for the same amount. Step 4 reduces Owner's Capital by the drawings of Tk 20,000.

**Answer.** The Owner's Capital account after closing is Tk 500,000 + 127,000 − 20,000 = Tk 607,000.

**Check.** Every revenue, expense and drawings account now has a zero balance. The Income Summary has a zero balance. The capital of Tk 607,000 equals the closing equity in 12.1.

| Stage | Debit | Credit | Balance |

> Closing never touches asset, liability or contra accounts; their balances are carried forward unchanged.

The permanent accounts take no part. Cash stays at Tk 400,000, and Accumulated Depreciation stays at Tk 2,000. The closing entries change only the temporary accounts and the Owner's Capital account.

> The second example shows a loss, and a drawings balance, in a business with new data.

*A second example: a loss.* Rahim Tailoring (illustrative) ends its first quarter with revenue of Tk 30,000, expenses of Tk 38,000 and drawings of Tk 5,000. Its capital before closing is Tk 100,000. Step 1 debits Sales Revenue 30,000 and credits Income Summary 30,000. Step 2 debits Income Summary 38,000 and credits the expense accounts 38,000. The Income Summary now has a debit balance of Tk 8,000, which is the net loss. Step 3 reverses the direction: debit Owner's Capital 8,000 and credit Income Summary 8,000. Step 4 debits Owner's Capital 5,000 and credits Owner's Drawings 5,000.

> The capital falls by the loss and by the drawings, from 100,000 to 87,000.

The capital after closing is Tk 100,000 − 8,000 − 5,000 = Tk 87,000. Notice the difference from the profit case: a profit increases capital, so the Income Summary is credited in step 1 more than it is debited in step 2; a loss decreases capital, so the order of balances is reversed.

> Four common slips: closing drawings through the income summary, forgetting step 4, closing a permanent account, and closing to the wrong side.

*Common slips.* (1) Drawings are closed to the Income Summary. This is wrong: drawings are not an expense, and the profit would be understated. (2) Step 4 is forgotten, and the capital is overstated by the drawings. (3) A permanent account, such as Accumulated Depreciation, is closed by mistake. (4) A balance is entered on its own side instead of the opposite side, which doubles the balance instead of removing it.

> Before moving on: four closing entries; the income summary shows the profit; capital absorbs profit and drawings.

*Before moving on.* There are four closing entries. The Income Summary shows the profit or loss, and then it is closed to capital. Drawings are closed directly to capital. You should now be able to say, for any temporary account, on which side the closing entry is made.

## 12.4 The Post-Closing Trial Balance

> After closing, a final test confirms that only permanent accounts remain, in balance.

After the closing entries have been posted, the accountant prepares one more list of balances. The **post-closing trial balance** is a trial balance of the permanent accounts only, prepared after the closing entries. It has two purposes. It proves that total debits still equal total credits. It also proves that no temporary account still holds a balance, so that the new period can begin clean.

@ Post-closing trial balance | A trial balance of permanent accounts only, prepared after the closing entries.

> The bakery's post-closing trial balance has ten permanent accounts, each carried into February.

For the bakery, only ten accounts remain with balances: five assets, one contra account, and four credit accounts. These are the opening balances of 1 February 2026.

| Accounts Receivable | 25,000 | |
| Supplies | 8,000 | |
| Prepaid Rent | 30,000 | |
| Equipment | 240,000 | |
| Accumulated Depreciation: Equipment | | 2,000 |

> The trial balance totals Tk 703,000 while total assets are Tk 701,000, and the reason is the contra account.

A careful reader will notice that the totals are Tk 703,000, while the balance sheet shows total assets of Tk 701,000. The difference of Tk 2,000 is not an error. The trial balance lists the accumulated depreciation as a credit, and the balance sheet deducts it from the equipment. A trial balance is a test of the ledger; the balance sheet is a report (Chapter 8). If the post-closing trial balance does not balance, or if it lists a revenue or expense account, the closing entries contain a mistake and must be corrected before the new period begins.

> Before moving on: the post-closing trial balance holds only permanent accounts and gives the opening balances.

*Before moving on.* The post-closing trial balance lists permanent accounts only. Its balances are the opening balances of the next period.

## 12.5 Reversing Entries (Advanced)

@! Advanced material. A reader may defer this section; nothing later in the chapter depends on it, and Chapter 13 explains each entry again where it is used.

> A payment on 1 February that includes January wages tempts the bookkeeper to split it by hand.

Recall adjustment A5 of Chapter 9. Wages of Tk 4,000 for 29 to 31 January were unpaid, so the bakery recorded Wages Expense and Wages Payable of Tk 4,000. On 27 February the bakery pays the whole month's wages of Tk 36,000. Part of this payment, Tk 4,000, settles the liability recorded in January. The bookkeeper must remember to split the payment: debit Wages Payable 4,000 and Wages Expense 32,000. A tired bookkeeper may forget the January accrual and debit Wages Expense 36,000, which overstates February's expense.

@ Reversing entry | An optional entry on the first day of a period that cancels an earlier accrual.

> A reversing entry cancels the accrual on the first day, so the later payment can be recorded in the usual way.

A **reversing entry** is an optional entry made on the first day of the new period. It is the exact opposite of an earlier adjusting entry for an accrual. On 1 February the bakery debits Wages Payable 4,000 and credits Wages Expense 4,000. The Wages Payable account is now zero, and Wages Expense has a credit balance of Tk 4,000. When the bakery pays Tk 36,000 on 27 February, it records the usual entry: debit Wages Expense 36,000 and credit Cash 36,000. The expense account now shows Tk 36,000 − 4,000 = Tk 32,000, which is the true February expense for the days from 1 to 28 February.

:table Paying Tk 36,000 of wages on 27 February, with and without a reversing entry (Tk)
| | Without reversing entry | With reversing entry |
| 1 Feb | No entry | Dr Wages Payable 4,000; Cr Wages Expense 4,000 |
| 27 Feb | Dr Wages Payable 4,000; Dr Wages Expense 32,000; Cr Cash 36,000 | Dr Wages Expense 36,000; Cr Cash 36,000 |
| Wages Expense for February | 32,000 | 32,000 |

> The accrued revenue of Tk 10,000 is reversed in the same way, and both methods give the same result.

The accrued revenue (adjustment A6) is treated in the same way. On 1 February the bakery debits Sales Revenue 10,000 and credits Accounts Receivable 10,000. When the hotel pays Tk 10,000 later in February, the bakery debits Cash and credits Sales Revenue 10,000. The two entries cancel in the Sales Revenue account, so February shows no revenue from a job that belongs to January. Without the reversal, the same receipt would be credited to Accounts Receivable instead. Both methods give the same figures in the statements. The reversing entry changes only the work of the bookkeeper.

> Only accruals are normally reversed; depreciation and prepaid expenses are not.

Not every adjustment is reversed. An adjusting entry for depreciation is not reversed, because the accumulated depreciation must continue to grow. A prepaid expense, such as the Tk 30,000 of prepaid rent, is not reversed in this book, because the later payment is recorded in the usual way and no cash is expected for it. Reversing entries are optional. A business that uses them must apply them to every accrual, so that the books remain consistent.

> Before moving on: a reversing entry mirrors an accrual on the first day, and changes the routine, not the result.

*Before moving on.* A reversing entry is the opposite of an accrual, made on the first day of the new period. It simplifies the next payment or receipt and does not change the profit.

## 12.6 The Whole Accounting Cycle

> Every step of the cycle supports the next, and together they turn transactions into statements.

Since Chapter 7 we have learned the steps of the accounting cycle one at a time. We now place them in order. The **accounting cycle** is the set of steps that turns a business's transactions into financial statements. It repeats in every period. Fig. 2 shows the ten steps in a loop.

:fig The ten steps of the accounting cycle | fig12_cycle.svg

> Steps 1 to 4 record and test; steps 5 and 6 update; step 7 reports; steps 8 to 10 close and prepare.

We may group the ten steps into four jobs. In steps 1 to 4 the accountant records and tests: the transactions are analysed, journalised and posted, and an unadjusted trial balance is prepared. In steps 5 and 6 the accounts are brought up to date by adjusting entries, and an adjusted trial balance is prepared. In step 7 the business reports, through the statements (with a worksheet as a help, if the accountant wishes). In steps 8 to 10 the books are closed, tested by the post-closing trial balance, and, if the business wishes, prepared by reversing entries.

> Each step has a purpose, and most steps include a check that finds mistakes early.

The steps are linked by checks. Each check catches a different kind of mistake, and the later checks rely on the earlier ones.

:table Each step of the cycle and what it checks
| Step | What it does | What it checks or proves |
| 1 Analyse | Decides which accounts change | Every transaction has two sides |
| 2 Journalise | Records in date order | A complete record of first entry |
| 3 Post | Copies to the ledger | Each account shows a balance |
| 4 Unadjusted trial balance | Lists all balances | Debits equal credits |
| 5 Adjust | Brings accounts up to date | Revenue and expenses are in the right period |
| 6 Adjusted trial balance | Lists the new balances | Debits still equal credits after the adjustments |
| 7 Statements | Reports results and position | Profit agrees with the equity change; the balance sheet balances |
| 8 Close | Zeroes temporary accounts | Profit and drawings reach capital |
| 9 Post-closing trial balance | Lists permanent accounts | Books are ready for the next period |
| 10 Reverse | Cancels accruals (optional) | Simplifies the next period's entries |

> Three trial balances appear in the cycle, each with a different total and a different job.

The cycle contains three lists of balances, and a reader may confuse them. For the bakery in January, the totals are these. The unadjusted trial balance (step 4) totals Tk 792,000. The adjusted trial balance (step 6) totals Tk 808,000. The post-closing trial balance (step 9) totals Tk 703,000. The adjusted total is larger by Tk 16,000, and the reason is that the adjustments added new accounts or enlarged balances on both sides: Tk 2,000 for depreciation, Tk 4,000 for accrued wages, and Tk 10,000 for accrued revenue. The post-closing total is smaller, because the temporary accounts have gone.

:table The three trial balances of the bakery, January 2026
| List | Step | Total (Tk) | Contains |
| Unadjusted trial balance | 4 | 792,000 | All accounts, before adjusting |
| Adjusted trial balance | 6 | 808,000 | All accounts, after adjusting |
| Post-closing trial balance | 9 | 703,000 | Permanent accounts only |

> A complete example for a new business follows the cycle from first transaction to post-closing trial balance.

*A full cycle for a new business.* Nadia Tutoring (illustrative) begins on 1 February. We follow its first month through the cycle, using round numbers. The transactions are: Nadia invests Tk 50,000 cash; she buys equipment for Tk 20,000 cash; she receives tuition fees of Tk 40,000 in cash; she pays rent of Tk 10,000; she takes Tk 5,000 as drawings. At the end of the month, wages of Tk 3,000 are unpaid, and depreciation of Tk 1,000 is charged.

> Steps 1 to 4 give an unadjusted trial balance of Tk 90,000, which agrees on both sides.

After steps 1 to 3 the ledger balances are: Cash Tk 55,000 (50,000 − 20,000 + 40,000 − 10,000 − 5,000); Equipment 20,000; Owner's Capital 50,000; Owner's Drawings 5,000; Fees Revenue 40,000; Rent Expense 10,000. Step 4 lists them. Debits are 55,000 + 20,000 + 5,000 + 10,000 = Tk 90,000. Credits are 50,000 + 40,000 = Tk 90,000.

> Steps 5 and 6 add two expenses and two matching credits, and the totals rise to Tk 94,000.

Step 5 records two adjusting entries: debit Wages Expense 3,000 and credit Wages Payable 3,000; debit Depreciation Expense 1,000 and credit Accumulated Depreciation 1,000. The adjusted trial balance (step 6) has debits of Tk 94,000 (90,000 + 3,000 + 1,000) and credits of Tk 94,000.

> Step 7 gives a profit of Tk 26,000 and a closing capital of Tk 71,000; steps 8 and 9 confirm it.

In step 7, revenue is Tk 40,000 and expenses are Tk 14,000 (rent 10,000, wages 3,000, depreciation 1,000), so the net profit is Tk 26,000. The closing capital is Tk 50,000 + 26,000 − 5,000 = Tk 71,000. Total assets are 55,000 + 20,000 − 1,000 = Tk 74,000, and liabilities (wages payable) are Tk 3,000, so 3,000 + 71,000 = 74,000. In step 8, the four closing entries bring Fees Revenue, the three expenses and Drawings to zero and leave Owner's Capital at Tk 71,000. In step 9, the post-closing trial balance has debits of Tk 75,000 (cash 55,000 and equipment 20,000) and credits of Tk 75,000 (accumulated depreciation 1,000, wages payable 3,000 and capital 71,000).

> Three views of one result: the cycle, the checks and the totals all tell the same story.

We can now state the central idea of the chapter. A single set of transactions passes through ten steps, and each step is tested. The trial balances test the ledger. The statements present the result. The closing entries prepare the next period. A mistake that escapes one test is likely to be caught by the next. A student who understands the cycle understands how accounting information is made.

:box IN SHORT
The accounting cycle begins with a transaction and ends, ten steps later, with a post-closing trial balance that is ready for the next period. Closing moves profit and drawings to capital and leaves every temporary account at zero.

> Before moving on: the cycle in order, the three trial balances and what each proves.

*Before moving on.* You should now be able to list the ten steps of the cycle in order, say which trial balance belongs to which step, and explain why the totals differ.

## Summary

> The chapter summarised: statements, closing, testing, reversing, and the cycle.

The final statements are prepared from the adjusted figures, in the order income statement, statement of owner's equity, balance sheet. Temporary accounts (revenue, expenses, drawings) measure one period and are closed; permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, capital) continue. The four closing entries move revenue and expenses to the Income Summary, the profit or loss to capital, and the drawings to capital. The post-closing trial balance of permanent accounts proves that the books are ready. A reversing entry, which is optional, cancels an accrual on the first day of the new period. The accounting cycle has ten steps, and the checks within it catch mistakes early.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five common mistakes about closing, each stated and corrected.

*"Closing removes the records."* It does not. The journal and the ledger entries remain as evidence; only the balances of the temporary accounts are moved. *"Drawings are an expense, so they go to the Income Summary."* They do not: drawings are a withdrawal by the owner and are closed to capital. *"The post-closing trial balance is a statement."* It is a test, and nobody outside the business needs it. *"Reversing entries change the profit."* They do not: they change only how the later payment is recorded. *"A balance sheet needs the closing entries first."* It does not: the statements are prepared from the adjusted figures, and closing follows.

## In History

> Public companies came to publish audited statements only after a financial crisis led lawmakers to require them.

In the United States, after the stock market collapse of 1929, Congress passed the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. The first required companies that offered securities to the public to provide financial statements examined by independent accountants. The second created a regulator, the Securities and Exchange Commission, and required listed companies to report regularly. These laws made a regular, standard cycle of statements a legal duty rather than a choice.^3 The cycle of this chapter, repeated every period, is the means by which a company meets such a duty.

^3: This account is general and is given for background; it concerns the United States, not Bangladesh. The reporting rules in force in Bangladesh are the subject of Chapter 3.
