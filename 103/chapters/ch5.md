:chapter V | The Financial Statements
:mood How a month of figures becomes three pages that a stranger can read
:desc A business holds its facts in a table of transactions, but no outsider can read a table of thirteen lines and understand a month. This chapter shows the reports that a business prepares, builds the first set from the bakery's January figures, and shows how the three reports are linked. You will need Chapter 4: the accounting equation, revenue, expense, drawings and the summary of transactions.

> At the end of January, Mitali holds Tk 400,000 in cash and cannot say whether she did well.

On the evening of 31 January 2026, Mitali Rahman counts the cash in her bakery's account: Tk 400,000. On 1 January she put Tk 500,000 into the business. The cash is therefore Tk 100,000 lower than at the start. Did the bakery lose money in its first month? A friendly bank officer, who may one day lend to her, asks the question in a different form: "How did the first month go?" Mitali has a table of 13 transactions from Chapter 4. Every fact that the officer needs lies somewhere in that table, but the table does not answer him. It lists events in the order in which they happened. The officer wants results, grouped by meaning.

> The answer needs reports that group the same facts by the question each reader asks.

The cash has fallen because Mitali spent money on an oven, on rent for three months and on supplies, and these purchases are not losses. They are things that the bakery now owns. To see this, the officer needs three reports: one on what the bakery earned and spent, one on what happened to the owner's share, and one on what the bakery owns and owes. These reports are called the financial statements. This chapter teaches each one, builds them from the January table, and shows how they fit together.

## 5.1 The reports an owner needs: the types of financial statement

> A financial statement is a formal report that shows results or position in numbers.

@ Financial statement | A formal report that shows the results or position of a business in numbers.

A **financial statement** is a formal report that shows the results or the position of a business in numbers. It has a fixed layout so that every reader knows where to look. Every financial statement carries a heading of three lines: the name of the business, the name of the statement, and the date or the period to which it refers. A statement without its heading is not usable, because a figure has no meaning unless the reader knows which business and which time it describes.

> Each statement answers one question: how did the business perform, how did the owner's share change, what is the position?

@ Income statement | A statement that shows revenue, expenses, and the resulting profit or loss for a period.
@ Statement of owner's equity | A statement that shows how the owner's equity changed during a period.
@ Balance sheet | A statement that lists assets, liabilities, and owner's equity on one date.
Three questions come up most often, and each has its own statement. How did the business perform in the period? The **income statement** answers this. How did the owner's claim on the business change in the period? The **statement of owner's equity** answers this. What does the business own and owe on one date? The **balance sheet** answers this. The names differ in some countries and some books. The income statement is also called the profit and loss account, and the balance sheet is also called the statement of financial position.^1

^1: The international standards of Chapter 3 use the names *statement of profit or loss* and *statement of financial position*. This book uses *income statement* and *balance sheet*, which are the names most students meet in their first course.

> Two statements describe a stretch of time, and one describes a single day.

@ Reporting date | The date to which a balance sheet is prepared.
An important difference separates the three. The income statement and the statement of owner's equity describe a **period**: a month, a quarter or a year. They are like a film of what happened between two dates. The balance sheet describes one **date**. It is like a photograph of the business on that day. For this reason the heading of the first two says *For the month ended 31 January 2026*, and the heading of the balance sheet says *At 31 January 2026*. The date on a balance sheet is the **reporting date**.

:fig Two statements film a period; one photographs a date. | ch5_fig1_period_date.svg

> A complete set also includes a cash flow statement and notes, which this book describes only.

@ Statement of cash flows | A statement that shows the cash a business received and paid during a period.

@ Notes to the accounts | Written explanations that accompany the financial statements.

A full set of statements for a company has two more parts. The **statement of cash flows** shows the cash that the business received and paid in the period, grouped by purpose. The **notes to the accounts** are written explanations that accompany the statements: the methods used, details of large items, and facts that numbers alone do not show. Both are important. A business can make a profit and still run short of cash, and the cash flow statement shows why. This book describes these two parts but does not teach how to prepare them. The three statements of this chapter are the foundation, and a student who masters them can learn the others with little difficulty.

> A sole trader needs the three statements; the legal duties of a company are wider.

Which statements must a business prepare? The answer depends on its form (Chapter 1). A company must prepare the complete set under the accounting standards in force; the rules for companies in Bangladesh are developed with the company form in Chapter 20. A sole trader such as Mitali has no such duty to outsiders, but the bank, the tax authority and the owner herself all need the income statement and the balance sheet. The statement of owner's equity connects the two. We therefore prepare all three, and we use them for every business in this book.

> Recap: the three types of statement, and the two further parts of a full set.
*Before moving on.* A financial statement is a formal report with a three-line heading. The income statement and the statement of owner's equity cover a period; the balance sheet shows one date. The statement of cash flows and the notes complete a full set.

## 5.2 The income statement

> The income statement subtracts expenses from revenue to find the profit of a period.

The income statement answers a plain question: after the business had earned its revenue and paid or used up its costs, what was left? The rule is short.

:box IN SHORT
Net profit = revenue − expenses. If expenses are more than revenue, the result is a net loss.

@ Net profit | The profit left after all expenses have been taken from revenue.

@ Net loss | The loss that results when expenses are more than revenue.

> Net profit is the profit left after all expenses; net loss is the shortfall when expenses are larger.

**Net profit** is the profit that remains after all expenses have been taken away from revenue. When expenses are larger than revenue, the result is a **net loss**. We use the word *net* to show that every expense has already been taken away. Chapter 4 defined revenue and expense. The first is the money earned from selling goods or services, and the second is a cost that the business uses up to earn it. Note that revenue is not the same as cash received: a sale on account is revenue on the day of the sale, even though the cash arrives later.

> The layout lists revenue first, then each expense, then the result.

The layout is always the same. Under the three-line heading, revenue comes first. Below it, each expense is listed on its own line, and the expenses are totalled. The last line shows the net profit or net loss. We begin with the smallest example that has all of these parts.

> The first example is a cycle repair shop, with six transactions in one month.

*Given.* Shuvo Cycle Repair (illustrative) begins on 1 April 2026. In the month: (1) Shuvo invests Tk 50,000 in cash; (2) he buys tools for Tk 12,000, paying Tk 7,000 in cash and owing Tk 5,000 on account; (3) he receives Tk 30,000 in cash for repairs; (4) he pays rent of Tk 8,000; (5) he pays wages of Tk 6,000; (6) he withdraws Tk 4,000 for himself.
*Find.* The net profit for April.
*Formula.* In words: net profit = revenue − expenses. In symbols: P = R − E.
*Steps.* Only transactions 3, 4 and 5 concern revenue and expenses. The investment is not revenue, because it is the owner's own money. The tools are not an expense this month, because the shop still owns them. The withdrawal is not an expense, because it is the owner taking money for personal use. Revenue is Tk 30,000. Expenses are 8,000 + 6,000 = Tk 14,000. Net profit is 30,000 − 14,000 = Tk 16,000.
*Answer.* The net profit for April is Tk 16,000.
*Check.* The shop took in Tk 30,000 and paid out Tk 14,000 for running costs. The difference is Tk 16,000.

:table Shuvo Cycle Repair: Income Statement for the month ended 30 April 2026 (Tk)
| Item | Tk | Tk |
| Revenue: repair fees | | 30,000 |
| Expenses | | |
| Rent | 8,000 | |
| Wages | 6,000 | |
| Total expenses | | 14,000 |
| Net profit | | 16,000 |

> Three items often cause doubt: the owner's investment, an asset bought, and the owner's withdrawals.

Three kinds of cash movement are not part of the income statement. The owner's investment is not revenue. An asset bought is not an expense, because the business still owns it. The owner's drawings are not an expense, because they are a share of the profit taken out, and not a cost of earning it. A reader who keeps these three in mind will avoid the most frequent error of beginners.

@! Go slowly here: only revenue and expenses belong in this statement.

> Now the bakery's January: revenue and expenses are picked out of the 13 transactions.

We now build the bakery's income statement from the 13 transactions of Chapter 4. We take each transaction and ask whether it is revenue or expense. Transactions 5, 6 and 11 are revenue: baked goods sold for cash on 10 January (Tk 90,000), catering for a school billed on account on 15 January (Tk 40,000), and baked goods sold for cash on 28 January (Tk 70,000). Total revenue is 90,000 + 40,000 + 70,000 = Tk 200,000. Transaction 6 is revenue even though only Tk 25,000 of the Tk 40,000 had arrived by month's end. The other Tk 15,000 is a claim on the school.

> Two transactions are expenses: the electricity bill and the wages.

Two transactions are expenses: the electricity bill of Tk 6,000 (transaction 10) and the wages of Tk 36,000 (transaction 12). Total expenses are Tk 42,000. The remaining transactions do not belong here. Transactions 1 and 13 concern the owner. Transactions 2, 3, 4, 7, 8 and 9 exchange one asset or liability for another, or concern a payment in advance, and they change the position of the business, not its profit.

| Item | Tk | Tk |
| Revenue: sales revenue | | 200,000 |
| Expenses | | |
| Wages expense | 36,000 | |

> The unadjusted January profit is Tk 158,000, and Chapter 9 will correct it.
:case Case file, illustrative. The unadjusted income statement of Mitali Bakery for January 2026 shows revenue of Tk 200,000, expenses of Tk 42,000 and net profit of Tk 158,000. The rent paid on 3 January (Tk 45,000 for three months) and the supplies bought on 5 January (Tk 30,000) are not yet in the expenses, because they have not yet been used up in the way that Chapter 9 describes. For this reason the net profit of Tk 158,000 is too high, and Chapter 9 corrects it.

> A net loss appears when the expenses are larger, and the layout changes only at the last line.

A loss has the same layout. Suppose a stall earns revenue of Tk 20,000 in a month and has expenses of Tk 26,000. The last line reads *Net loss* and shows 20,000 − 26,000 = −Tk 6,000, written as (6,000) in the column. The brackets are the usual sign that a figure is negative.

> Recap: the layout of the income statement and what stays out of it.
*Before moving on.* The income statement lists revenue, then each expense, then net profit or net loss. Investments, assets bought and drawings stay out of it.

## 5.3 The statement of owner's equity

> This statement shows how the owner's claim moved from the start of the period to its end.

Chapter 4 showed that the owner's equity changes in four ways: the owner invests, the business makes a profit or a loss, and the owner takes drawings. The statement of owner's equity collects all of these in one short list. It begins with the equity at the start of the period, adds the investment and the net profit, subtracts the drawings, and arrives at the equity at the end of the period.

:box IN SHORT
Closing equity = opening equity + investment + net profit − drawings. A net loss is subtracted instead of added.

> The net profit comes from the income statement, so that statement must be prepared first.

The net profit does not come from the transactions directly. It is carried from the income statement. For this reason the order of preparation is fixed: income statement first, equity statement second, balance sheet third. The cycle shop shows the method.

> The equity example is laid out as given, find, formula, steps, answer, check.
*Given.* Shuvo Cycle Repair starts with nothing on 1 April, invests Tk 50,000, earns a net profit of Tk 16,000 and withdraws Tk 4,000.
*Find.* The owner's equity at 30 April.
*Formula.* Closing equity = opening equity + investment + net profit − drawings.
*Steps.* 0 + 50,000 = 50,000. Then 50,000 + 16,000 = 66,000. Then 66,000 − 4,000 = 62,000.
*Answer.* The owner's equity at 30 April is Tk 62,000.
*Check.* The shop owns cash of Tk 55,000 and tools of Tk 12,000, which together are Tk 67,000. It owes Tk 5,000. The difference, Tk 62,000, agrees.

:table Shuvo Cycle Repair: Statement of Owner's Equity for the month ended 30 April 2026 (Tk)
| Item | Tk |
| Owner's equity at 1 April | 0 |
| Add: investment by the owner | 50,000 |
| Add: net profit for April | 16,000 |
| Less: drawings | (4,000) |
| Owner's equity at 30 April | 62,000 |

> The bakery's equity grows from nil to Tk 638,000 in January.
:case Case file, illustrative. The statement of owner's equity of Mitali Bakery for January 2026 starts with equity of nil on 1 January, because the business began on that day. It then adds Mitali's investment of Tk 500,000 and the net profit of Tk 158,000, giving Tk 658,000, and subtracts her drawings of Tk 20,000. Closing owner's equity at 31 January is Tk 638,000.

:table Mitali Bakery: Statement of Owner's Equity for the month ended 31 January 2026 (Tk)
| Item | Tk |
| Owner's equity at 1 January | 0 |
| Add: investment by the owner | 500,000 |
| Add: net profit for January | 158,000 |
| Subtotal | 658,000 |
| Less: drawings | (20,000) |
| Owner's equity at 31 January | 638,000 |

> The statement is read from the top, and each line is the result of the line above plus a change.

In later months the statement begins with the equity that the previous month ended with. The bakery's second month will therefore start with the corrected closing figure of January. In a month with a net loss, the loss is subtracted at the same place where the profit would have been added.

> Recap: the line-by-line method of the equity statement.
*Before moving on.* The statement of owner's equity starts with opening equity, adds investment and profit, subtracts drawings, and ends with closing equity. The profit comes from the income statement.

## 5.4 The balance sheet

> The balance sheet lists what the business owns, what it owes, and the owner's claim, on one date.

The balance sheet is a photograph of the accounting equation of Chapter 4: assets = liabilities + owner's equity. It lists every asset, every liability and the owner's equity at the reporting date. Because the equation always holds, the two sides of the sheet always agree. If they do not agree, a mistake has been made in the preparation.

> Two layouts exist, and both show the same figures.

@ Account form | A balance sheet layout with assets on one side and liabilities and equity on the other.
@ Report form | A balance sheet layout that lists assets, then liabilities, then equity, down the page.
Two layouts are in use. In the **account form**, assets are listed on the left side of the page, and liabilities and owner's equity on the right side. In the **report form**, the sheet runs down the page: assets first, then liabilities, then owner's equity. The account form shows the equation at a glance. The report form is easier to fit on a narrow page and is more common in printed reports.

:fig The two layouts of the balance sheet show the same figures in a different arrangement. | ch5_fig2_forms.svg

> Assets are listed in a usual order, with the most easily turned into cash first.

Within each group, items follow a usual order. Assets begin with the one that is closest to cash. Cash comes first, followed by accounts receivable, supplies, prepaid items and, last, long-lived assets such as equipment. Liabilities are listed with the one that is due earliest first. Chapter 13 develops this idea into classified statements. Here the order is only a convenience.

> The cycle shop's balance sheet is built from the same six transactions.

:table Shuvo Cycle Repair: Balance Sheet at 30 April 2026, account form (Tk)
| Assets | Tk | Liabilities and owner's equity | Tk |
| Cash | 55,000 | Accounts payable | 5,000 |
| Tools | 12,000 | Owner's equity | 62,000 |
| Total assets | 67,000 | Total liabilities and owner's equity | 67,000 |

> The bakery's balance sheet draws on the transactions and on the closing equity just found.

@! Go slowly here: the owner's equity on the sheet is the closing figure from the previous statement.

Now we build the bakery's balance sheet at 31 January 2026. For each asset and liability we use the balance that the 13 transactions leave. Cash has a balance of Tk 400,000, accounts receivable Tk 15,000 (the school still owes Tk 40,000 less the Tk 25,000 received), supplies Tk 30,000, prepaid rent Tk 45,000 and equipment Tk 240,000. The accounts payable is Tk 140,000 less the Tk 60,000 paid, which is Tk 80,000. A further liability is the Tk 12,000 received in advance for six cake deliveries. The bakery has not yet delivered the cakes, so it owes them. This is called unearned revenue (Chapter 9). The owner's equity is the Tk 638,000 found in 5.3.

> The bakery's equation holds: Tk 730,000 of assets against Tk 92,000 plus Tk 638,000.
:case Case file, illustrative. At 31 January 2026 the unadjusted balance sheet of Mitali Bakery shows total assets of Tk 730,000, total liabilities of Tk 92,000 and owner's equity of Tk 638,000. The sum of the last two figures is Tk 730,000, equal to the assets, so the equation holds. The bakery's cash of Tk 400,000 is Tk 100,000 below the owner's investment, yet the bakery is worth more than that investment: its equity is Tk 638,000.

> The opening puzzle is solved: cash fell because cash was turned into assets and not lost.

The opening puzzle is now solved. The cash fell from Tk 500,000 to Tk 400,000, but most of the money was spent on an oven and mixer, rent in advance and supplies, which are assets of Tk 315,000 together. The bakery also earned a profit. The cash balance alone could not show this, and that is why the three statements are needed.

> Recap: the balance sheet, its two layouts and its equality.
*Before moving on.* The balance sheet lists assets, liabilities and owner's equity at one date, in account form or report form. The total assets equal the total liabilities plus owner's equity.

## 5.5 How the statements connect

> The three statements form a chain, in which each passes one figure to the next.

@! Go slowly here. One link at a time: first profit, then equity, then the sheet.

The three statements are not separate documents. They are three views of the same facts, and they pass figures to each other. Take the first link. The income statement ends with the net profit, which is Tk 158,000 for the bakery. This figure appears again in the statement of owner's equity, on the line *Add: net profit*. That is the first link.

> The second link carries the closing equity to the balance sheet.

The statement of owner's equity ends with the closing equity, Tk 638,000. This figure appears again in the balance sheet, on the line *Owner's equity*. That is the second link. The balance sheet therefore does not show the profit directly. It shows it as part of the owner's equity, which has already been increased by the profit and reduced by the drawings.

:fig The net profit and the closing equity pass from one statement to the next. | ch5_fig3_chain.svg

> The chain gives a check on the work: if the sheet does not balance, a link is wrong.

The chain is also a check. If the balance sheet does not balance, the mistake is in one of three places: in the figures of the assets and liabilities, in the equity statement, or in the net profit. A careful accountant looks first at the links. Does the net profit on the equity statement equal the last line of the income statement? Does the closing equity equal the figure on the balance sheet? If both links hold and the sheet still does not balance, the mistake lies in a balance of an asset or a liability.

> Seen from the owner's side, the three statements answer three questions.

Each statement looks different from the owner's side. For Mitali, the income statement says that she earned Tk 158,000 and that she should be pleased. The equity statement says that her share of the business has grown from Tk 500,000 to Tk 638,000, although she took Tk 20,000 out. The balance sheet says what the business has to show for it. The bank officer reads the same pages and finds a business with Tk 730,000 of assets and a modest Tk 92,000 of debts.

> One more example, with messier figures, shows the whole chain in a single month.

*Given.* Dilruba Flower Stall (illustrative) has these figures for the month ended 30 June 2026. Owner's equity at 1 June was Tk 60,000. No investment was made in June. Revenue was Tk 96,450. Expenses were: wages 22,300; rent 12,000; utilities 3,870; transport 4,230. Drawings were Tk 18,500. At 30 June the stall had cash of Tk 61,350, accounts receivable of Tk 12,470, supplies of Tk 6,800 and equipment of Tk 24,800. It owed Tk 9,870 to suppliers.
*Find.* The three statements, and whether the balance sheet balances.
*Formula.* P = R − E; closing equity = opening equity + investment + P − drawings; assets = liabilities + owner's equity.
*Steps.* Expenses are 22,300 + 12,000 + 3,870 + 4,230 = 42,400. Net profit is 96,450 − 42,400 = 54,050. Closing equity is 60,000 + 54,050 − 18,500 = 95,550. Assets are 61,350 + 12,470 + 6,800 + 24,800 = 105,420. Liabilities plus equity are 9,870 + 95,550 = 105,420.
*Answer.* Net profit is Tk 54,050, closing equity is Tk 95,550, and total assets are Tk 105,420. The sheet balances.
*Check.* Both sides are Tk 105,420, so both links of the chain hold.

:table Dilruba Flower Stall: three statements, June 2026 (Tk)
| Statement | Key lines | Result |
| Income statement | Revenue 96,450 less expenses 42,400 | Net profit 54,050 |
| Statement of owner's equity | 60,000 + 54,050 − 18,500 | Closing equity 95,550 |
| Balance sheet | Assets 105,420; liabilities 9,870; equity 95,550 | Both sides 105,420 |

> A statement can also be built backwards from the equity balance, when a figure is missing.

Sometimes a figure is missing, and the chain helps to find it. *Given.* A bookbinder (illustrative) had owner's equity of Tk 140,000 on 1 December, invested Tk 20,000, withdrew Tk 15,000, and had owner's equity of Tk 168,000 on 31 December. Expenses were Tk 31,000. *Find.* The net profit and the revenue. *Steps.* The equity statement reads 140,000 + 20,000 + P − 15,000 = 168,000. Then 145,000 + P = 168,000, so P = Tk 23,000. Revenue is net profit plus expenses: 23,000 + 31,000 = Tk 54,000. *Check.* 140,000 + 20,000 + 23,000 − 15,000 = Tk 168,000. The chain, run backwards, gives the missing figures.

> Three slips are common: wrong heading, a loss given a plus sign, drawings placed in expenses.

Three slips are common in preparing the statements. The first is a wrong or missing heading, especially the confusion of a period with a date. The second is to add a net loss in the equity statement, when it must be subtracted. The third is to list the owner's drawings among the expenses, which lowers the profit and raises the equity wrongly. Check each statement against its heading and its link before you pass it on.

> The test sentence for the whole section.
*You should now be able to say:* "The income statement gives the net profit, the equity statement uses it to find the closing equity, and the balance sheet shows that equity next to the assets and liabilities."

> Recap: order of preparation and the two links.
*Before moving on.* The statements are prepared in order: income statement, statement of owner's equity, balance sheet. Each passes one figure on, and the balance sheet must balance.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in one paragraph.

Financial statements are formal reports with a three-line heading. The income statement shows revenue less expenses for a period and ends with net profit or net loss. The statement of owner's equity starts with opening equity, adds investment and profit, subtracts drawings and shows closing equity. The balance sheet lists assets, liabilities and owner's equity at one date, in account form or report form, and the two sides agree. The statements are prepared in that order, and each passes a figure to the next. The statement of cash flows and the notes complete a full set. For Mitali Bakery, the unadjusted January statements show net profit of Tk 158,000, closing equity of Tk 638,000 and total assets of Tk 730,000.

## Where people go wrong

> Five common misunderstandings, each stated and corrected.

*"The business with more cash is the more profitable one."* Cash can be turned into assets, as the bakery's did. Profit is revenue less expenses, which is a different figure from the cash balance.

> The balance sheet shows one date; the other two statements show a period.
*"The balance sheet shows what the business did during the year."* It does not. It shows one date. The income statement and the equity statement show what happened during a period.

> Drawings are subtracted in the equity statement, not the income statement.
*"The owner's drawings are an expense."* They are not. They are a share of the profit taken out by the owner, and they are subtracted in the equity statement, not in the income statement.

> A sale on account is revenue before the cash arrives.
*"Revenue is the same as cash received."* A sale on account is revenue on the day of the sale. The cash may come later, and the amount waiting is an asset: accounts receivable.

> The profit reaches the balance sheet inside the owner's equity.
*"The net profit appears on the balance sheet as a separate line."* In the balance sheet of this chapter, the profit is part of the owner's equity. It reaches the sheet through the equity statement.

## In history

> A law of 1844 told the directors of joint-stock companies to prepare a balance sheet.

For most of history, outsiders had no right to see the accounts of a business. In Britain, the Joint Stock Companies Act of 1844 changed this for the companies that registered under it. It required the directors to have the books balanced and a "full and fair" balance sheet made up, and it required the auditors to see the accounts and report on them. A copy of the balance sheet and of the auditors' report was also to be filed with the registrar of companies.^2 The Act did not work well in practice, and later Acts changed the rules. Its importance for this chapter is that it made the balance sheet a duty owed to the shareholders. The idea that owners and lenders have a right to a standard report is now common to every country.

^2: Details of the Act are from a paper on the development of the statutory financial audit published by the United Kingdom's competition authority (section on 1844 to 1900), and from the text of the Act as it was passed.
