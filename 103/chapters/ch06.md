:mood How can a business find each amount at once, and know that it is right?
:desc This chapter teaches the account, the six kinds of account, and the two words debit and credit. > What you must know before you begin.
*You will need.* From Chapter 4: the accounting equation, the six kinds of item (assets, liabilities, owner's capital, drawings, revenue and expenses), and the analysis of a transaction. From Chapter 3: the dual aspect concept, which was previewed there and is taught here. From Chapter 5: the January statements, which we use again in section 6.5.

> The table method of Chapter 4 worked for thirteen transactions.
In Chapter 4 you analysed the thirteen transactions of Mitali Bakery's first month. Each transaction was one line in a table, and every line showed which items changed. That method is clear, and it was the right way to begin. Now imagine the same bakery two years later. It sells bread to hundreds of customers every day. It buys flour every week, pays wages every Thursday, and receives payments from hotels and schools.

> A long table has two weaknesses: amounts are hard to find, and mistakes stay hidden.
A table with one line for each transaction would soon fill hundreds of pages. Mitali asks a plain question at the end of each day: how much cash do we have? To answer it, she must read a whole column from the top. She asks another: how much do our customers owe us? She must search a different column. That is the first weakness. The second is worse. If one number in the table is wrong, nothing in the table shows it. The page looks tidy, and the error stays hidden until a customer, a banker or a tax officer finds it.

> Merchants solved both problems with one page for each kind of item, and one rule.
Merchants met the same problems long ago, and they found an answer with two parts. First, keep a separate page for each kind of item, so that every amount has a place. Second, follow one rule when writing amounts, so that a mistake makes the pages disagree. The answer is called the double-entry system, and it is still the basis of business records today.

> This chapter is an absolute first, so it moves slowly.
This chapter teaches that answer. It introduces more new words than any earlier chapter, but the ideas are few. We take them one at a time, from an everyday notebook to the full rule, and we use small numbers until the method is secure. Each section ends with a short recap. Where an idea is difficult, a note in the side column says so. By the end you will be able to say where any transaction belongs, and why.

## 6.1 The Account and Its Parts

> A household can keep its cash in a notebook with two pages.
@! Go slowly here.
Begin with a household, not a business. Farida keeps one notebook for the cash of her family. On the left page she writes every amount that comes in: her salary of Tk 1,000 and a gift of Tk 200 from an uncle. On the right page she writes every amount that goes out: Tk 300 for rice and Tk 80 for bus fares. The figure below shows the two pages.

:fig The cash notebook of a household: money received on the left page, money paid on the right page. | figures/fig06_notebook.svg

> One pair of pages answers the question: how much cash is there?
The notebook answers one question quickly. Farida adds the left page: Tk 1,000 + Tk 200 = Tk 1,200. She adds the right page: Tk 300 + Tk 80 = Tk 380. The difference is Tk 1,200 − Tk 380 = Tk 820. That is the cash in the house. She did not search through the whole month, because everything about cash is on one pair of pages.

> A second notebook for a different item shows why each item has its own pages.
Farida keeps a second, smaller notebook for something else: Tk 500 that she borrowed from a neighbour. When she borrows more, she writes the amount on one page. When she repays, she writes the amount on the other page. If she mixed the loan into the cash notebook, she could no longer see at once how much cash she holds and how much she still owes. One notebook for each kind of item answers one question each. A business has many kinds of item, so it keeps many such notebooks.

@ Account | In daily life, a record of money held in a bank. In accounting, a named record of one kind of item, such as Cash or Sales.
> A business keeps one such record for each kind of item.
A business uses the same idea. For each kind of item it keeps one named record, and the record is called an **account**. An account is a named record of one kind of item. Mitali Bakery has an account named Cash, an account named Equipment, an account named Rent Expense, and many more. Each has its own page, and everything about that item is found there.

@ T-account | A simple picture of an account, shaped like the letter T, with a left side and a right side.
> Accountants draw an account in the shape of the letter T.
Accountants need a quick way to draw an account. They draw it in the shape of the capital letter T, and they call the drawing a **T-account**. A T-account is a simple picture of an account, with a left side and a right side. The title of the account is written across the top. A vertical line divides the account into its two sides. These sides are the left page and the right page of the notebook. The next figure names the parts.

:fig The parts of a T-account: the title, the left side and the right side. The amounts are small, to keep the idea clear. | figures/fig06_taccount.svg

> Each amount is written on one side only, and the side has a meaning.
Each amount that we write in an account goes on one side only. The side tells the reader what kind of change the amount shows. In the notebook, the left page showed money coming in. Section 6.3 gives the full rule for every kind of account. For now, only the shape matters: a title, a left side and a right side.

> The difference between the two sides shows how much the account holds.
Look at the Cash account in the figure. Its left side holds Tk 500 and Tk 90, a total of Tk 590. Its right side holds Tk 100 and Tk 45, a total of Tk 145. The difference is Tk 590 − Tk 145 = Tk 445. That is the cash that the business holds. Accountants call this difference the balance of the account. Chapter 7 teaches how to find it. For now, remember only that every account can answer the question: how much?

> The T-account is a sketch; the full ledger page comes in Chapter 7.
The T-account is a tool for thinking and for quick sketches. It is not the form in which a business keeps its permanent records. Chapter 7 shows the page of the ledger, which has columns for the date and for notes. The T-account and the ledger page hold the same facts, and the rules of this chapter apply to both.

> Pause and check: find the difference between two sides.
*Pause and check.* A page for Rent Expense shows Tk 900 on its left side and Tk 350 on its right side. What is the difference between the two sides? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> Recap of 6.1: an account is a named record with a title and two sides.
*Before moving on.* An account is a named record of one kind of item. Its picture, the T-account, has a title, a left side and a right side. Each amount is written on one side only, and the difference between the two sides shows how much the account holds. You should now be able to say: *an account is a named record of one kind of item, with a left side and a right side.*

## 6.2 The Six Kinds of Account

> Six kinds of item need six kinds of account.
In Chapter 4 you met six kinds of item: assets, liabilities, owner's capital, drawings, revenue and expenses. Each account takes its kind from the item that it records. So there are six kinds of account, and the bakery has several accounts of most kinds. We meet them one at a time. Each is defined in the same way: first the kind, then an example from Mitali Bakery.

@ Asset account | An account that records one kind of asset, such as Cash or Equipment.
> An asset account records one kind of thing that the business owns.
An **asset account** records one kind of asset. Cash is an asset account: it records the cash that the bakery holds. Equipment is another: it records the oven and the mixer. Accounts Receivable is a third: it records the money that customers owe to the bakery.

@ Liability account | An account that records an amount owed, such as Accounts Payable.
> A liability account records one kind of amount that the business owes.
A **liability account** records one kind of amount that the business owes. Accounts Payable is the main example. It records the money that the bakery owes to its suppliers, such as the Tk 140,000 that remains unpaid for the oven and the mixer after the purchase of 2 January.

@ Owner's capital account | The account that records the owner's investment and the owner's share of profit.
> The owner's capital account records what the owner has invested.
The **owner's capital account** records the owner's investment in the business. In January, Mitali Rahman invests Tk 500,000, and the Owner's Capital account records it. In Chapter 12 we shall see that the profit of a period is also added to this account. In this chapter it holds the investment only.

@ Drawings account | The account that records what the owner takes out of the business.
> The drawings account records what the owner takes out.
The **drawings account** records the cash or goods that the owner takes from the business for personal use. It is kept apart from the capital account on purpose. The owner can then see how much was invested and how much was taken out, without searching for the difference.

@ Revenue account | An account that records one kind of earnings, such as Sales Revenue.
> A revenue account records one kind of earnings.
A **revenue account** records one kind of earnings from selling goods or performing services. The bakery has one revenue account, named Sales Revenue. It records every sale of bread, cakes and catering, whether the customer pays at once or later.

@ Expense account | An account that records one kind of cost used up, such as Rent Expense.
> An expense account records one kind of cost that is used up.
An **expense account** records one kind of cost that the business uses up in order to earn revenue. Wages Expense records the pay of the baker and the helpers. Rent Expense records the rent of the shop. Utilities Expense records the electricity bill. Each cost has its own account, so that the owner can see where the money goes.

> The table sums up the six kinds, with an example of each.
The table below sums up the six kinds. Read each row from left to right: the kind of account, what it records, and an example from the bakery's books.

:table The six kinds of account
| Kind of account | What it records | Example in the bakery |
| Asset | Things the business owns | Cash; Equipment |
| Liability | Amounts the business owes | Accounts Payable |
| Owner's capital | The owner's investment | Owner's Capital |
| Drawings | What the owner takes out | Owner's Drawings |
| Revenue | Earnings from sales | Sales Revenue |
| Expense | Costs used up | Rent Expense; Wages Expense |

> Separate accounts keep different facts apart, so that each user can find what is needed.
Why does the bakery not write sales and costs straight into the capital account, since in the end they change the owner's equity? The reason is information. The owner wants to know how much came from sales and how much went on wages. The bank wants the same facts. The tax officer wants revenue and expenses stated separately. One mixed account would hide all of this. Separate accounts keep the facts apart, and anyone can add them together when needed.

> A sorting exercise: eight items of the bakery, each placed in its kind.
Sorting is the skill that this section builds, so here are eight items from the bakery, each placed in its kind. The oven is an asset, because the bakery owns it. The flour in the store room is an asset, because it is owned and will be used. The Tk 140,000 still owed to the oven supplier is a liability. The Tk 40,000 that the school owes for catering is an asset, Accounts Receivable, because the bakery will receive it. The bread sold on 10 January brought revenue. The electricity bill is an expense. Mitali's first Tk 500,000 is owner's capital. The Tk 20,000 that she took on 30 January is drawings. Two points deserve notice. The school's debt is an asset to the bakery and a liability to the school: one fact, seen from two seats. And the flour in the store room is an asset today, but when the flour is used, its cost becomes an expense (Chapter 9).

> The expanded equation joins the six kinds of account.
The six kinds fit the equation of Chapter 4. In words: assets equal liabilities plus owner's capital plus revenue, less expenses, less drawings. In symbols, let A stand for assets, L for liabilities, C for owner's capital, R for revenue, X for expenses and D for drawings. Then A = L + C + R − X − D. Revenue increases the owner's claim, so it stands with capital. Expenses and drawings reduce the owner's claim, so they stand with the minus sign. The next section depends on this equation, so read it once more before you continue.

> Recap of 6.2: six kinds of account, each with a place in the equation.
*Before moving on.* The six kinds of account are asset, liability, owner's capital, drawings, revenue and expense. Revenue stands with capital in the equation. Expenses and drawings stand against it. You should now be able to say: *each kind of item has its own kind of account, and the equation shows how the six kinds are related.*

## 6.3 Debit and Credit: Two Words with One Job

> Debit and credit are two words that name the two sides of an account.
@! Go slowly here.
Debit and credit are the two hardest words in this book. They are hard for a plain reason: everyday English already gives them meanings, and those meanings mislead. You may think that credit is good and debit is bad. You may think that credit means money in and debit means money out. None of this is true in accounting. In accounting each word has one job, and the job is to name a side.

@ Debit | An entry on the left side of an account.
@ Credit | An entry on the right side of an account. In daily life, credit can mean praise, or buying now and paying later. Here it names only a side.
> A debit is an entry on the left; a credit is an entry on the right.
A **debit** is an entry on the left side of an account. A **credit** is an entry on the right side of an account. That is the whole definition. To debit an account means to write an amount on its left side. To credit an account means to write an amount on its right side. If the bakery writes Tk 30 on the left side of the Supplies account, we say that Supplies is debited by Tk 30. If it writes Tk 30 on the right side of the Cash account, we say that Cash is credited by Tk 30.

> A debit may increase an account or decrease it, depending on the kind of account.
Notice what the definition does not say. It does not say that a debit increases an account. It does not say that a debit is good. A debit increases some accounts and decreases others. Which one happens depends on the kind of account, and the rest of this section gives the rule.^1

^1: Your bank may say that it has credited your account when it adds money to it. The bank keeps its own records from its own point of view, and in them your money is something the bank owes you. Chapter 16 explains this with care. In this book, debit and credit always describe the books of the business that we are studying.

### Step one: assets

> Assets increase on the left, because assets stand on the left of the equation.
The rule is built from the equation, one piece at a time. On the page, the equation of Chapter 4 puts assets on the left, and liabilities and owner's equity on the right. Merchants used this layout to fix the rule for assets: an increase in an asset is written on the left side of its account, as a debit, and a decrease is written on the right side, as a credit. This was a choice made long ago. Once it is made, every other rule follows from it.

> Cash shows the rule: money in is a debit, money out is a credit.
Test the rule on Cash, an asset account. The business receives Tk 100 in cash. Cash increases, so we debit Cash by Tk 100. The business then pays Tk 30 in cash. Cash decreases, so we credit Cash by Tk 30. The left side holds Tk 100 and the right side holds Tk 30, so the difference is Tk 70. This agrees with the notebook: money in on the left, money out on the right.

### Step two: liabilities and owner's capital

> Liabilities and capital increase on the right, which is the opposite of assets.
Now turn to the right side of the equation, where liabilities and owner's capital stand. Their rule is the opposite of the rule for assets: an increase is written on the right side, as a credit, and a decrease on the left side, as a debit. The reason lies in the equation. Every transaction must keep the equation in balance. If an asset increases by a debit, the matching change on the other side of the equation must be a credit. Then the debits and the credits of the transaction are equal.

> First example, with the smallest numbers: the owner invests Tk 1,000.
An example shows this. The owner invests Tk 1,000 in cash. Assets increase, so we debit Cash by Tk 1,000. Owner's capital increases by the same amount, so we credit Owner's Capital by Tk 1,000. The debit is Tk 1,000 and the credit is Tk 1,000. They are equal, and the equation still holds: assets are Tk 1,000, and owner's capital is Tk 1,000.

> Second example: buying on account makes a liability increase with a credit, and paying makes it decrease with a debit.
A second example uses a liability. A supplier allows the business to buy flour worth Tk 400 on account. The asset Supplies increases, so we debit Supplies by Tk 400. The liability Accounts Payable increases, so we credit Accounts Payable by Tk 400. Later the business pays the supplier Tk 400 in cash. The liability decreases, so we debit Accounts Payable by Tk 400. The asset Cash decreases, so we credit Cash by Tk 400. Here a debit decreases an account. Remember this example whenever you are tempted to think that a debit always means an increase.

### Step three: revenue, expenses and drawings

> Revenue, expenses and drawings follow from the expanded equation.
The last three kinds follow from the expanded equation of section 6.2. Revenue increases the owner's claim, as capital does. So revenue behaves like capital: it increases on the right, with a credit. Expenses and drawings reduce the owner's claim. So they behave in the opposite way to capital: they increase on the left, with a debit. A decrease in any of the three kinds uses the opposite side.

> Two examples: a sale for cash and a payment of rent.
Two examples with small numbers show the rule. The bakery sells bread for Tk 200 in cash. Cash, an asset, increases: we debit Cash by Tk 200. Revenue increases: we credit Sales Revenue by Tk 200. Next the bakery pays rent of Tk 50 in cash. The expense increases: we debit Rent Expense by Tk 50. Cash decreases: we credit Cash by Tk 50. In each transaction the debits equal the credits.

> The figure gathers all six rules in one picture.
The figure below gathers the six rules. Read each small T-account in the same way. The title names the kind of account. The left half shows what a debit does, and the right half shows what a credit does.

:fig The rules of debit and credit for the six kinds of account. A plus sign means increase; a minus sign means decrease. | figures/fig06_rules.svg

> The same rules appear again in a table, for easy reference.
The table below shows the same rules in another form. It is worth learning, because every later chapter uses it.

:table Rules of increase and decrease
| Kind of account | A debit | A credit |
| Asset | increases | decreases |
| Liability | decreases | increases |
| Owner's capital | decreases | increases |
| Drawings | increases | decreases |

A debit is an entry on the left side of an account, and a credit is an entry on the right side. A debit increases assets, drawings and expenses, and decreases liabilities, owner's capital and revenue. A credit does the opposite in each case.

> The six transactions pass two tests: equal totals, and the equation.
Now test the whole set. The debit amounts total Tk 1,000 + Tk 400 + Tk 400 + Tk 200 + Tk 50 + Tk 100 = Tk 2,150, and the credit amounts total the same. Look at Cash: the debits are Tk 1,000 + Tk 200 = Tk 1,200, the credits are Tk 400 + Tk 50 + Tk 100 = Tk 550, and the difference is Tk 650. The other asset, Supplies, holds Tk 400, so the assets are Tk 1,050. On the other side of the equation, the liability is nil because the supplier was paid, the capital is Tk 1,000, the revenue is Tk 200, the expense is Tk 50 and the drawings are Tk 100. Then Tk 0 + Tk 1,000 + Tk 200 − Tk 50 − Tk 100 = Tk 1,050. The two sides agree.

> The rule asks what kind of item changed, not whether the change is good.
The rule never asks whether a transaction is good for the business. It asks only two things: what kind of account changed, and in which direction. There are two limits. The rules apply to ordinary accounts. In Chapter 9 you will meet accounts that are deducted from another account, and they run the opposite way. Also, an account that shows its difference on the unexpected side is often a sign of a mistake, as section 6.5 explains.

> Recap of 6.3: debit is left, credit is right, and the kind of account decides the effect.
*Before moving on.* Debit means the left side and credit means the right side. Assets, drawings and expenses increase with a debit. Liabilities, owner's capital and revenue increase with a credit. Each decrease uses the opposite side. You should now be able to say: *a debit and a credit name two sides, and the kind of account decides whether each one increases or decreases it.*

## 6.4 The Double-Entry System

> Every transaction has two sides: something is given and something is received.
@! Go slowly here.
A transaction is never a single event. When the bakery buys flour, it receives flour, and it gives up cash or promises cash later. When it sells bread, it receives cash and gives up bread. Chapter 3 called this the dual aspect concept: every transaction has two sides. The two sides are equal in value. If the bakery gives Tk 100 in cash for flour, it receives flour that is worth Tk 100 to it.

@ Double-entry system | A method in which every transaction is recorded in two places, with equal debits and credits.
> The double-entry system records both sides of every transaction.
The **double-entry system** uses this fact. It is a method in which every transaction is recorded in two places, with equal debits and credits. One side of the event is recorded as a debit in one account, and the other side is recorded as a credit in another account. The word double means two sides of one event. It does not mean that the same fact is written twice. Merchants used a method of this kind more than seven hundred years ago, and the story is told at the end of the chapter.

> The rule of double entry: in every transaction, total debits equal total credits.
The rule can be stated in one sentence. In every transaction, the total of the debits equals the total of the credits. If the two totals are not equal, the transaction has been recorded wrongly.

> Three January transactions show the rule at work.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* We test the rule on three transactions from the bakery's first week. On 1 January 2026, Mitali Rahman invests Tk 500,000 in cash. Cash, an asset, increases, so Cash is debited by Tk 500,000. Owner's capital increases, so Owner's Capital is credited by Tk 500,000. On 3 January, the bakery pays Tk 45,000 in cash for three months' rent in advance. The asset Prepaid Rent increases, so Prepaid Rent is debited by Tk 45,000.^2 Cash decreases, so Cash is credited by Tk 45,000. On 10 January, the bakery sells baked goods for Tk 90,000 in cash. Cash is debited by Tk 90,000, and Sales Revenue is credited by Tk 90,000. In each transaction the debits equal the credits.

^2: Prepaid Rent is an asset: it is rent paid before the months to which it belongs. Chapter 9 shows how it is used up month by month.

> The rule works because each side of the equation changes by the same amount.
Why does the rule work? Recall how the rules of section 6.3 were built. Each rule was chosen so that a debit is always matched by a credit of equal size. An increase in an asset (a debit) is matched by an increase in a liability or in capital (a credit), or by a decrease in another asset (a credit). So when the debits equal the credits, the changes on the two sides of the equation are equal. The equation stays in balance after every transaction.

> Most transactions follow a few patterns, which the table lists.
Most transactions follow a few patterns. The table below lists nine of them. Read each row from left to right: the event, the account that is debited, and the account that is credited.

:table Nine common transactions and their two sides
| Event | Debit | Credit |
| Owner invests cash | Cash | Owner's Capital |
| Business buys equipment for cash | Equipment | Cash |
| Business buys equipment on account | Equipment | Accounts Payable |
| Business pays a supplier what it owes | Accounts Payable | Cash |
| Business sells goods for cash | Cash | Sales Revenue |
| Business sells goods on account | Accounts Receivable | Sales Revenue |
| Customer pays what it owes | Cash | Accounts Receivable |
| Business pays an expense in cash | The expense account | Cash |
| Owner takes cash for personal use | Owner's Drawings | Cash |

> A transaction can involve three accounts; the single debit then equals the two credits.
Some transactions involve three accounts. The rule is the same: the total of the debits equals the total of the credits. The case below is the first of this kind that you will see.

> The oven and the mixer: one debit equals two credits together.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 2 January 2026 the bakery buys the oven and the mixer for Tk 240,000. It pays Tk 100,000 in cash and promises the remaining Tk 140,000 to the supplier. Equipment increases by Tk 240,000, so Equipment is debited by Tk 240,000. Cash decreases by Tk 100,000, so Cash is credited by Tk 100,000. Accounts Payable increases by Tk 140,000, so it is credited by Tk 140,000. The one debit equals the two credits together, because Tk 100,000 + Tk 140,000 = Tk 240,000.

:fig The purchase of the oven and the mixer: one debit equals two credits. | figures/fig06_threeaccounts.svg

> The opposite shape: two debits and one credit in one payment.
A transaction can also have two debits and one credit. Suppose that a shop owner pays one electricity bill of Tk 7,200 in cash. Tk 6,000 of it is for the shop and Tk 1,200 is for the owner's home. The shop's share is an expense, so Utilities Expense is debited by Tk 6,000. The home share is a personal use of the business's cash, so Owner's Drawings is debited by Tk 1,200. Cash decreases by Tk 7,200, so Cash is credited by Tk 7,200. The two debits, Tk 6,000 + Tk 1,200, equal the one credit. This entry also shows the business entity concept of Chapter 3: the private bill is kept apart from the expenses of the business.

> Double entry proves that debits equal credits, but not that every amount or account is right.
Double entry gives a strong check, but it has limits. It proves that the debits and the credits are equal. It does not prove that the right accounts were used, or that the amount is correct. Suppose the bakery records a payment of rent in the Wages Expense account. The debit still equals the credit, but the expense is in the wrong place. Chapter 8 shows how the books are tested, and which mistakes escape the test.

> One transaction looks different from three seats.
The same transaction looks different from three seats. The owner asks: what has changed in my business? The lender asks: does the business owe more than before? The accountant asks: do the debits equal the credits? Double entry serves all three. The two accounts show what changed, a liability account shows what is owed, and the equal totals show that both sides of the event were recorded.

> Pause and check: record two purchases of flour.
*Pause and check.* (a) The bakery buys flour for Tk 800 in cash. Which account is debited, and which is credited? (b) The bakery buys the same flour for Tk 800 on account. Which accounts change now? (The answers are at the back of the book.)

> Recap of 6.4: record both sides of each transaction, with equal debits and credits.
*Before moving on.* Every transaction has two sides. The double-entry system records both: it debits one or more accounts and credits one or more accounts, and the total of the debits equals the total of the credits. You should now be able to say: *in double entry, every transaction is recorded in at least two accounts, and the debits equal the credits.*

## 6.5 Normal Balances and the Equation Check

@ Normal balance | The side of an account, debit or credit, on which increases are recorded.
> The normal balance of an account is the side on which it increases.
In section 6.1 we met the difference between the two sides of an account. Accountants call it the balance of the account, and Chapter 7 teaches how to find it. Here we need one fact about it. Every account has a **normal balance**. A normal balance is the side of an account, debit or credit, on which increases are recorded. An account usually shows its balance on that side.

> The normal balance follows from the rules of section 6.3.
The table below shows the normal balance of each kind of account. It repeats section 6.3 in a new form. Assets increase on the left, so their normal balance is a debit. Liabilities increase on the right, so their normal balance is a credit. The other rows follow in the same way.

:table Normal balance of each kind of account
| Kind of account | Increases on | Normal balance |
| Asset | the left | Debit |
| Liability | the right | Credit |
| Owner's capital | the right | Credit |
| Drawings | the left | Debit |
| Revenue | the right | Credit |
| Expense | the left | Debit |

> A balance on the unexpected side is a warning.
The idea gives a quick test. Cash should show a debit balance, because a business cannot pay out more cash than it holds. If the Cash account shows a credit balance, something is probably wrong. A payment may have been recorded twice, or a receipt may have been left out. A balance on the unexpected side is a warning, not a proof. A few accounts are meant to run the opposite way, as Chapter 9 explains.

> A warning in practice: a Cash account that shows a credit balance.
Here is an example of a warning. The Cash account of a small shop holds Tk 5,000 on its left side and Tk 5,800 on its right side. The difference, Tk 800, is on the credit side. A shop cannot pay out more cash than it received, so a payment has probably been recorded twice, or a receipt has been left out. The unexpected side tells the accountant where to look.

> The expanded equation gives a check on all the accounts together.
The normal balances also give a check on all the accounts at once. Start from the equation of section 6.2: A = L + C + R − X − D. Add X and D to both sides. Then A + X + D = L + C + R. The left side holds the accounts whose normal balance is a debit: assets, expenses and drawings. The right side holds the accounts whose normal balance is a credit: liabilities, owner's capital and revenue. So, if every transaction has been recorded with equal debits and credits, the total of the debit balances equals the total of the credit balances.

From Chapter 5: total assets Tk 730,000; liabilities Tk 92,000; revenue Tk 200,000; expenses Tk 42,000; drawings Tk 20,000. > Recap of 6.5: each kind of account has a normal balance, and debit-side items equal credit-side items.
*Before moving on.* The normal balance of an account is the side on which it increases. Assets, drawings and expenses have a debit balance; liabilities, owner's capital and revenue have a credit balance. When all transactions are recorded correctly, the debit-side items equal the credit-side items. You should now be able to say: *the equation, rearranged, tells us that total debit balances equal total credit balances.*

## 6.6 The Chart of Accounts

@ Chart of accounts | A list of all the accounts a business uses, each with a number.
> A chart of accounts lists every account a business uses, with numbers.
Before it records any transaction, a business decides which accounts it will use. The list is called a **chart of accounts**. A chart of accounts is a list of all the accounts a business uses, each with a number. The numbers follow a plan. Accounts of one kind share a first digit: assets begin with 1, liabilities with 2, owner's equity with 3, revenue with 4 and expenses with 5. The gaps between numbers leave room for new accounts, so that no other number needs to change.

## Summary

> An account is a named record, drawn as a T-account, and there are six kinds.
An account is a named record of one kind of item. Its picture is the T-account, with a title, a left side and a right side. There are six kinds of account: asset, liability, owner's capital, drawings, revenue and expense.

> Debit means left and credit means right; the kind of account decides the effect.
A debit is an entry on the left side of an account, and a credit is an entry on the right side. Assets, drawings and expenses increase with debits. Liabilities, owner's capital and revenue increase with credits. Each decrease uses the opposite side.

> Double entry records each transaction in two places with equal debits and credits, which keeps the equation in balance.
In the double-entry system, every transaction is recorded in at least two accounts, and the total of the debits equals the total of the credits. Each account has a normal balance, on the side where it increases. When all transactions are recorded correctly, the debit-side items equal the credit-side items. The chart of accounts lists every account with its number.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: believing that a debit always decreases and a credit always increases.
*"A debit decreases an account and a credit increases it."* This comes from daily life, and it is wrong in the books. A debit increases an asset account and decreases a liability account. The effect depends on the kind of account. Learn the side first and the effect second.

> Mistake two: believing that credit is good and debit is bad.
*"Credit is good news and debit is bad news."* Neither word carries a judgement. A sale is good news, and it is recorded with a credit to revenue and a debit to Cash. A large expense is bad news, and it is recorded with a debit. Both words only name sides.

> Mistake three: debiting revenue because money has come in.
*"Money came in, so I debit the revenue account."* Money came in to Cash, so Cash is debited. Revenue is a different account, and revenue increases with a credit. Every sale for cash needs both entries.

> Mistake four: expecting one account to have equal sides.
*"Each account should have equal debits and credits."* The equality belongs to the transaction, not to the single account. The Cash account usually has more on one side than on the other, and that difference is what we want to know.

> Mistake five: thinking that double entry means writing the same fact twice.
*"Double entry means writing the same thing twice."* It means writing the two sides of one event, and those two sides are different facts: one account gains and another account gives. Writing the same side twice would break the equality at once.

> Mistake six: trusting equal totals as proof of accuracy.
*"The totals agree, so the books are right."* Equal totals prove only that the debits equal the credits. A transaction recorded in the wrong account, or with the wrong amount in both places, still balances. Chapter 8 explains these errors.

## In History

> Genoa, 1340: books of a city treasury were long called the oldest double-entry ledger.
Historians once named the account book of the Massari for the year 1340 as the oldest surviving double-entry ledger. The Massari were the officials who handled the money of the city of Genoa. Their accounts were set out in two-sided form, with debits and credits, and balances were carried forward from the year before. Later research found earlier ledgers kept by merchants in Tuscany that already used the method, among them the ledger of the Farolfi firm of 1299 to 1300. So the method was older than the Genoese book, and it was used by merchants before governments.

> Pacioli, 1494: a printed treatise spread the method to many readers.
A printed book then gave the method a wide audience. In 1494, Luca Pacioli, a friar and teacher of mathematics, published a large book on arithmetic in Venice. It contained a short treatise on bookkeeping that described the method used by Venetian merchants. Pacioli did not invent double entry. His treatise was the first printed account of it, and later writers in many countries learned from it. The lesson for us is plain. A rule that made mistakes visible was valuable enough to be taught, copied and printed, and it has served business records ever since.
