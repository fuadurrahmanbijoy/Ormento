:chapter III | The Rules Behind the Records
:mood Why do two honest bakers report two different results?
:desc This chapter shows why accountants follow shared rules. It teaches the basic concepts of entity, money measurement, cost and going concern, the idea of the accounting period, and the standards that let one business be compared with another.

> Two identical bakeries give two different answers to one question.
Two bakers, Karim and Salma, open shops on the same street on the same day. Their shops are alike in every way that matters. Each baker sells bread worth Tk 60,000 in the first month. Each buys flour, sugar and fuel worth Tk 25,000, and each pays helpers Tk 15,000. Both bakers are honest, and both can add and subtract.

> A bank officer asks for the profit and receives two numbers.
At the end of the month a bank officer asks each baker the same question: how much profit did you make? Karim answers Tk 20,000. Salma answers Tk 15,000. The officer is puzzled, because the two shops seem to be the same. Which baker has the better shop?

> The difference comes from one payment that only one baker counted.
The officer asks Salma to explain. She says that during the month she took Tk 5,000 from the shop's cash drawer to buy rice and vegetables for her family, and that she counted this money as a cost of the shop. Karim paid for his family's food from his own pocket and left it out of the shop's results. Neither baker made an error of arithmetic. They disagreed about what belongs to the shop.

> A second question about one oven gives three answers.
The officer then asks a second question: what is your oven worth? Each baker bought the same oven for Tk 100,000 on the first day. Karim says it is worth Tk 70,000, because a dealer offered him that sum on the last day of the month. Salma says Tk 100,000, because that is the price she paid. A third baker, on the next street, says Tk 130,000, because his oven bakes so well that he could not replace it for less. There are three answers for one kind of oven.

> A third difficulty is that each baker reports a different stretch of time.
A third difficulty follows. One baker reports results for the busy week before a festival. Another reports results for the whole year so far. A third reports results since the shop opened. The officer cannot compare a week with a year, and cannot trust any figure without knowing how it was counted.

> The officer's trouble is the trouble of every reader of accounts, and shared rules are the cure.
The officer's trouble is the trouble of every person who reads accounts. A number has meaning only when everyone who produces it follows the same rules about what to include, how to measure it and which period to cover. This chapter teaches those rules. It begins with the reason rules are needed, then teaches four basic concepts and the idea of the accounting period, and ends with the standards in which the rules are written down.

> This chapter relies on the words of Chapters 1 and 2.
*You will need* the following earlier ideas: from Chapter 1, business, owner, profit and cash; from Chapter 2, accounting information and its users. No new arithmetic is needed. This chapter has no figures for the running bakery yet; they begin in Chapter 4.

## 3.1 Why Shared Rules Are Needed

> A kilogram must mean the same weight at every market stall.
@! Go slowly here.
Begin with a market. A buyer of rice wishes to compare the price at two stalls. This is possible only because a kilogram means the same weight at both. The law and long custom fix the unit. If each seller used a private stone as a weight, no buyer could compare prices, and every buyer would have to check every stall. Trust in the market rests on a shared unit.

> Accounting information also needs a shared unit before anyone can trust it.
Accounting information is in the same position. Chapter 2 showed that owners, lenders and tax officers read the same records for different decisions. None of them can watch every payment in every business. Each must trust the figures on the page, and each wishes to compare one business with another, or one year with another. That trust and that comparison are possible only if the people who prepare the figures follow the same rules.

> An accounting concept is a basic rule that tells accountants how to record and report.
@ Accounting concept | A basic rule that tells accountants how to record and report.
The basic rules of accounting are called concepts. An **accounting concept** means a basic rule that tells accountants how to record and report. For example, the rule that a business is kept apart from the private affairs of its owner is an accounting concept. Some books call such rules *principles*. This book uses one word, *concept*, for the basic rules, and keeps the word *standard* for the detailed written rules of section 3.4.

> Rules answer three questions: what to record, how to measure it, and for which period.
Rules do three jobs. First, they say what is included in the records and what is left out. Second, they say how an included item is measured, so that the same event always receives the same amount. Third, they say for which stretch of time the results are reported. The two bakers of the opening puzzle differed on all three jobs: Salma included a private payment, the three bakers measured the oven in three ways, and the reporting periods were different.

> Why not let each business choose its own way of counting?
One might ask why each business should not choose its own way of counting, since the owner knows the business best. There are three reasons against it. The first is trust: a lender cannot check how every borrower counts, and so would trust none. The second is comparison: a buyer, an investor or a tax officer must set one business beside another. The third is fairness: people outside the business, who cannot see inside it, must be protected from figures that flatter the owner.

> Owners gain too, because shared rules let them compare their own months and years.
Owners gain from shared rules as well. An owner who counts in the same way every month can see whether the shop is improving. An owner who changes the method each month cannot tell whether a better result comes from better trading or from a different way of counting. From the owner's seat, then, shared rules are also a tool for honest self-knowledge.

> Three users rely on the same rules for three different purposes.
Consider how three users depend on the rules. The bank officer relies on them to judge whether a loan will be repaid. The tax officer relies on them to find the profit on which tax is due. The owner relies on them to decide whether to hire another helper or to buy a second oven. If the rules differed from business to business, each user would need a different method of reading every set of records, and few would take the trouble. Shared rules save every reader this labour.

> Rules reduce disagreement but cannot remove judgement.
There are limits to what rules can do. Accounting rules are agreements made by people, not laws of nature, and different countries have agreed on somewhat different rules. The rules also change over time as business changes. Finally, rules leave room for judgement: two honest accountants may still estimate the same item differently within the rules. Shared rules reduce disagreement, but they do not abolish it.

> A short calculation shows that one shared rule gives both bakers the same profit.
Return to the first difference of the opening puzzle and apply a rule. *Given.* Each baker sold bread for Tk 60,000, bought flour, sugar and fuel for Tk 25,000, and paid helpers Tk 15,000. Salma also took Tk 5,000 from the cash drawer for her family. *Find.* The profit of each shop under one shared rule. *Formula.* In words: profit equals earnings less costs, and only costs of the business count as costs. In symbols: P = E − C.

> The steps, answer and check complete the calculation.
*Steps.* Earnings are Tk 60,000. Costs of the business are 25,000 + 15,000 = Tk 40,000. The family's rice is not a cost of the business. So P = 60,000 − 40,000 = Tk 20,000. *Answer.* Both shops made a profit of Tk 20,000, and Salma's figure of Tk 15,000 was wrong under the shared rule. *Check.* The two shops are alike in every way that matters, so their profits should be equal. Both are Tk 20,000, so the check agrees.

> Pause and check: name the three differences between the bakers.
*Pause and check.* In the opening puzzle, name the three ways in which the bakers' figures were not comparable. (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> A short recap of section 3.1 for the reader.
*Before moving on.* Rules are needed so that accounting figures can be trusted and compared. A basic rule is called an accounting concept. Rules say what to record, how to measure it and for which period. Rules reduce disagreement; they do not remove judgement. *You should now be able to say:* "Without shared rules, two honest businesses could report different results for the same facts."

## 3.2 The Basic Concepts: Entity, Money Measurement, Cost and Going Concern

> Four basic concepts answer the questions raised by the puzzle.
Four basic concepts answer the questions of the opening puzzle. The business entity concept answers what belongs to the shop. The money measurement concept answers what can be recorded at all. The cost concept answers how an item is measured. The going concern concept answers how long the business is expected to last. We take them one at a time, each with a bridge from daily life, an example, the rule and its limits.

### The Business Entity

> Two purses: your own, and one that you manage for a class picnic.
@! Go slowly here.
Suppose a class chooses you to hold the money for a picnic. You keep it in a separate purse. When you buy a bus ticket for yourself, you pay from your own purse, never from the picnic purse. The class can then see exactly what the picnic received and spent. If your own spending were mixed with the picnic money, nobody could tell what the picnic really cost.

> A business needs the same separation between its money and its owner's money.
@ Business entity concept | The rule that a business is kept apart from the private affairs of its owner.
A business needs the same separation. The **business entity concept** means the rule that a business is kept apart from the private affairs of its owner. The books record only what happens to the business. The rice that Salma bought for her family is a private matter of the owner, so it does not belong in the shop's records. With this rule, Karim and Salma would both report Tk 20,000.

> Money crosses the line only when the owner puts money in or takes money out.
The line between owner and business is crossed in only two ways. The owner may put money or other things into the business, and the owner may take money or goods out for private use. Both movements are recorded as dealings between the owner and the business. Chapter 4 teaches how. In the opening puzzle, Salma should have recorded the Tk 5,000 as money withdrawn by the owner, not as a cost of the shop.

:fig The business entity concept: the books of the business record only what is inside the line; money crosses the line only when the owner puts it in or takes it out. | ch3_fig1.svg

> One question helps to apply the entity concept: whose affair is this?
In practice, an owner can apply the entity concept by asking one question about each payment: whose affair is this? If the payment buys something for the shop, the shop pays. If it buys something for the family, the owner pays from private money. Suppose Mitali buys a bag of rice. If the rice will be baked into the shop's bread, the bag is a cost of the shop. If the rice will be cooked at home, it is a private matter. The same bag of rice can be either, and only its use decides. Honest owners keep the two uses apart even when the sums are small, because small habits become large errors.

> A sole trader is one person in law, yet the books still treat the business as separate.
There is an important exception to explain. In law, a sole trader and the business are one person, so the owner is personally responsible for the business's debts (Chapter 1). The law does not separate them. Accounting does, because users of the records need to judge the business alone. A company, by contrast, is a separate person in law as well as in the books (Chapter 20).

> A lender wants the business judged alone, and a family may want private life left private.
The two sides of the line also have different readers. A lender wishes to know whether the business alone can repay a loan, and a tangle of private spending would hide the answer. The owner's family, for its part, may prefer that the shop's records do not describe the household's private spending. The business entity concept serves both: it keeps the shop's figures clean and the household's affairs private.

### Money Measurement

> You can add taka to taka, but you cannot add taka to friendliness.
@! Go slowly here.
Consider what a baker owns. There is an oven, a stock of flour, a cash drawer, a good location, a loyal group of customers, and a head baker whose bread is famous. All of these have value to the shop. Yet only some can be written as a number of taka that everyone would accept. A record is useful for adding and comparing only when all its items are measured in one unit.

> The money measurement concept records only facts that can be shown in money.
@ Money measurement concept | The rule that only facts that can be shown in money are recorded.
The rule is called the **money measurement concept**. It means that only facts that can be shown in money are recorded. The oven, the flour and the cash have prices, so they are recorded. The fame of the head baker has no price that two honest people would agree on, so it is not recorded, however valuable it may be. The accounts are therefore a record of money facts, not of everything that matters to a business.

> What the money records leave out can still matter, and careful users ask about it separately.
Users should remember what the money records leave out. A bank may lend more willingly to a bakery with famous bread and loyal customers than to a bakery whose records look the same but whose bread is poor. The records do not show the difference, so a careful lender asks about it separately. Accounts are an important source of facts, but not the only one. Some businesses add written explanations to their accounts for this reason (Chapter 5).

> Money itself changes in value, which is a limit of the rule.
The rule has limits. Money is not a fixed measure like a kilogram: when prices rise, one taka buys less than before, and the records of earlier years use taka of different buying power. Accounts usually ignore this change and treat every taka alike. Users must remember the limit when they compare figures from distant years. The events that the records do hold are called transactions, and Chapter 4 teaches them.

### Cost

> A price list in a shop is a fact; an opinion about worth is not.
@! Go slowly here.
Return to the three answers about the oven: Tk 70,000, Tk 100,000 and Tk 130,000. Two are opinions, and the third is a fact. The price paid, Tk 100,000, appears on the seller's invoice and in the buyer's payment, and anyone may check it. The dealer's offer and the third baker's pride are views that may change tomorrow. Accountants prefer the fact.

> In accounting, cost means the amount paid to get something.
@ Cost | In daily life, the price you pay. In accounting, the amount paid to get something, kept as its value in the books.
The rule is the cost concept. In accounting, **cost** means the amount paid to get something; it is the value of the item to the business at the time of purchase. Because the amount is not changed later to follow opinions, it is also called *historical cost*. By this rule, both Karim and Salma record the oven at Tk 100,000, whatever a dealer might offer.

> A small example shows that cost stays fixed while opinions change.
Take a small case. A shop buys a table for Tk 8,000. A year later a buyer offers Tk 6,500 for it, and a year after that another buyer offers Tk 9,000. What amount does the cost concept record for the table? It records Tk 8,000, the amount paid, and it does not change with either offer. The records of the shop therefore do not rise and fall with every opinion. Users can see what the shop paid, and they can form their own view of what the table is worth today.

> Some readers ask why current value does not replace cost, and the answer weighs trust against usefulness.
Some readers ask why accountants do not record the current value instead. Current value is more useful, since it tells what the item would bring today. But it is harder to measure and easier to bend. Two honest accountants may disagree about a value, but they cannot disagree about the price on an invoice. Accountants have therefore chosen trust first. The standards allow current value in a few cases where it can be measured reliably, and these cases are outside this chapter.

> Cost is checkable, so it supports trust.
The strength of cost is that it can be checked against a document, so two accountants reach the same figure. Its weakness is that cost may differ from what the item could be sold for today. For a long-lived item such as an oven, later chapters show how the cost is spread over the years in which the oven is used (Chapters 9 and 10). The standards also allow other measures in a few cases, which are outside this chapter.

> The bakery's oven and mixer will be recorded at the price paid.
:case Case file, illustrative. Mitali Rahman's bakery (Chapter 1) will buy an oven and a mixer in January 2026 (Chapter 4). Under the cost concept, the books of the bakery will record the oven and the mixer at the price that Mitali pays, and not at any later offer from a dealer. No figures are fixed yet.

### Going Concern

> A baker who plans to open tomorrow and next year buys differently from one who plans to close.
@! Go slowly here.
Ask two bakers what their ovens are worth. The first expects to bake bread for ten more years. The second must close the shop next week and will sell the oven at once. The second baker's answer should be lower, because a quick sale rarely gives a fair price. The expected future of the business changes the sensible answer.

> The going concern concept assumes the business will continue.
@ Going concern concept | The rule that a business is expected to continue for the foreseeable future.
Accountants make a simple assumption about the future. The **going concern concept** means the rule that a business is expected to continue for the foreseeable future. Under it, the oven is kept in the books at cost and used up over its working years, not valued at a quick-sale price. The assumption also lets the accounts treat a bill due next month as an ordinary matter of the business.

> The concept is dropped when closure is expected.
There is an exception. If the owner plans to close the business, or if the business cannot pay its debts and closure is likely, the assumption is no longer fair. The accounts must then be prepared in a different way, with items measured by what they would bring in a sale. Users should always ask whether the accounts assume a going concern, because the answer changes the meaning of every figure.

> The going concern concept is an assumption, not a promise.
The school canteen at the end of this section shows the exception at work. Notice a more general point: the going concern concept is an assumption, not a promise. It is made so that accounts can be prepared in a sensible way, and it is dropped the moment it ceases to be fair. Auditors, who check accounts independently (Chapter 2), always consider whether the assumption is reasonable.

### Further Concepts, in One Line Each

> Four more concepts are named here and taught later.
Four further ideas are named here so that the reader can recognise them. They are previewed in one line each, with a pointer to the place where they are taught. Do not try to master them now.

> The dual aspect concept says every transaction has two sides.
@ Dual aspect concept | The rule that every transaction has two sides, which is why the accounting equation always balances.
The **dual aspect concept** means the rule that every transaction has two sides. When a baker buys an oven for cash, the oven increases and the cash decreases. Chapter 4 uses this idea in the accounting equation, and Chapter 6 develops it into the system of double entry.

> Accrual and matching ask that costs be reported in the period that earned the revenue.
Two further ideas concern timing. The *accrual* idea says that earning and spending are recorded when they happen, not only when cash moves. The *matching* idea says that a cost is reported in the same period as the earnings it helped to produce. Chapter 9 teaches both. A fifth idea, which concerns using the same method from year to year, is taught in section 3.4.

> A small example shows why timing matters, before the idea is taught.
Here is a small example of the timing ideas, to be taught properly in Chapter 9. The bakery pays its helpers on the fifth of each month for work done in the month before. In January the helpers work for the whole month, but the pay arrives in February. If the bakery counted only the cash paid, January would look cheaper than it was, and February would look dearer. The two timing ideas place each cost in the month of the work. Do not try to master this now; recognise only that cash and cost may fall in different months.

> Prudence asks for care in counting profit and assets.
@ Prudence | Taking care not to overstate assets or profit, and not to understate liabilities or losses.
The next two ideas concern care and importance. **Prudence** means taking care not to overstate what the business owns or earns, and not to understate what it owes or may lose. For example, if the baker is unsure that a customer will pay, the baker should not count that payment as certain. Prudence guards users against figures that are too hopeful.

> Materiality allows small items to be treated simply.
@ Materiality | An item is material if leaving it out or getting it wrong could change a user's decision.
**Materiality** means that an item is material if leaving it out or getting it wrong could change a user's decision. The cost of one broom for the bakery is too small to change any decision, so it may be handled in the quickest way. The cost of a new oven would change decisions, so it must be recorded with care. Materiality is a matter of judgement, and it depends on the size of the business.

> Prudence and materiality both call for judgement, which standards help to guide.
Prudence and materiality differ from the other concepts in one way: neither gives a fixed answer. How careful is careful enough? How large is large enough to matter? Accountants answer with judgement, guided by the standards of section 3.4 and by the needs of the users of the particular accounts. A student should not expect a formula here.

The business entity concept keeps the business apart from its owner. The money measurement concept records only what can be shown in money. The cost concept records an item at the amount paid. The going concern concept assumes the business will continue. Four further ideas, dual aspect, accrual and matching, prudence and materiality, are named here and taught later.

The happiness of the pupils who eat there is valuable but is not recorded (money measurement). A table bought for Tk 8,000 is recorded at Tk 8,000 (cost). ## 3.3 Accounting Periods: Fiscal Year, Calendar Year and Interim Periods

> A baker cannot wait fifty years to learn whether the shop does well.
@! Go slowly here.
How long must a baker wait to know whether the bakery is doing well? If the baker waited until the shop closed for ever, perhaps after fifty years, the answer would arrive too late to help. Every school knows the remedy. It does not wait until a pupil leaves to report progress; it reports at the end of each term. Business uses the same remedy and cuts its long life into equal stretches of time.

> An accounting period is a fixed length of time for which results are reported.
@ Accounting period | A fixed length of time, such as a month or a year, for which results are reported.
Such a stretch of time is called an accounting period. An **accounting period** means a fixed length of time, such as a month or a year, for which results are reported. For example, the bakery may report the results of January, and then the results of February. Three groups need the periods. The owner wants early signs of trouble. The lender wants to see the trend. The tax authority asks for results at regular dates.

> The usual period is one year, and a business may choose which twelve months.
@ Fiscal year | A twelve-month accounting period that a business chooses, which may start in any month.
@ Calendar year | The twelve months from 1 January to 31 December.
The main period of most businesses is twelve months. A **fiscal year**, also called a financial year, means a twelve-month accounting period that a business chooses, and it may start in any month. A **calendar year** means the twelve months from 1 January to 31 December. In Bangladesh the government's own financial year runs from 1 July to 30 June.^1 A business often chooses a year-end that falls in a quiet season, when counting is easier. Tax law may set its own rules on the year used for tax; this book does not teach tax law.

^1: The government's financial year is the period of the national budget. A business may use a different year for its own accounts, but tax rules should be checked before a year-end is chosen.

> An interim period is shorter than a year and sits inside it.
@ Interim period | A period shorter than a year, such as a month or quarter, reported inside the year.
Within the year there may be shorter periods. An **interim period** means a period shorter than a year, such as a month or a quarter of three months, reported inside the year. A business may report each month for its own use and still produce one full report at the end of the year. The results of the interim periods, added together, give the results of the year.

:fig Two ways to cut the year: a calendar year and a year ending on 30 June, each divided into twelve monthly interim periods. | ch3_fig2.svg

> The figure shows twelve months in two different year frames.
Read the figure from left to right. The upper bar shows a calendar year. The lower bar shows a year that begins on 1 July. Each small division is one month, which is an interim period. The two bars both hold twelve months, and so both are fiscal years, though only the upper one is a calendar year. The result of either bar is the result of its twelve months added together.

> Cutting time into periods creates a new problem for events that cross the cut.
The cutting of time creates a difficulty that students often find confusing. Business is a continuous flow, and the cut falls in the middle of events. Rent paid in December may cover January. Wages earned in the last days of the month may be paid in the next month. Chapter 9 teaches how such items are placed in the right period. For now, remember that the choice of period is a convenience and not a fact of nature.

> A short calculation shows that interim results add up to the result of a longer period.
Suppose a small shop earns a profit of Tk 10,000 in the first month, Tk 12,000 in the second and Tk 8,000 in the third. *Given.* Three monthly profits. *Find.* The profit for the quarter, which is an interim period of three months. *Steps.* 10,000 + 12,000 + 8,000 = 30,000. *Answer.* The profit for the quarter is Tk 30,000. *Check.* The average month is Tk 10,000, and three months at that average give Tk 30,000, the same answer.

> Choosing a year-end is a practical decision, and it should then be kept.
Several reasons guide the choice of a year-end. A business may choose the end of a quiet season, since there is less trading to count. It may choose a date that matches the year of its main lender, or of owners who live abroad. It may choose a date that suits the busy season of its accountants. Whatever the reason, the choice should then be kept, because changing the year-end makes one period shorter or longer than the others and so spoils comparison.

> Using the same length of period each time allows fair comparison.
Finally, periods must be treated in the same way each time. A month should be compared with a month, and a year with a year. A business that reports for four weeks in one period and five weeks in the next period gives its users a comparison that is not fair. Section 3.4 returns to this need for sameness.

> The bakery's year is the calendar year, reported month by month.
:case Case file, illustrative. Mitali's bakery keeps its books on the calendar year, from 1 January to 31 December. For learning, its results are reported month by month. The first period of the bakery is the month from 1 January 2026 to 31 January 2026, and Chapters 4 to 12 follow this month. No figures are fixed yet.

> A recap of section 3.3 for the reader.
*Before moving on.* An accounting period is a fixed length of time for which results are reported. A fiscal year is any twelve months that the business chooses; a calendar year runs from 1 January to 31 December. An interim period is shorter than a year. *You should now be able to say:* "The bakery's year is the calendar year, and its monthly reports are interim periods."

## 3.4 Accounting Standards and Generally Accepted Principles

> The basic concepts are broad, and many questions remain after them.
The basic concepts are broad rules, and a broad rule leaves many questions. How should a loan from a relative be shown? At what point is a sale complete? Which costs belong in the price of an oven: the invoice amount only, or also the cost of bringing it to the shop and installing it? If each accountant answered such questions alone, the problem of the opening puzzle would return at a smaller scale.

> An accounting standard is a written rule from an official body.
@ Accounting standard | A written rule, issued by an official body, on how to record and report an item.
To give one answer to each such question, official bodies write detailed rules. An **accounting standard** means a written rule, issued by an official body, on how to record and report an item. For example, a standard may state that the cost of an oven includes the invoice price, the cost of delivery and the cost of installing it. Standards turn a broad concept into a clear instruction that every accountant can follow.

> A short calculation shows a standard at work on the cost of an oven.
Suppose that a standard says the cost of an oven includes the invoice price, the cost of delivery and the cost of installation, but not a fine for paying late. *Given.* For a different oven: invoice price Tk 100,000; delivery Tk 3,000; installation Tk 2,000; late-payment fine Tk 500. *Find.* The cost at which the oven is recorded. *Steps.* 100,000 + 3,000 + 2,000 = 105,000. The fine is left out. *Answer.* The oven is recorded at Tk 105,000. *Check.* Every accountant who follows the same standard reaches Tk 105,000, which is the purpose of the standard.

> GAAP is the whole body of rules and practices that accountants in a country follow.
@ GAAP | Generally accepted accounting principles: the set of rules and practices that accountants in a country agree to follow.
Concepts, standards, laws and long custom together form one body of rules. **GAAP**, short for generally accepted accounting principles, means the set of rules and practices that accountants in a country agree to follow. The word *accepted* shows that the profession and the regulators broadly agree. When a business says that its accounts follow GAAP, it says that they follow this shared body of rules.

> Concepts, standards and law are three layers, each narrower than the last.
It helps to see the three layers of rules together. Concepts are the broadest: they apply to every business. Standards are narrower and more detailed, and each deals with one kind of item. Law is the layer of compulsion. It may require businesses to follow the standards, and it adds rules of its own, such as the duty to keep records and to file tax returns. A business must satisfy all three layers, and where they conflict, the law decides.

> Three qualities explain what the rules protect: reliability, comparability and consistency.
Standards and concepts protect three qualities of accounting information. Each quality supports the trust that users place in the figures. We take them in turn, and then show how the rules, together, produce trust.

> Reliable information can be trusted to be accurate and free of bias.
@ Reliability | Information is reliable when users can trust it to be accurate and free of bias.
**Reliability** means that information is reliable when users can trust it to be accurate and free of bias. A figure that rests on an invoice is more reliable than a figure that rests on the owner's hope. The cost concept supports reliability, because cost can be checked. Prudence also supports reliability, because it guards against figures that are too hopeful.

> Comparable information lets users set one business or year beside another.
@ Comparability | Information is comparable when users can compare one business, or one year, with another.
**Comparability** means that information is comparable when users can compare one business, or one year, with another. A lender who studies two bakeries needs both to count in the same way. Standards supply this sameness across businesses. Without it, the bank officer of the opening puzzle could not say which baker did better.

> Consistency means using the same method from one period to the next.
@ Consistency | Using the same accounting method from one period to the next.
**Consistency** means using the same accounting method from one period to the next. A baker who counts the oven in one way in January should count it in the same way in February, unless there is a good reason to change. Then any change in the results comes from the business and not from the method. Chapters 10 and 14 show two places where a business chooses a method and must keep it.

> Together, standards make accounts reliable, comparable and consistent, and so worthy of trust.
Now the support for trust can be stated in a chain. Standards make the same event receive the same treatment, which makes information reliable and comparable. Consistency makes it comparable across time. Users who know that these rules were followed can rely on the figures without checking every payment themselves. The rules are therefore not paperwork for its own sake: they are the reason a stranger may trust a set of accounts.

:fig How rules support trust: events in the business are recorded under the basic concepts, reported under the standards, and then read by users who can trust and compare the figures. | ch3_fig3.svg

> Auditors check that accounts follow the rules, which gives users added confidence.
An auditor, introduced in Chapter 2, is an independent person who checks that accounts are fair and correct. The standards give the auditor a measuring stick: the auditor asks whether the accounts follow the concepts and the standards. A statement by an auditor that the accounts follow the rules gives users added confidence, especially lenders and investors who cannot examine the books themselves.

> International standards are written by one board and used in many countries.
@ IFRS | International Financial Reporting Standards: accounting standards issued by an international board and used in many countries.
Standards are written by official bodies, and many countries use the same ones. **IFRS** means International Financial Reporting Standards: accounting standards issued by an international board and used in many countries. The board that issues them sits in London and is called the International Accounting Standards Board. The history of the board is told at the end of this chapter.

> Bangladesh uses the international standards through its own institute and its own council.
In Bangladesh the professional body of accountants, the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Bangladesh, adopted the international standards for use in the country under the name Bangladesh Financial Reporting Standards. Later, the Financial Reporting Act 2015 created the Financial Reporting Council, which is given the task of issuing financial reporting standards in line with the international ones for public interest entities, such as large and listed companies.^2 This book teaches the ideas that all these standards share, and it names the rules of Bangladesh where they matter.

^2: The detailed status of each standard in Bangladesh has changed over time and may change again. A reader who must prepare accounts for a real business should read the current rules of the Financial Reporting Council.

> Which businesses must follow the full standards depends on law, size and kind of business.
Which businesses must follow the full standards depends on the law, on the size of the business and on its kind. A large company that offers shares to the public must follow them closely. A small sole trader, such as a neighbourhood bakery, is guided more by the tax rules and by the wishes of the bank. The ideas of this book, however, are the same at every size, and a student who knows them can read the accounts of any business.

> Standards have limits: they need judgement, they change, and they cannot stop dishonesty.
Standards also have limits that a careful reader should know. They require judgement, because no rule can foresee every case. They change, as business changes, so the current version must always be checked. Tax rules may differ from accounting rules, so one set of records may serve two purposes with two answers. And no standard can prevent a person who wishes to deceive; Chapter 8 shows one reason why records that agree may still be wrong.

> Standards change, so a reader should always ask which version applies.
Standards are revised from time to time. A treatment that was allowed ten years ago may be forbidden now, and a new standard may bring a new treatment. For this reason, accounts state which standards they follow, and a reader who compares figures across many years should check whether the rules changed in between.

> A recap of section 3.4 for the reader.
*Before moving on.* An accounting standard is a written rule from an official body. GAAP is the whole body of rules that accountants in a country follow. Standards protect reliability, comparability and consistency, and so support trust. IFRS are international standards, used in Bangladesh through its institute and its council. *You should now be able to say:* "Standards are necessary because without them two honest accountants could treat the same event in two ways, and users could not trust or compare the accounts."

## The Worked Case: Mitali's First Week

> The worked case applies entity, money measurement, cost and period to a bakery owner's papers.
:case Case file, illustrative. *Situation.* In her first week of trading, Mitali Rahman collects a pile of papers on her desk: the invoice for the oven and the mixer; a bill for flour and sugar; the shop's rent; her son's school fees; a dealer's message offering to buy the oven for less than she paid; a hotel that says it may order catering next month; the loyalty of her first customers; and the takings from her first bread sales. No figures are fixed yet. This is a question of what belongs in the books.

> The question asks where each paper belongs.
*Question.* Which of these items belong in the books of the bakery, and how is each to be measured? For which period are they reported?

> Reasoning first uses the entity and money measurement tests.
*Reasoning.* First apply the entity test: does the item concern the bakery, or Mitali's private life? The school fees are private, so they stay out. Next apply the money measurement test: can the item be shown in money that two people would accept? The loyalty of the customers and the hotel's possible order cannot, so they stay out. The oven, the mixer, the flour, the rent and the bread sales pass both tests.

> Cost fixes the amount of each item; the period fixes the time.
Then apply the cost concept. The oven and the mixer are recorded at the price on the invoice, and the dealer's message is ignored. Last, apply the period: all the items belong to the first accounting period, the month of January 2026, because the bakery reports month by month on the calendar year. The going concern concept tells Mitali that she may treat the oven as an item to be used over several years.

> The table is read row by row: the item, the decision, the reason.
Read the table one row at a time. The first column names an item. The second column gives the decision. The third column names the concept that decides it. Every row of the table can be justified by one of the four basic concepts.

> The conclusion and the check complete the case.
*Conclusion.* Five of the eight items enter the bakery's books, each at its checkable amount, for the month of January 2026. Three stay out, two because no reliable money figure exists and one because it is private. *Check.* Repeat the test on one item, the school fees. They concern Mitali's household, not the bakery, so the entity concept excludes them. The check agrees with the table.

## Summary

> The summary gathers the whole chapter in a few lines.
Accounting needs shared rules so that figures can be trusted and compared. The basic concepts are the business entity concept, the money measurement concept, the cost concept and the going concern concept. Further concepts, dual aspect, accrual and matching, prudence and materiality, are taught in later chapters. Results are reported for accounting periods: the fiscal year, the calendar year and shorter interim periods. Accounting standards turn broad concepts into detailed written rules. GAAP is the body of rules that accountants in a country follow. Reliability, comparability and consistency are what the rules protect. IFRS are international standards, and Bangladesh applies them through its institute and its council.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Cost is not the same as what something is worth today.
*Error: "Cost means what the item is worth today."* Cost means the amount paid at the time of purchase. Today's worth is a different figure, and the cost concept does not change the books to follow it.

> A valuable item is not always a recorded item.
*Error: "If something is valuable it must appear in the accounts."* The money measurement concept records only what can be shown in money. A famous head baker is valuable but has no checkable price, so the books do not hold it.

> The entity concept is not limited to companies.
*Error: "The business entity concept applies only to companies."* It applies to every business, including a sole trader, whose books are kept apart from the owner's household even though the law sees one person.

> A standard is not the same as a law, and a calendar year is not the only year.
*Error: "A standard is the same as a law, and the year must be the calendar year."* Standards are rules from official bodies; a country's law may require a business to follow them, but the two are not the same thing. A business may choose any twelve months as its fiscal year.

## In History

> The international board began in 1973 as a committee and became a board in 2001.
In June 1973, accountancy bodies from several countries founded the International Accounting Standards Committee, with the aim of reducing the differences between national ways of reporting. It issued a series of standards between 1973 and 2001. On 1 April 2001 it was replaced by the International Accounting Standards Board, which issues the standards now called International Financial Reporting Standards.^3 Before this work, a company's reports in one country could not easily be compared with those of a company in another.

^3: The older standards issued by the committee are labelled IAS, and the newer ones issued by the board are labelled IFRS. The whole set is commonly called IFRS.

> Bangladesh added its own law in 2015.
In Bangladesh the Parliament passed the Financial Reporting Act in September 2015. It created the Financial Reporting Council to regulate financial reporting and auditing for public interest entities and to issue standards in line with the international ones. Before that time the country's professional institute had been the main body adopting standards. The law shows that a rule written by accountants may later be backed by the state.
