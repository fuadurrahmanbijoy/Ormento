:chapter XVI | The Bank Reconciliation
:mood Two records may disagree without either being wrong.
:desc A bank reconciliation explains the difference between the cash book and the bank statement. It separates timing differences from items not yet recorded by the business, then brings both records to one adjusted balance.

> The same bank account can show two balances on the same day.

At the end of a month, Mitali Bakery looks at its cash book and receives a statement from its bank. The cash book says Tk 427,500. The bank statement says Tk 428,500. The difference is Tk 1,000. Has someone made an error? Not necessarily. A cheque may have been issued but not yet presented. A deposit may have been made but not yet credited. The bank may have charged the account or collected an amount that the bakery has not yet recorded. The two records can therefore disagree because they were prepared at different times and from different information.

> Reconciliation is the process of explaining why two records of the same bank account differ.

@ Bank reconciliation | A comparison of the cash book and bank statement that explains their difference and establishes an adjusted balance.

A **bank reconciliation** does not replace the cash book, and it does not correct the bank statement. It explains the difference between them. Some differences require only a note in the reconciliation because the bank or the business has not yet processed the same transaction. Other differences require an entry in the cash book. The distinction is the heart of the chapter.

## 16.1 The puzzle of two balances

> The cash book records the business's view of its bank account.

The cash book is maintained by the business. Its bank column records deposits, withdrawals, cheques, transfers and other movements known to the business. Chapter 15 showed how this column can be part of a cash book. When a cheque is written, the business records the payment. When a customer pays into the bank, the business records the receipt. The cash book therefore follows the transactions as the business understands them.

> The bank statement records the bank's view of the same account.

The bank keeps its own record. It receives deposits, pays cheques, charges fees, collects some amounts, and records other instructions. It then sends a statement to the account holder. The statement may contain transactions that have not yet appeared in the business's cash book. It may also omit a transaction that the business has recorded because the bank has not processed it yet.

> A difference is useful information when its cause can be identified.

Suppose the business deposits Tk 8,500 late on 31 March. The cashier records the deposit in the cash book immediately. If the bank processes it on 1 April, the March bank statement may not include it. The cash book will therefore be higher than the bank statement by Tk 8,500. Neither record is necessarily wrong. They are reporting the same event at different stages of processing.

> A cheque can create the opposite timing difference.

Suppose the bakery issues a cheque for Tk 12,000 to a supplier on 30 March. The bakery records the payment at once. If the supplier deposits the cheque on 2 April, the bank has not yet paid it on 31 March. The cash book is therefore lower than the bank statement by Tk 12,000. This item is an **outstanding cheque**.

@ **Outstanding cheque** | A cheque recorded by the business as paid but not yet presented to the bank for payment.

The word outstanding does not mean that the cheque is invalid. It means that the payment is still waiting to pass through the bank. Once the bank pays it, the difference disappears without a new entry in the business's cash book.

> A deposit in transit is a timing difference on the receipt side.

@ **Deposit in transit** | A deposit recorded by the business but not yet credited by the bank at the statement date.

A deposit in transit may also be called an outstanding deposit in some systems. The important idea is timing. The business has recorded the receipt, but the bank statement has not yet included it. When the bank credits the deposit, the two records move closer together.

## 16.2 Reading a bank statement: debit and credit memos

> Bank terminology can look reversed because the bank is writing from its own viewpoint.

A business normally thinks of money in its bank account as an asset. An increase in that asset is a debit in the business's cash account. But the bank's records treat the customer's deposit as an amount owed by the bank to the customer. From the bank's viewpoint, the customer's account is a liability. An increase in that liability is a credit in the bank's records. This is why a deposit may appear as a credit on the bank statement while the business records a debit to Cash.

@ **Credit memo** | A bank notification showing an amount credited to the customer's account.

A credit memo may arise when the bank collects a note or other amount for the customer, credits interest, or records another increase. The exact practice depends on the bank and the account. The accounting task is to identify what caused the credit and whether the business has already recorded it.

> A debit memo usually reduces the balance shown by the bank.

@ **Debit memo** | A bank notification showing an amount charged or debited to the customer's account.

Bank charges are a common example. If the bank deducts Tk 1,200 as a service charge, the statement records the deduction. If the bakery has not yet received or entered that information, its cash book will still show too high a balance. The reconciliation identifies the difference, and the business then records the bank charge in its books.

> A bank statement must be read from the bank's side before its entries are translated into the business's accounts.

This is why the words debit and credit should not be memorised without context. Ask first: whose books are being described? Then ask whether the customer's balance has increased or decreased. That reasoning prevents the common mistake of treating every bank credit as a credit to the business's Cash account.

## 16.3 Why the two records differ

> Most reconciliation differences fall into timing differences, unrecorded bank items, or errors.

The first class contains transactions already recorded by the business but not yet processed by the bank. Outstanding cheques and deposits in transit belong here. The second class contains items recorded by the bank but not yet entered in the cash book. Bank charges, interest, collections and dishonoured cheques are common examples. The third class contains mistakes in either record.

> A bank charge reduces the business's cash balance even when the business did not initiate the entry.

Suppose the bank deducts Tk 1,200. The business's cash book must eventually record the decrease. The journal entry is:

:table Bank charge entered after reconciliation

| Account | Debit (Tk) | Credit (Tk) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Bank Charges Expense | 1,200 | — |
| Cash | — | 1,200 |

The reconciliation itself explains the difference. The journal entry then brings the cash book up to date. The two jobs should not be confused.

> A dishonoured cheque reverses an expected collection.

@ **Dishonoured cheque** | A cheque received from a customer that the bank refuses to pay, so the expected collection is not completed.

Suppose a customer paid Tk 4,000 by cheque and the bakery recorded the receipt. If the cheque is later dishonoured, the bank removes Tk 4,000 from the account. The business must reduce Cash and restore the customer's receivable if the amount is still owed. A possible entry is:

:table Dishonoured customer cheque

| Account | Debit (Tk) | Credit (Tk) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Accounts Receivable | 4,000 | — |
| Cash | — | 4,000 |

The customer may later settle the amount by another method. Until then, the receivable exists again.

> Errors must be corrected in the records in which they occurred.

Suppose the bakery recorded a Tk 5,000 cheque as Tk 7,000 in its cash book. The cash book has been reduced by Tk 2,000 too much. The correction increases Cash by Tk 2,000 and records the appropriate debit to the account that was originally charged. The reconciliation should identify the error rather than conceal it inside a miscellaneous adjustment.

> A reconciliation is strongest when every difference has a named cause.

A list that says “difference Tk 1,000” is not a reconciliation. The accountant should be able to point to each amount and say what happened, which record contains it, and whether an entry is still needed. This makes the process useful for both accuracy and control.

## 16.4 Preparing the reconciliation statement

> A reconciliation starts with one balance and adjusts it until it reaches the balance shown by the other side.

@ **Adjusted balance** | The balance that remains after all relevant reconciling items have been considered.

There are two common starting points. The reconciliation can begin with the cash book balance and adjust for items appearing only in the bank statement, then deal with timing differences. It can also begin with the bank statement balance and adjust in the opposite direction. Both approaches should reach the same adjusted balance when the items are classified correctly.

> The direction of each adjustment depends on the balance from which the reconciliation starts.

Consider the bakery at 31 March. Its cash book shows Tk 427,500 and the bank statement shows Tk 428,500. The following items are identified:

:table Mitali Bakery reconciliation items at 31 March 2026

| Item | Tk | Treatment from cash-book balance |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Deposit in transit | 8,500 | Add |
| Outstanding cheque | 12,000 | Subtract |
| Bank interest not yet recorded | 700 | Add |
| Bank charges not yet recorded | 1,200 | Subtract |
| Dishonoured cheque not yet recorded | 4,000 | Subtract |
| Cash-book error: payment overstated | 2,000 | Add |

> The adjusted balance is found by applying the items, not by forcing the two original balances to agree.

Starting from Tk 427,500:

Tk 427,500 + Tk 8,500 − Tk 12,000 + Tk 700 − Tk 1,200 − Tk 4,000 + Tk 2,000 = Tk 421,500.

The bank statement balance used for the corresponding reconciliation must therefore contain the timing items in the opposite direction. If the statement shows Tk 425,000, then adding the deposit in transit and subtracting the outstanding cheque gives Tk 421,500. Thus both records lead to the same adjusted balance.

> The same item changes direction when the starting balance changes.

If the bank statement is the starting point, the deposit in transit is added and the outstanding cheque is subtracted when moving to the adjusted balance in this example. If the bank statement includes neither item, the direction depends on how the statement balance is defined and on the exact reconciliation format used. The safe method is to draw the timeline and ask which record already contains each transaction.

:fig A reconciliation as a bridge from two balances to one adjusted balance | figures/fig16_3_reconciliation_bridge.svg

> A good reconciliation can be checked by reversing the route.

After reaching the adjusted balance, reconstruct the other side from its original balance. If the same figure appears, the arithmetic and classification agree. If not, inspect the sign of each item before assuming the source record is wrong.

## 16.5 Entries after the reconciliation

> Items already recorded in the cash book do not need another journal entry.

An outstanding cheque has already reduced Cash in the business's books. A deposit in transit has already increased Cash. These are timing differences. The business waits for the bank to process them. Making another entry would double-count the transaction.

> Items found only on the bank statement normally require cash-book entries.

Bank charges, interest credited by the bank, collections made by the bank, and dishonoured cheques are examples. The accountant identifies the corresponding account and updates the cash book. If the business uses a journal rather than direct cash-book posting, the entry follows the same double-entry principle taught earlier.

> The reconciliation therefore has two outcomes: an explanation and a list of required entries.

The first outcome explains why the balances differ. The second updates the business's records. Once the cash book has been corrected, a new cash-book balance can be compared with the bank statement after timing differences are considered. The process should not leave unexplained amounts behind.

## 16.6 Why businesses reconcile: control

> Reconciliation is also a control over cash.

Cash is easy to move and easy to misstate. A comparison with an independent bank record can reveal missing entries, duplicated entries, unusual payments, recording errors and delays. The Bank of England's investigation into the 1995 collapse of Barings Bank identified serious failures of controls and managerial confusion among the reasons the true position was not detected earlier.^1 The lesson for a small business is not that a bakery resembles a bank. It is that independent checking can expose information that one record alone may not reveal.

> A reconciliation works best when responsibility and timing are clear.

A business should know who prepares the cash book, who receives the bank statement, who reviews the reconciliation, and how unusual items are followed up. A reconciliation prepared but never reviewed is weaker than one that becomes part of a regular control routine.

:case Situation: At 31 March 2026, Mitali Bakery has a cash-book balance of Tk 427,500 and a bank-statement balance of Tk 425,000. The differences are the six items listed in section 16.4.
Question: What adjusted balance should both records produce, and which items require cash-book entries?
Reasoning: The deposit in transit and outstanding cheque are timing differences. Bank interest, bank charges and the dishonoured cheque are bank-side items not yet recorded. The Tk 2,000 error belongs to the cash-book correction.
Conclusion: Both routes produce an adjusted balance of Tk 421,500. The bank interest, bank charges, dishonoured cheque and cash-book error require entries; the timing differences do not require duplicate entries.

## Summary

> Reconciliation explains a difference; it does not make the difference disappear.

The cash book is the business's record of its bank account. The bank statement is the bank's record from the bank's viewpoint. Their balances may differ because transactions are processed at different times, because the bank has recorded an item the business has not yet entered, or because one record contains an error. Outstanding cheques and deposits in transit are common timing differences. Bank charges, interest, collections and dishonoured cheques commonly require cash-book entries. The final test is that both sides, after proper adjustments, lead to one adjusted balance.

## Think it through

> The important question is always what happened, not which word was printed on the statement.

A customer deposits a cheque late in the afternoon. The business records it, but the bank credits it the next morning. Which balance changes first, and why?

A supplier has received a cheque but has not deposited it. Why can the bank statement show more cash than the cash book?

A bank charges Tk 900 that the business has not recorded. Explain the effect on the cash book and the reconciliation.

A cheque received from a customer is dishonoured. Explain why the original receipt cannot remain as a permanent increase in Cash.

## Where people go wrong

> Most errors come from forgetting whose viewpoint is being used.

**Treating a bank credit as a credit to Cash.** The bank statement is written from the bank's viewpoint. Translate the event into the business's accounts before deciding the debit or credit.

**Posting an outstanding cheque again.** The cash book already records the payment. It is a timing difference, not a second payment.

**Using the reconciliation to hide an error.** A known error should be identified and corrected in the relevant record.

**Forcing the arithmetic.** If the two sides do not agree after the adjustments, inspect the classification and signs. Do not invent a balancing figure.

## In history

> The Barings collapse showed why control procedures matter when information and responsibility become separated.

In 1995, Barings Bank collapsed after losses connected with unauthorised and concealed trading activities at its Singapore operation. The Bank of England's inquiry concluded that serious failures of controls and managerial confusion contributed to the failure to detect the true position earlier. The report was published in July 1995.^1 The case is far larger than an ordinary bank reconciliation, but it provides a useful historical reminder: records are valuable only when people compare, review and act on them.

^1: Bank of England, Banking Act Report 1995–96, inquiry into the collapse of Barings.
