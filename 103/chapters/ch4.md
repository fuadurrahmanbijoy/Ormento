:chapter IV | The Accounting Equation and Transactions
:mood Whose money is in the cash drawer?
:desc This chapter teaches what a business owns, what it owes, and what is left for its owner. It builds the accounting equation, shows what counts as a transaction, and follows the first month of the bakery as each event keeps the equation in balance.

> At the end of her first month, Mitali counts her cash and asks whether she is richer or poorer.
On 30 January 2026, after the last payment of her first month, Mitali Rahman counts the cash in the bakery's drawer and bank account. She finds Tk 400,000. Four weeks earlier she placed Tk 500,000 of her own savings in the shop. For a moment she believes that she is poorer by Tk 100,000.

> The shop is full of useful things that the cash once bought.
Then she looks around the shop. There is a new oven and a new mixer. There are sacks of flour and sugar in the store. Rent has been paid for three months. Bread and cakes have been sold, and a school has been served. The cash is lower than at the start, yet the shop holds many useful things that the cash became.

> Not all the cash is free, because some of it is already promised to others.
A second thought troubles her. Not all the Tk 400,000 is hers to spend. She still owes the supplier of the oven. A customer has paid in advance for cakes that she has not yet delivered. And a school still owes her money for a catering order. Money has moved in several directions, and some of it belongs to other people.

> Three questions about one month have three connected answers.
So three questions arise. How much does the bakery own? How much does it owe? And how much of the bakery truly belongs to Mitali? The three answers are different numbers, yet they are tied together by one rule that never fails. This chapter teaches that rule, and then uses it to follow every event of the month.

> This chapter relies on ideas from Chapters 1 and 3.
*You will need* the following earlier ideas: from Chapter 1, business, owner, cash, on account and debt; from Chapter 3, the money measurement concept and the business entity concept. The arithmetic is only addition and subtraction. The chapter is slow in sections 4.1, 4.2 and 4.4, because it contains absolute firsts.

## 4.1 Things Owned and Things Owed: Assets, Liabilities and Owner's Equity

> A student's bicycle, purse and small debt show the whole idea in miniature.
@! Go slowly here.
Begin with a student. Rina owns a bicycle that is worth Tk 12,000, and she has Tk 3,000 in her purse. She also owes her brother Tk 5,000, which she borrowed last month. How much of what she holds is truly hers? She owns Tk 15,000 in all. She owes Tk 5,000. So Tk 10,000 is hers. Everything in this chapter is this small example made larger and made exact.

> An asset is something of value that a business owns and expects to use to earn money.
@ Asset | Something of value that a business owns and expects to use to earn money.
The first word is *asset*. An **asset** means something of value that a business owns and expects to use to earn money. For example, the cash in the drawer is an asset, and so is the oven. Two earlier rules apply. By the business entity concept, the thing must belong to the business, not to the owner's household. By the money measurement concept, it must have a price that people would accept.

> Two kinds of asset appear at once in the bakery: equipment and supplies.
@ Equipment | Tools and machines, such as an oven, that a business uses for several years.
@ Supplies | Small items, such as flour or packaging, that a business keeps to use up soon.
The bakery will hold several kinds of asset. Cash is one. Money that a customer will pay later is another, and we shall name it in section 4.2. **Equipment** means tools and machines, such as an oven, that a business uses for several years. **Supplies** means small items, such as flour or packaging, that a business keeps to use up soon. The difference lies in the time of use: an oven lasts years, but flour lasts days.

> Some valuable things are not assets of the business, for three reasons.
There are limits to the word. A thing is not an asset of the business if it cannot be shown in money. The skill of the head baker is valuable, but no price can be agreed, so it is not an asset in the books. A thing is not an asset if the business does not own it: a shop that is rented belongs to the landlord. And a thing is not an asset if it belongs to the owner privately, such as Mitali's motorbike used only for family journeys.

> Pause and check on assets.
*Pause and check.* Which of the following are assets of the bakery: the oven; Mitali's family motorbike; the cash in the drawer; the head baker's skill; flour in the store? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> A recap of the first idea: assets.
*Before moving on.* An asset is something of value that the business owns and expects to use to earn money. Equipment lasts several years; supplies are used up soon. Things that cannot be shown in money, that the business does not own, or that belong to the owner privately, are not assets of the business. *You should now be able to say:* "An asset is a thing of value that the business owns."

> Rina's debt to her brother is the second idea: something owed.
@! Go slowly here.
Return to Rina. The Tk 5,000 that she owes her brother is not hers. It is a claim that her brother holds on what she has. If she sold her bicycle tomorrow, she would first repay him. A business has such claims too, and they are the second kind of item in the accounts.

> A liability is an amount a business owes to someone outside the business.
@ Liability | An amount that a business owes to someone outside the business.
A **liability** means an amount that a business owes to someone outside the business. It is a debt of the business, settled later by giving money, goods or service. For example, the bakery owes its supplier for the oven, and the supplier can demand payment. A bank loan would also be a liability. Notice the words *outside the business*: the owner is not an outsider, as the next idea shows.

> A liability may also be a promise to deliver goods or service, not only to pay money.
Not every liability is a sum of money owed to a supplier. Suppose a customer pays the bakery in advance for six cakes to be delivered later. The bakery has received the cash, but it has not yet done the work. It now owes the customer six cakes. This duty to deliver is a liability, and it will end when the cakes are delivered. Chapter 9 teaches this case in full; here the reader only needs to see that a debt can be paid in goods.

> A liability needs a present duty, not just a plan.
There is an important limit. A liability exists only when a duty already exists because of something that has happened. A plan to buy flour next week creates no liability today, because nothing has been bought. Wages that workers have not yet earned are not owed. The test is whether, on this date, someone outside the business can rightly demand money, goods or service.

> Pause and check on liabilities.
*Pause and check.* On 1 March the bakery orders flour that will arrive on 10 March. On 10 March the flour arrives and is not yet paid. On which date does the bakery owe money to the supplier? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> A recap of the second idea: liabilities.
*Before moving on.* A liability is an amount that the business owes to someone outside it. It may be settled by money, goods or service. It exists only when a duty already exists because of an event that has happened. *You should now be able to say:* "A liability is a debt of the business to an outsider."

> The part that is left for the owner is the third idea.
@! Go slowly here.
Now return to the question that Rina answered: how much is truly hers? She had Tk 15,000 of things and owed Tk 5,000, so Tk 10,000 was hers. That share has a name in business. It is the owner's claim on what the business holds, after the claims of outsiders are met.

> Owner's equity is the owner's claim: assets less liabilities.
@ Owner's equity | The owner's claim on the business: what is left after liabilities are taken from assets.
**Owner's equity** means the owner's claim on the business: what is left after liabilities are taken from assets. For Rina, it is Tk 15,000 less Tk 5,000, which is Tk 10,000. The same idea is sometimes called *net assets*. This book uses one name, owner's equity, for a sole trader. Partners and shareholders have their own names for the same idea, and the book will introduce them in Chapters 18 and 20.

:fig Rina's household: what she owns on one side, and the claims on those things on the other. The two sides are equal. | ch4_fig1.svg

> The figure shows two bars of equal height, owned and owed plus owned by Rina.
Read the figure from left to right. The left bar shows the things that Rina owns, from the bottom: cash of Tk 3,000 and a bicycle of Tk 12,000. The right bar shows who has a claim on those same things: her brother for Tk 5,000, and Rina herself for Tk 10,000. The two bars have the same height, Tk 15,000. They must, because every taka of a thing is claimed by someone.

> Owner's equity can fall to zero or below zero.
Owner's equity is not always a positive number. If Rina's bicycle were damaged and fell in value to Tk 1,000, her things would be worth Tk 4,000 against a debt of Tk 5,000. Her equity would be minus Tk 1,000: she would owe more than she holds. For a business, such a result means that the owner has no claim left and that the outsiders may not be paid in full.

> Different users watch different parts: the lender watches liabilities, and the owner watches equity.
The idea looks different from different seats. A lender asks whether the assets are large enough to repay the liabilities, and so watches the gap between them. A supplier asks the same. The owner asks how much of the business is truly hers, and so watches owner's equity. One set of figures answers both people, but each reads a different part of it.

*Before moving on.* Assets are what a business owns, liabilities are what it owes to outsiders, and owner's equity is what is left for the owner. ## 4.2 The Accounting Equation

> The bicycle story already contains the main rule of this subject.
@! Go slowly here.
Rina's story already holds the main rule of this subject. What she owned, Tk 15,000, was equal to what she owed, Tk 5,000, together with what was left for her, Tk 10,000. Say it in words: what is owned equals what is owed plus what is the owner's. A business is the same, only larger, and the rule is written in a short form.

> The accounting equation says that assets equal liabilities plus owner's equity.
@ Accounting equation | The rule that assets always equal liabilities plus owner's equity.
The **accounting equation** means the rule that assets always equal liabilities plus owner's equity. In words: Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity. In symbols: A = L + OE, where A stands for assets, L for liabilities and OE for owner's equity. For Rina, it reads Tk 15,000 = Tk 5,000 + Tk 10,000. The left side lists what the business has. The right side lists who has claims on it.

> The equation balances because every asset has someone's claim behind it.
Why does the equation always balance? Every asset in a business came from somewhere. Either an outsider supplied it, and so holds a claim, which is a liability. Or the owner supplied it, or earned it for the owner, and so holds a claim, which is equity. There is no third source. If every asset has one of these two kinds of claim, the claims added together must equal the assets. This is the dual aspect concept of Chapter 3: the same value is seen from two sides.

:fig The accounting equation shown as a balance: the bakery after its first two events. The assets on one side equal the claims on the other. | ch4_fig2.svg

> The figure is read as a balance with two pans, one for what the bakery has and one for the claims.
Read the figure as a balance. The left pan holds the assets of the bakery after its first two events: cash of Tk 400,000 and equipment of Tk 240,000, a total of Tk 640,000. The right pan holds the claims: Tk 140,000 owed to the supplier and Tk 500,000 that belongs to Mitali, also a total of Tk 640,000. The beam stays level because the two totals are equal.

> The equation can be rearranged to find any one part.
The equation can be rearranged. If assets and liabilities are known, owner's equity is found by taking liabilities from assets: OE = A − L. If assets and equity are known, liabilities are A − OE. Rina's equity was found in this way. The rearranged forms are the same rule; they are written differently to answer different questions.

> A short calculation applies the rearranged form to the bakery on 2 January.
*Given.* On 2 January, after the second event, the bakery has assets of Tk 640,000 and liabilities of Tk 140,000. *Find.* Owner's equity. *Formula.* In words: owner's equity equals assets less liabilities. In symbols: OE = A − L. *Steps.* OE = 640,000 − 140,000 = 500,000. *Answer.* Owner's equity is Tk 500,000. *Check.* This equals the amount that Mitali contributed, and nothing has yet been earned or spent, so the answer is reasonable.

> Owner and lender read the two sides of the equation for different purposes.
The two sides of the equation answer different questions for different readers. A lender reads the left side to see what could be sold to repay a debt, and the liabilities on the right to see how much is due. An owner reads the equity to see what is truly hers. Because the sides must be equal, a larger liability means a smaller equity for the same assets. Borrowing more does not make the owner richer.

> A company calls the owners' claim stockholders' equity.
@ Stockholders' equity | The owners' claim in a company; the same idea as owner's equity.
In a company, the same idea has a different name. **Stockholders' equity**, also called shareholders' equity, means the owners' claim in a company; it is the same idea as owner's equity. The equation is unchanged. Chapter 20 gives the full treatment.

> Owners put money in and the amount is called capital.
@ Invest | To put money or assets into a business.
@ Capital | In daily life, money or wealth in general. In accounting, money or assets that the owner puts into the business to start or grow it.
Now we begin to use the equation. An owner who puts money or assets into a business is said to **invest** them. The amount invested is called **capital**. In this book, capital means only what the owner has contributed. The whole of the owner's claim, which also includes profit that is kept and is reduced by withdrawals, is owner's equity.^1

^1: Some books use the word capital for the whole owner's claim as well. The two meanings agree on the day the owner invests, and differ only after the business earns or the owner withdraws. This book keeps two words for two ideas.

> A transaction is an event with money value that changes the business.
@ Transaction | An event that has money value and changes a business's assets, liabilities, or equity.
Each event that has money value and changes the items of the equation is called a **transaction**. For example, the owner investing cash is a transaction, and so is buying an oven. Section 4.3 examines what counts as one and what does not. For now, the rule is that after each transaction, the equation must still balance. We try the rule on a very small business.

> Habib's tea stall is the first example, with four events and round numbers.
Habib opens a tea stall. The first example uses four transactions and round figures, so that only the method must be learned. *Given.* (1) Habib invests Tk 10,000 in cash. (2) He buys a kettle and a stove for Tk 4,000 cash. (3) He buys tea leaves and sugar worth Tk 1,000 on account from a supplier. (4) He pays Tk 600 of that debt in cash. *Find.* The total of assets, of liabilities and of owner's equity after each transaction, and whether the equation holds.

> The formula is the equation, applied after every event.
*Formula.* In words: Assets equal Liabilities plus Owner's equity. In symbols: A = L + OE. *Steps.* We take each transaction in turn, name the items that change, and write the new totals.

> Transaction 1: cash and owner's equity both rise by Tk 10,000.
Transaction 1. Cash, an asset, rises by Tk 10,000. Nobody outside the business has a claim, so no liability appears. The claim belongs to Habib, so owner's equity rises by Tk 10,000. Totals: A = 10,000; L = 0; OE = 10,000.

> Transaction 2: one asset replaces another, so totals do not change.
Transaction 2. Cash falls by Tk 4,000 and equipment rises by Tk 4,000. One asset has replaced another. The total of assets does not change, and nothing on the right side changes. Totals: A = 10,000; L = 0; OE = 10,000.

> Transaction 3: supplies and the debt to the supplier rise together.
@ Accounts payable | Money that the business owes to suppliers for goods or services bought on account.
Transaction 3. Supplies, an asset, rise by Tk 1,000. Habib has paid nothing yet, so his debt to the supplier rises by Tk 1,000. This debt has a name. **Accounts payable** means money that the business owes to suppliers for goods or services bought on account. Totals: A = 11,000; L = 1,000; OE = 10,000.

> Transaction 4: paying part of the debt lowers cash and the debt together.
Transaction 4. Habib pays Tk 600 in cash. Cash falls by Tk 600, and accounts payable falls by Tk 600, leaving Tk 400 owed. Totals: A = 10,400; L = 400; OE = 10,000.

Read the table one row at a time. The first column gives the number of the transaction. The next column names the event. The last three columns give the totals of the equation after that event. In every row, the assets equal the liabilities plus the owner's equity. *Answer.* The equation holds after every transaction, and Habib's equity stays at Tk 10,000 because nothing yet has earned or reduced it. *Check.* After transaction 4, cash is Tk 5,400, equipment Tk 4,000 and supplies Tk 1,000. > Now the bakery: the first four transactions of January 2026.
Now the bakery. The case file lists thirteen transactions for January 2026, and this section follows the first six. *Transaction 1, on 1 January:* Mitali invests Tk 500,000 in cash. Cash rises by Tk 500,000 and owner's equity rises by Tk 500,000. *Transaction 2, on 2 January:* she buys an oven and a mixer for Tk 240,000, paying Tk 100,000 in cash and owing Tk 140,000 on account. Equipment rises by Tk 240,000, cash falls by Tk 100,000, and accounts payable rises by Tk 140,000. Assets rise by Tk 140,000 net, and liabilities rise by Tk 140,000.

> Transactions 3 and 4 each replace cash by another asset.
*Transaction 3, on 3 January:* she pays Tk 45,000 in cash for three months' rent in advance, January to March. She has paid for the right to use the shop for a time that has not yet come, and we record this right as an asset named Prepaid Rent. Chapter 9 explains why. Cash falls by Tk 45,000, and Prepaid Rent rises by Tk 45,000. *Transaction 4, on 5 January:* she buys baking supplies for Tk 30,000 cash. Cash falls by Tk 30,000, and supplies rise by Tk 30,000. In both, one asset has replaced another, and the totals do not change.

> Transactions 5 and 6 are earnings, which raise owner's equity.

@ Accounts receivable | Money that customers owe to the business for goods or services sold on account.
*Transaction 5, on 10 January:* the bakery sells baked goods for Tk 90,000 in cash. Cash rises by Tk 90,000, and nobody outside the business has a claim, so no liability appears. The new value belongs to the owner, so owner's equity rises by Tk 90,000. *Transaction 6, on 15 January:* a school orders catering worth Tk 40,000, to be paid later. The school now owes the bakery this money, and we call it **accounts receivable**: money that customers owe to the business for goods or services sold on account. Accounts receivable rises by Tk 40,000, and owner's equity rises by Tk 40,000. Section 4.4 teaches this kind of change in full.

:box IN SHORT: THE ACCOUNTING EQUATION
Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity. What the business has is equal to the claims on it: the claims of outsiders, which are liabilities, and the claim of the owner, which is owner's equity. After every transaction, the two sides are still equal.

> The equation holds after every event, yet it can still hold when a figure is wrong.
There are limits that careful students must keep in mind. First, the equation holds after each single transaction, not only at the end of a month. Second, the equation can balance even when an amount is wrong. If Mitali wrote Tk 54,000 in place of Tk 45,000 on both sides of an entry, the two sides would remain equal and the mistake would not be seen. Chapter 8 returns to this limit. Third, the equation holds only when both effects of an event are recorded. An event recorded on one side only breaks the equation, and that break is how many mistakes are found.

*Pause and check.* Assets are Tk 90,000 and liabilities are Tk 35,000. What is owner's equity? The owner then invests another Tk 10,000 in cash. What are the new totals of assets, liabilities and owner's equity? (The answers are at the back of the book.)

> A recap of section 4.2 for the reader.
*Before moving on.* The accounting equation is Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity. It balances because every asset has either an outsider's claim or the owner's claim behind it. An event that has money value and changes the items is a transaction, and after each transaction the equation must still hold. *You should now be able to say:* "After every transaction, what the business has still equals what is claimed on it."

## 4.3 What Counts as a Transaction

> A neighbour's promise to buy bread each morning does not change the bakery's accounts.
Before the bakery opens, a neighbour tells Mitali: "I shall buy bread from you every morning." Mitali is pleased. Does the accounting equation of the bakery change at that moment? It does not. No money has moved, no bread has been handed over, and nothing owned or owed has changed. The promise is useful news, but it is not yet an event that the books record. Section 4.2 gave the word *transaction*; this section shows which events qualify.

> Four tests decide whether an event is recorded.
An event is recorded as a transaction when it passes four tests. First, it concerns the business, and not the private life of the owner (the business entity concept). Second, it has a money value that people would accept (the money measurement concept). Third, it changes at least one item of the accounting equation: an asset, a liability or owner's equity. Fourth, it can be supported by evidence, such as an invoice or a receipt. Chapter 7 names these papers and shows how they are used.

:fig The test for a transaction: an event is recorded only if it passes all three questions in turn. | ch4_fig4.svg

> The figure turns the first three tests into three questions asked in order.
Read the figure from the top. The event first meets a question about the business and the owner. If the answer is no, the event leaves the diagram to the right and is not recorded. If the answer is yes, it passes down to the next question, and then to the third. Only an event that passes all three is a transaction, and it goes forward to analysis and recording. The fourth test, evidence, is checked when the event is recorded.

> Evidence protects both the owner and the users of the records.
The fourth test, evidence, matters more than it first seems. A record without a document can be questioned by anyone, including a tax officer or a lender, and it cannot be defended. A supplier's invoice, a receipt for cash paid, or a bank record shows that the event happened and gives its amount. This is the cost concept at work again: the amount comes from a checkable fact. Keeping the papers also helps the owner to find and correct mistakes later.

> The main business events are transactions, and a table shows some that are not.
Most of the ordinary events of a business are transactions: the owner invests, the business buys equipment or supplies, buys on account, pays a debt, sells for cash or on account, receives payment from a customer, pays wages and bills. In the bakery, all thirteen events of January 2026 are transactions. Other events are not. The next table shows a mixture, so that the tests can be practised. Read each row, decide, and then read the reason.

> The table is read row by row: the event, the decision, then the reason.
Each row of the table follows one pattern. The first column names the event. The second gives the decision. The third gives the reason in terms of the tests above. Notice that a promise or an agreement, however important, is not a transaction until something is done or something is owed. The agreement to hire a baker becomes part of the accounts only when the baker works and wages become due.

> Three further events show the tests on borderline cases.
Three more cases show the tests. A customer returns to say that she liked the cakes: this is not a transaction, since nothing has moved and no money is involved. In some later month the oven breaks, and a repair costing Tk 2,000 is paid in cash: this is a transaction, since cash falls and owner's equity falls. Mitali buys a gift for a friend from her own money: this is not a transaction of the bakery, since it is private. In each case, ask the questions of the figure in order.

> Both sides of a sale are transactions, but each business records its own side.
There is another point of view. One event may be a transaction for two businesses at the same time. When the bakery buys flour on account, the bakery records supplies and accounts payable. The flour seller records a sale and accounts receivable. Each business records only its own side, because the business entity concept keeps each business apart. The same invoice is therefore recorded twice, in two sets of books.

> An event may become a transaction later, when something is done or owed.
Timing needs care. A promise today may become a transaction tomorrow, when the bread is delivered or the money is paid. The question of exactly when to record an event that spreads across several days is important, and Chapter 9 gives a full answer. In this chapter the thirteen events are recorded on the day the money or the goods change hands.

> A promise is ignored; a cash sale is recorded.
:case Case file, illustrative. On the day before the shop opens, a neighbour promises to buy bread each morning. The bakery's accounts do not change. On 10 January 2026 the bakery sells baked goods for Tk 90,000 in cash. This is a transaction: cash and owner's equity both rise by Tk 90,000.

> A recap of section 4.3 for the reader.
*Before moving on.* A transaction is an event that concerns the business, has money value, changes at least one item of the accounting equation, and can be supported by evidence. A promise or an agreement is not a transaction until something is done or owed. *You should now be able to say:* "An event is recorded only if it changes what the business owns, owes or is owed to the owner, and can be shown in money."

## 4.4 Revenue, Expenses and Drawings: How Equity Changes

> Takings in the cash drawer raise the question: whose is the new value?
@! Go slowly here.
Look again at transaction 5. The bakery sold bread for Tk 90,000 in cash. Cash, an asset, rose by Tk 90,000. Nobody outside the business has a claim on that new value, so no liability appeared. Whose, then, is it? The accounting equation gives the answer. If assets rise and liabilities do not, owner's equity must rise by the same amount. The earnings belong to the owner.

> Think of a market stall holder at the end of the day.
Picture a stall holder who counts the takings at the end of the day. She takes away the cost of the goods she sold and the small costs of the day. What remains is her gain. If the day was good, her share in the stall has grown; if it was bad, her share has fallen. Business does the same, but it keeps careful names for each part, and this section teaches three of them.

> Revenue is money earned from selling goods or performing services.
@ Revenue | Money earned by a business from selling goods or performing services.
**Revenue** means money earned by a business from selling goods or performing services. The sale of bread for Tk 90,000 is revenue, and so is the catering for the school. Business English also uses the words *sales* and *income* for this idea. This book uses *revenue* throughout, and from Chapter 13, where a business buys goods to resell, it uses *sales* for the selling price of those goods. Revenue increases owner's equity.

> Revenue is earned when the work is done, not only when cash arrives.
Revenue is not the same as cash received. On 15 January the bakery catered for a school, billed on account, for Tk 40,000. The school has not paid, but the bakery has done the work and the school owes the money. The bakery has earned revenue. Owner's equity rises by Tk 40,000, and accounts receivable, an asset, rises by the same amount.

> A receipt of cash in advance is not yet revenue.
The opposite case also exists. On 22 January a customer pays the bakery Tk 12,000 in advance for six weekly cake deliveries. Cash has arrived, yet the bakery has not done the work. It owes the customer six cakes, so the Tk 12,000 is a liability and not revenue. It becomes revenue only as the cakes are delivered. Chapter 9 follows the idea in detail. For now, remember: cash and revenue are different things.

> Pause and check on revenue.
*Pause and check.* A baker receives Tk 5,000 in cash today for bread sold today, and Tk 3,000 in cash today for cakes to be delivered next week. How much revenue does the baker earn today? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> An expense is a cost that a business uses up to earn revenue.
@ Expense | A cost that a business uses up in order to earn revenue.
@! Go slowly here.
Now the other side of earning. **Expense** means a cost that a business uses up in order to earn revenue. Wages paid to a helper are an expense, because the helper's work helped to earn revenue. So is the electricity bill. An expense decreases owner's equity. When the bakery pays Tk 6,000 for electricity on 25 January, cash falls by Tk 6,000, and owner's equity falls by Tk 6,000.

> Not every payment is an expense: buying an asset or paying a debt is different.
Careful readers must not confuse payment with expense. When the bakery paid Tk 240,000 for an oven and a mixer, it bought assets, and it is still the owner of the same value; the cash changed into equipment. When the bakery pays a supplier part of what it owes, it reduces a liability and reduces cash; its equity is not touched. The cost of an oven or of flour becomes an expense only as it is used up in earning revenue. Chapter 9 shows how.

> Drawings are the owner's withdrawals for private use, and they reduce equity.
@ Drawings | Cash or goods that the owner withdraws from the business for personal use.
**Drawings** means cash or goods that the owner withdraws from the business for personal use. They are also called withdrawals. On 30 January 2026, Mitali withdraws Tk 20,000 in cash. Cash falls by Tk 20,000, and owner's equity falls by Tk 20,000. Salma's family rice, in the opening puzzle of Chapter 3, was a drawing and not a cost of her shop.

> Drawings are not an expense, because they do not help to earn revenue.
Why are drawings not an expense? An expense is used up to earn revenue. Drawings do not help to earn anything; they merely transfer part of the owner's claim from the business to the owner's household. The two have the same effect on owner's equity, a fall, but different reasons. Keeping them apart lets the owner see both what the business cost to run and how much she took from it.

> The expanded equation shows owner's equity as capital plus revenue, less expenses and drawings.
We can now write the equation in a longer form that shows what changes owner's equity. Owner's equity equals capital, plus revenue, less expenses, less drawings. The full equation is Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Revenue − Expenses − Drawings. Capital and revenue raise owner's equity. Expenses and drawings lower it. The figure below shows the five parts in order.

> The figure shows how each part raises or lowers owner's equity.
Read the figure from the left. The two sides of the equals sign are the same two sides as before. The right side is now divided: liabilities, and then four parts that together make owner's equity. The words *raises* and *lowers* show the direction in which each part moves owner's equity. The line below the figure gives the profit of the period: revenue less expenses.

> Profit is revenue less expenses; a negative result is a loss.
We can now say exactly what profit is. Chapter 1 introduced the word, and we can now state it with precision: profit for a period is revenue less expenses for the same period. If the result is negative, there is a loss. Profit increases owner's equity, and loss decreases it. Drawings are not part of the calculation of profit. Chapter 5 builds the reports that show profit.

> A short calculation shows profit for one shop and loss for another.
*Given.* Shop A has revenue of Tk 25,000 and expenses of Tk 18,000. Shop B has revenue of Tk 12,000 and expenses of Tk 15,000. *Find.* The result of each shop. *Formula.* In words: profit equals revenue less expenses. In symbols: P = R − E. *Steps.* Shop A: 25,000 − 18,000 = 7,000. Shop B: 12,000 − 15,000 = −3,000. *Answer.* Shop A has a profit of Tk 7,000. Shop B has a loss of Tk 3,000. *Check.* Shop B's expenses are larger than its revenue, so a negative result, a loss, is expected.

> A lender reads expenses differently from an owner, though both read the same figures.
Different readers see different things in the same expenses. The owner sees wages and electricity as costs to control. The lender sees the same costs as claims that must be met before money is left to repay a loan. The employee sees wages as income. One word, *expense*, therefore looks different from each seat, but the amount is the same for all.

> An expense may be used up before it is paid, which a later chapter teaches.
Sometimes a cost is used up before the cash is paid, as when helpers work for a week and are paid in the next week. The expense belongs to the week of the work, even though the cash moves later. The opposite case, a cost paid before it is used, as with rent paid in advance, is also met in Chapter 9. In this chapter, the thirteen transactions of January are plain enough that cash and expense fall on the same day.

## 4.5 Analysing Transactions Step by Step: The Summary of Transactions

> A fixed routine of four questions turns any event into numbers.
@ Transaction analysis | Finding which items a transaction changes, and by how much.
Sections 4.2 to 4.4 analysed transactions one at a time, in prose. Accountants use a fixed routine for the same task, so that no step is forgotten. **Transaction analysis** means finding which items a transaction changes, and by how much. The routine has four questions. After the fourth, the event is fully recorded in the summary of transactions, a table of running totals.

> The four questions are listed in order: what, which, how much, still balanced.
The four questions are these. First, what happened, and is it a transaction (section 4.3)? Second, which items change, and is each an asset, a liability or part of owner's equity? Third, by how much does each change, and in which direction, up or down? Fourth, does the equation Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity still hold? Always name at least two items in the second question, because every transaction has two sides. If only one item seems to change, a second one has been forgotten.

> A table of patterns shows the five usual ways in which items move together.
Most transactions fall into a few patterns. The next table shows five of them, each with an example from this chapter. Learn to recognise the pattern first; the amounts follow. Other patterns exist, such as an expense that is owed and not yet paid, and Chapter 9 teaches them.

### Common Slips

> Slips of direction: a rise recorded as a fall, or the wrong side changed.
The first common slip is a wrong direction. A student pays a debt of Tk 60,000 and increases accounts payable instead of reducing it. The check is quick: if the equation no longer balances, a direction is wrong. Say each change aloud in words, "cash falls, debt falls," before writing a number.

> Slips of omission: a forgotten second item.
The second slip is a forgotten item. When the oven is bought partly for cash and partly on account, three items change, not two: equipment, cash and accounts payable. A student who records only equipment and cash will find that the equation fails by Tk 140,000. Count the items before the amounts.

> Slips of confusion: cash against on account, and payment against expense.
The third slip mixes cash with credit. A sale on account raises accounts receivable and not cash. The fourth slip treats payments as expenses: buying supplies, paying a debt and withdrawing cash for the owner are not expenses. The fifth treats an advance receipt as revenue. Ask in each case: has the work been done, and has the cost been used up?

## Summary

> The summary gathers the whole chapter in a few lines.
An asset is something of value that the business owns; equipment and supplies are two kinds. A liability is an amount owed to someone outside the business. Owner's equity is the owner's claim: assets less liabilities. The accounting equation states that assets equal liabilities plus owner's equity, and it holds after every transaction. A transaction is an event that concerns the business, has money value and changes the items of the equation. Owner's equity rises with capital and revenue, and falls with expenses and drawings. Transaction analysis asks four questions and records the result in a summary of transactions. Accounts receivable and accounts payable are the amounts owed to and by the business on account. In a company the owners' claim is called stockholders' equity.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Owner's equity is not the cash the owner invested.
*Error: "Owner's equity is the money the owner invested."* Capital is the amount invested. Owner's equity is the whole claim, and it also includes revenue earned and is reduced by expenses and drawings.

> A liability is not only a bank loan.
*Error: "A liability is only money owed to a bank."* A liability is any amount owed to an outsider. It includes money owed to suppliers and promises to deliver goods or service for money already received.

> Cash received is not always revenue, and cash paid is not always an expense.
*Error: "Cash received is revenue; cash paid is an expense."* Cash received in advance is a liability. Cash paid for equipment or supplies buys an asset, and cash paid to settle a debt reduces a liability. Revenue and expense depend on work done and cost used up, not on the date of the cash.

> Drawings are not an expense.
*Error: "Money the owner withdraws is an expense of the business."* Drawings are a reduction of the owner's claim, not a cost of earning revenue. They are shown apart from expenses, so that the true cost of running the business can be seen.

## In History

> Pacioli taught merchants to begin their books by listing what they own and owe.
In 1494, the Italian teacher of mathematics Luca Pacioli published in Venice a large book on arithmetic and geometry. One part of it was a short treatise on bookkeeping, describing the method then used by Venetian merchants. The treatise advises the merchant to begin the books by listing everything that he owns and owes, with a value for each.^2 A later Venetian author, Domenico Manzoni, published in 1540 a bookkeeping text whose worked example opens in the same way, with a complete inventory of a merchant's possessions on one date.

^2: Pacioli's bookkeeping treatise is called *Particularis de computis et scripturis*, and it forms part of his *Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalita* (Venice, 1494).

> The list of owned and owed is still the first step of every set of books.
The merchant's list of what he owned and owed is the accounting equation in its earliest form. Subtract the second list from the first, and the result is what belongs to the merchant himself. More than five hundred years later, every set of books still begins from the same two lists. Chapter 6 shows how the method of Venice records the two sides of every transaction.

## Review Questions

> Question on the three items.
1. Define asset, liability and owner's equity. Give one example of each from a bakery.

> Question on the equation.
2. State the accounting equation in words and in symbols. Explain why it always balances.

> Question on equity and capital.
3. Explain the difference between capital and owner's equity. What name does a company give to the owners' claim?

> Question on transactions.
4. What is a transaction? Give two examples of events that are transactions and two that are not, with reasons.

> Question on revenue, expense and drawings.
5. Define revenue, expense and drawings. Explain how each changes owner's equity, and why drawings are not an expense.

> Question on receivable and payable.
6. Distinguish accounts receivable from accounts payable. Which is an asset and which is a liability?

> Question on advance receipts.
7. A customer pays in advance for goods to be delivered next month. Is the payment revenue? Explain.

> Question on analysis.
8. List the four questions of transaction analysis. Use them to analyse the purchase of equipment for Tk 50,000, of which Tk 20,000 is paid in cash and the rest is owed.
