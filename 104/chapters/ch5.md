:mood How hard do buyers push back when a price moves?
:desc The law of demand says that a higher price brings smaller purchases. It does not say how much smaller. This chapter builds a number that measures the reaction, applies it to price, income and the prices of other goods, and shows why every seller, farmer and tax collector needs it.

> Two tea stalls raise their prices by the same amount and meet very different results.

Two tea stalls stand side by side near a bus station in a small Bangladeshi town. On the same morning each raises the price of a cup by Tk 2. By evening the first stall has lost almost half of its customers. The second stall has lost hardly any, and its owner finds more money in the cash box than before.

> The law of demand gives only the direction of the change, not its size.

The law of demand, met in Chapter IV, predicts that both stalls will sell fewer cups, and both did. But the law speaks only of direction. It says that quantity falls. It does not say whether the fall is large or small, and a seller who must choose a price needs to know which. To tell the two stalls apart, we need a number. This chapter builds that number and uses it to answer four questions. Why did the stalls differ? Why do governments tax some goods heavily and others lightly? Why can a good harvest hurt the farmer? When does a price cut help a seller, and when does it not?

> This chapter rests on Chapter IV and on percentages.

*You will need* the law of demand and the demand curve (Chapter IV), and percentage change, which the appendix *Maths you will use* explains.

## Why Measure Reaction?

> A household reacts to a price rise in salt very differently from one in mangoes.

@! Go slowly here.

Start with a household. In one month the price of salt rises by 10 per cent, and the price of mangoes also rises by 10 per cent. The family buys almost the same amount of salt as before, because food without salt is hard to eat. But the family buys far fewer mangoes, because many other fruits are available. The two prices moved by the same amount. The two reactions were very different.

> To compare reactions, count them as percentages, not as units.

Now put numbers on it. Salt rises from Tk 20 to Tk 22 a kilogram, and the family's purchase falls from 100 kg to 99 kg. Mangoes rise from Tk 100 to Tk 110 a kilogram, and the purchase falls from 20 kg to 16 kg. The raw changes are 1 kg of salt and 4 kg of mangoes. We cannot compare them. A kilogram of salt is not a kilogram of mangoes, and the family buys far more salt. We need a measure that does not depend on the unit. A percentage is such a measure.

@ Percentage change | The change divided by the starting value, times 100.

> Percentage change: change over starting value, times 100.
A **percentage change** means the change divided by the starting value, multiplied by 100. A price that moves from Tk 20 to Tk 22 changes by Tk 2. Divide by the starting value: 2 ÷ 20 = 0.10. Multiply by 100: the price rose by 10 per cent. Always divide by the value you started from, not the value you ended with.

> Salt: price up 10 per cent, quantity down 1 per cent. Mangoes: quantity down 20 per cent.

Apply this to both goods. The price of each rose by 10 per cent. The quantity of salt fell by 1 ÷ 100 × 100 = 1 per cent. The quantity of mangoes fell by 4 ÷ 20 × 100 = 20 per cent. Now the reactions can be placed side by side. For salt, a 10 per cent price rise brought a 1 per cent fall in quantity. For mangoes, the same rise brought a 20 per cent fall. Mangoes reacted twenty times as strongly.

@ Elasticity | How strongly one thing reacts when another thing changes.
@ Price elasticity of demand | Percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price.

> Two new terms: elasticity and price elasticity of demand.
Economists compress this comparison into one number. **Elasticity** means how strongly one thing reacts when another thing changes. A rubber band is elastic: a small pull stretches it a long way. **Price elasticity of demand** means the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. From now on this book writes "elasticity" for price elasticity of demand, and says so whenever another kind is meant.

> Formula: elasticity equals % change in quantity divided by % change in price.

Here is the formula, first in words and then in symbols. Elasticity = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price. In symbols, Ed = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP. Here Ed is elasticity, Q is quantity demanded, P is price, and Δ (the Greek letter delta) means "change in". For salt, Ed = 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1. For mangoes, Ed = 20 ÷ 10 = 2.0.^1 Salt has the small number and mangoes the large one, as the household story suggested.

^1: A rise in price brings a fall in quantity, so the true ratio is negative: −20 ÷ 10 = −2.0. For an ordinary demand curve the sign is always negative, so economists drop it and quote the size. This book does the same for price elasticity. For income and cross elasticity, later in this chapter, the sign carries meaning and is kept.

> A short test of the formula.

*Pause and check.* The price of a school bag rises by 5 per cent and the quantity demanded falls by 10 per cent. What is the elasticity? The answer is at the back of the book.

> Elasticity is a ratio of two percentages, so it has no units.

*Before moving on.* Elasticity compares two percentages, so it has no units and can be used for any good. *You should now be able to say:* "Elasticity is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, and a larger number means a stronger reaction."

## Price Elasticity of Demand

> This is the longest section of the chapter, and it repays slow reading.

@! Go slowly here.

This section is the centre of the chapter. It sorts demand into five types, shows two ways of measuring elasticity, explains why one straight line has many elasticities, and ends with worked examples. Take it one subsection at a time.

### The Five Types

> The number 1 is the dividing line between strong and weak reaction.

The number 1 divides strong reactions from weak ones. When quantity changes by a larger percentage than price, buyers react strongly, and the number is above 1. When quantity changes by a smaller percentage, the number is below 1. Economists name five cases. Figure 4 shows the five curves side by side. Price is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, as in Chapter IV.

@ Elastic | Quantity changes by a larger percentage than price.
@ Inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price.
@ Unit elastic | Quantity and price change by equal percentages.

> Elastic, inelastic and unit elastic are the three main types.
Demand is **elastic** when the quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than the price, so elasticity is above 1. Mangoes, with 2.0, are an example. Demand is **inelastic** when the quantity changes by a smaller percentage, so elasticity is below 1. Salt, with 0.1, is an example. Demand is **unit elastic** when the two percentages are equal, so elasticity is exactly 1. A 10 per cent price rise then brings a 10 per cent fall in quantity.

@ Perfectly elastic | Buyers take any amount at one price and nothing above it.
@ Perfectly inelastic | Quantity demanded does not change at all when price changes.

> The two extremes are horizontal and vertical curves.
Two extreme cases complete the list. Demand is **perfectly elastic** when buyers will take any amount at one price and nothing at a higher price. Elasticity is then infinite, and the curve is a horizontal line. A small seller in a market where thousands of others set the price meets this demand (Chapter XII). Demand is **perfectly inelastic** when quantity does not change at all, so elasticity is 0 and the curve is a vertical line. It is rare. It fits only a need with no substitute, such as a life-saving medicine for one patient.

The table below gathers the five types in one place. Read each row from left to right: the name, the value of elasticity, how quantity reacts, and the shape of the curve.

> "Elastic" is about percentages, not about the number of units.

Many beginners read "elastic" to mean that buyers change by many units. That is wrong. The test is always a comparison of two percentages. A seller whose customers fall from 1,000 to 990 may still face elastic demand, if the price rose by only 0.5 per cent. Then the quantity fell by 1 per cent, and 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2.0.

### Point and Arc Methods

> Over a large change, the percentage method gives two answers, one for each direction.

The percentage formula has a weakness. Take the neighbourhood bun market of the case file, where demand is Qd = 540 − 30P a day. If the price rises from Tk 8 to Tk 10, the quantity falls from 300 to 240. The price changed by 2 ÷ 8 × 100 = 25 per cent, and the quantity by 60 ÷ 300 × 100 = 20 per cent. Elasticity is 20 ÷ 25 = 0.8. Now travel the other way, from Tk 10 to Tk 8. The price falls by 2 ÷ 10 × 100 = 20 per cent, and the quantity rises by 60 ÷ 240 × 100 = 25 per cent. Elasticity is 25 ÷ 20 = 1.25. The same two points give two answers, because the starting value changed.

@ Point elasticity | Elasticity at one point of the demand curve, for a very small price change.

> Point elasticity measures one point, using the slope.
Two remedies exist. The first is to measure at one point, for a very small change. **Point elasticity** means elasticity measured at a single point of a demand curve. On a straight demand curve, quantity changes by the same amount for each Tk 1 of price, so we can use that amount. The formula in words: elasticity = (change in quantity ÷ change in price) × (price ÷ quantity). In symbols, Ed = (ΔQ ÷ ΔP) × (P ÷ Q), where P and Q are the price and quantity at the point. This is the percentage formula rearranged, because (ΔQ ÷ Q) ÷ (ΔP ÷ P) is the same as (ΔQ ÷ ΔP) × (P ÷ Q).

@ Arc elasticity | Elasticity between two points, using average price and average quantity.

> Arc elasticity uses average price and average quantity.
The second remedy suits a real change between two points. **Arc elasticity** means elasticity between two points, found by using the average of the two prices and the average of the two quantities as the starting values. The formula is Ed = (ΔQ ÷ ΔP) × (average P ÷ average Q). For Tk 8 to Tk 10: ΔQ = 60, ΔP = 2, average P = 9 and average Q = 270. So Ed = (60 ÷ 2) × (9 ÷ 270) = 30 × 9 ÷ 270 = 270 ÷ 270 = 1.0. The answer is the same in both directions, and it lies between the two earlier answers, 0.8 and 1.25.

### Elasticity Along a Straight Line

> One straight line has the same slope everywhere but a different elasticity at each point.

Look again at the bun demand curve. Its slope is the same everywhere: every Tk 1 added to the price removes 30 buns. Yet the elasticity is not the same everywhere. The point formula shows why. The slope part, 30, stays fixed, but the part P ÷ Q changes. High on the curve the price is large and the quantity small, so P ÷ Q is large. Low on the curve the price is small and the quantity large, so P ÷ Q is small. The table gives six rows. To read a row, take the price, find the quantity from Qd = 540 − 30P, and then compute 30 × P ÷ Q.

:table Elasticity along the bun demand curve, Qd = 540 − 30P
| Price (Tk) | Quantity | Elasticity | Type |
| 2 | 480 | 0.125 | Inelastic |
| 8 | 300 | 0.8 | Inelastic |
| 9 | 270 | 1.0 | Unit elastic |
| 10 | 240 | 1.25 | Elastic |
| 14 | 120 | 3.5 | Elastic |
| 16 | 60 | 8.0 | Elastic |

:fig Elasticity changes along a straight demand curve. It is unit elastic at the middle point. | fig_line.svg

> Steepness is not the test: the upper half is elastic, the lower half inelastic.

The curve is elastic in its upper half, unit elastic at the middle point (price Tk 9, quantity 270), and inelastic in its lower half. Many students think that a steep curve means inelastic demand and a flat curve means elastic demand. That is true only when we compare two curves at the same point. A single straight line shows both kinds.

> Case file: bun elasticities at Tk 8, 10 and 14.
:case Case file, illustrative. In the neighbourhood bun market, demand is Qd = 540 − 30P a day. At Tk 10 the quantity is 240 and elasticity is 30 × 10 ÷ 240 = 1.25. At Tk 8 it is 0.8, and at Tk 14 it is 3.5. The arc elasticity between Tk 8 and Tk 10 is 1.0. For a straight demand curve, the percentage method from a starting price equals the point elasticity at that price, which is why 0.8 and 1.25 match the earlier results.

### Worked Examples

> First example: small clean numbers, so the method is the only new thing.

*Example 1.* *Given:* the price of an exercise book rises from Tk 20 to Tk 24, and monthly purchases fall from 500 to 450. *Find:* the elasticity. *Formula:* elasticity = percentage change in quantity ÷ percentage change in price, or Ed = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP.

> The steps: two percentage changes, then one division.

*Steps.* Change in price = 24 − 20 = Tk 4. Percentage change in price = 4 ÷ 20 × 100 = 20 per cent. Change in quantity = 500 − 450 = 50. Percentage change in quantity = 50 ÷ 500 × 100 = 10 per cent. Ed = 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5. *Answer:* elasticity is 0.5, so demand is inelastic: quantity falls by half a per cent for each 1 per cent rise in price. *Check:* the quantity fell by a smaller percentage than the price (10 against 20), so the number must be below 1, and it is.

> Second example: the point formula applied to the bun market.

*Example 2.* *Given:* demand is Qd = 540 − 30P, and the price is Tk 10. *Find:* the point elasticity at this price. *Formula:* Ed = (ΔQ ÷ ΔP) × (P ÷ Q). *Steps.* Quantity at Tk 10 = 540 − 30 × 10 = 240. The change in quantity per Tk 1 of price is 30 (size only). Then P ÷ Q = 10 ÷ 240. So Ed = 30 × 10 ÷ 240 = 300 ÷ 240 = 1.25. *Answer:* elasticity at Tk 10 is 1.25, so demand is elastic there. *Check:* Tk 10 lies above the middle point (Tk 9), where elasticity is 1, so the value must exceed 1. It does.

> Six slips cause most wrong answers.

*Common slips.* First, dividing by the new value instead of the starting value. Second, comparing units instead of percentages. Third, putting price on top: quantity always goes on top. Fourth, calling a steep line inelastic without checking the point. Fifth, using arc elasticity when the question asks for a point, or the reverse. Sixth, mixing periods, such as a weekly price with a monthly quantity.

> A second test: use the point formula at the top of the curve.

*Pause and check.* Using Qd = 540 − 30P, find the point elasticity at a price of Tk 16. The answer is at the back of the book.

> Elasticity sorts demand into five types and has two measuring methods.

*Before moving on.* The five types are perfectly elastic, elastic, unit elastic, inelastic and perfectly inelastic. Use the point method for a tiny change and the arc method for a visible change. *You should now be able to say:* "On a straight demand curve, elasticity falls steadily from infinity at the top to zero at the bottom, and equals 1 at the middle."

## What Makes Demand Elastic

> Four things decide how strongly buyers react: substitutes, need, share of income and time.

Four main things decide whether demand is elastic or inelastic. The first and strongest is the number of close substitutes. If a buyer can switch easily to another good, a price rise sends buyers away quickly. Tea from one particular stall has many substitutes, namely the other stalls. Tea as a drink has fewer. The narrower the good is described, the more elastic its demand.

@ Necessity | A good people need and cannot easily do without.
@ Luxury | A good people can do without when money is short.

> Necessities have inelastic demand; luxuries elastic.
The second is how much the buyer needs the good. A **necessity** is a good that people need and cannot easily do without, such as salt or medicine. A **luxury** is a good that people can do without when money is short, such as a restaurant meal. Demand for necessities tends to be inelastic and demand for luxuries elastic. The line is not fixed. A good that is a necessity to one family may be a luxury to another.

> Large shares of income and long time spans make buyers more careful.

The third is the share of income the good takes. A price rise in matches costs a family almost nothing, so it does not notice. A rise in the rent of a flat takes a large part of income, so it reacts. The fourth is time. In the first days after a price rise buyers have little chance to change habits, so demand is inelastic. Over months they find substitutes, change their equipment and move their homes, and demand becomes more elastic. Chapter VII shows that the same idea applies to sellers.

> The two stalls differ in substitutes, not in prices.

Return to the two tea stalls. The first stall sits among several rivals, so a customer who dislikes the new price walks a few metres to another cup. Its demand is elastic, and the price rise lost it custom. The second stall serves people who wait for a bus and have no time to go elsewhere. Its demand is inelastic, and the price rise raised its takings.

> A seller who knows elasticity knows whether a price rise will bring more money or less.

The number matters to three kinds of people. A seller needs it before changing a price (the last section of this chapter shows how). A government needs it before laying a tax. A tax on a good with inelastic demand raises a large sum, because buyers go on buying. A tax on a good with elastic demand raises less, because buyers switch away. Salt would be a better source of revenue than mangoes for this reason, though other concerns, such as fairness to poor families, also count.

> A good harvest can leave the farmer worse off if demand is inelastic.

A farmer needs the number too. Suppose the demand for potatoes has an elasticity of 0.5. A bumper harvest lowers the price by 20 per cent, and quantity bought rises by only 10 per cent. Revenue is then 0.8 × 1.10 = 0.88 of its former level, a fall of 12 per cent. The farmer harvested more and earned less. This is why farmers' groups sometimes ask the government to buy the extra crop, and why a poor harvest can raise a farmer's income.

> Four factors, one number, three users.

*Before moving on.* Demand is more elastic when there are close substitutes, when the good is a luxury, when it takes a large share of income, and when buyers have more time. *You should now be able to say:* "Demand for a good is inelastic when buyers cannot easily switch away from it."

## Income and Cross Elasticity

> Demand reacts to income and to other prices as well, and the same method measures both.

Price is not the only thing that moves demand. Chapter IV listed income and the prices of related goods among the determinants. The same percentage method measures their effects, with one change: the sign is now kept, because it carries meaning.

@ Income elasticity | Percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in income.

> Income elasticity shows normal, inferior and luxury goods.
**Income elasticity** means the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in consumers' income. In words: income elasticity = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in income. A positive value means a normal good, because demand rises with income. A negative value means an inferior good, because demand falls as income rises. A positive value above 1 marks a good whose demand grows faster than income, which is what is meant when economists call it a luxury. A positive value between 0 and 1 marks a necessity.

@ Cross elasticity | Percentage change in demand for one good divided by percentage change in the price of another.

> Cross elasticity shows substitutes and complements.
**Cross elasticity** means the percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another good. In words: cross elasticity = percentage change in quantity of good A ÷ percentage change in price of good B. A positive value means the goods are substitutes: when the price of B rises, buyers move to A. A negative value means the goods are complements: when the price of B rises, buyers buy less of both. A value near zero means the goods are unrelated.

> Worked example: income and cross elasticity with new numbers.

*Example 3.* *Given:* a family's income rises from Tk 20,000 to Tk 22,000 a month. Restaurant meals rise from 40 to 50 a month, and coarse rice falls from 30 kg to 27 kg. Separately, the price of petrol rises by 10 per cent and demand for CNG rises by 8 per cent. *Find:* the income elasticities and the cross elasticity. *Formulas:* as stated above. *Steps.* Income change = 2,000 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = 10 per cent. Meals: 10 ÷ 40 × 100 = 25 per cent, so 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5. Rice: −3 ÷ 30 × 100 = −10 per cent, so −10 ÷ 10 = −1.0. Cross: 8 ÷ 10 = 0.8. *Answer:* restaurant meals have income elasticity 2.5 (a luxury); coarse rice −1.0 (an inferior good); CNG has cross elasticity 0.8 with petrol (substitutes). *Check:* each sign agrees with the story. More income brings more meals and less coarse rice, and a dearer fuel sends buyers to the other fuel.

> Common slip: dropping the sign here loses the meaning.

A common slip is to drop the sign, as we do for price elasticity. Here the sign tells us whether a good is normal or inferior and whether two goods are substitutes or complements. Keep it.

> Case file: cake income elasticity and bun cross elasticity.
:case Case file, illustrative. When customers' income rises by 10 per cent, cake sales rise by 15 per cent: income elasticity 1.5, a luxury. When a rival bakery raises its bun price by 10 per cent, demand for this bakery's buns rises by 5 per cent: cross elasticity 0.5, so the buns are substitutes.

> Income and cross elasticity use the same method with the sign kept.

*Before moving on.* Income elasticity tells us about normal and inferior goods; cross elasticity tells us about substitutes and complements. *You should now be able to say:* "A negative cross elasticity means that the two goods are used together."

## Price and Revenue

> A seller wants to know what a price change does to the money received.

@ Total revenue | Price multiplied by the quantity sold.

A seller who changes a price wants to know what happens to the money received. **Total revenue** means the price multiplied by the quantity sold. In symbols, TR = P × Q. If a bakery sells 240 buns at Tk 10, its total revenue is 240 × 10 = Tk 2,400.^2

^2: Some books say *sales* or *turnover* for the same thing. This book uses *revenue* throughout.

> Elasticity predicts the effect of a price change on revenue.
Elasticity tells us the result. When the price changes, two things happen at once. The price per unit moves, and the quantity sold moves the other way. If demand is elastic, the quantity changes by the larger percentage, so the quantity effect wins. If demand is inelastic, the price effect wins. If demand is unit elastic, the two cancel exactly. The table sets out the rule.

:table Elasticity and total revenue
| Demand is | When price rises | When price falls |
| Elastic | Revenue falls | Revenue rises |
| Unit elastic | No change | No change |
| Inelastic | Revenue rises | Revenue falls |

> The bun market shows revenue rising, peaking at the unit-elastic point, then falling.

:table Total revenue in the neighbourhood bun market, per day
| Price (Tk) | Quantity | Total revenue (Tk) | Elasticity |
| 8 | 300 | 2,400 | 0.8 |
| 9 | 270 | 2,430 | 1.0 |
| 10 | 240 | 2,400 | 1.25 |
| 12 | 180 | 2,160 | 2.0 |
| 14 | 120 | 1,680 | 3.5 |
| 16 | 60 | 960 | 8.0 |

> The bun table shows revenue peaking at the unit-elastic price.
The table confirms the rule. Between Tk 16 and Tk 12 demand is elastic, and each cut in price raises revenue, from Tk 960 to Tk 2,160. Revenue reaches its highest value, Tk 2,430, at Tk 9, where elasticity is exactly 1. Below Tk 9 demand is inelastic, and a further cut lowers revenue. Between Tk 8 and Tk 10 the arc elasticity is 1.0, so revenue is the same, Tk 2,400, at both ends.

> Case file: the bun revenue test.
:case Case file, illustrative. The revenue test for the bun market gives Tk 2,400 at Tk 8, 2,400 at Tk 10, 2,160 at Tk 12, 1,680 at Tk 14 and 960 at Tk 16, with a peak of Tk 2,430 at Tk 9 (derived). Revenue is not profit: the cost of making buns is counted in Chapter X.

> A lower price is not always better for a seller.

Is a lower price always better for a seller? It is not, for four reasons. First, when demand is inelastic, a price cut lowers revenue and raises the number of buns to be baked. Second, revenue is not profit, because each extra unit costs money to make. Third, a very low price may signal poor quality, so that some buyers stay away. Fourth, rivals may match the cut, so that the extra buyers never arrive. Chapter XI turns this into a rule for profit, and Chapter XIV shows what rival replies can do.

> Revenue rises with price cuts only in the elastic range.

*Pause and check.* In the table, the price falls from Tk 14 to Tk 12. By how much does revenue change? Is this what the rule predicts? The answer is at the back of the book.

> Elasticity and revenue move together by a simple rule.

*Before moving on.* A price cut raises revenue when demand is elastic and lowers it when demand is inelastic. *You should now be able to say:* "At the point of unit elasticity, a small change in price leaves revenue almost unchanged."

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

Elasticity measures how strongly quantity demanded reacts to a change in price. It equals the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price, and the size is quoted without sign. Demand is elastic above 1, inelastic below 1, and unit elastic at 1, with perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic as the extremes. Use the point method for a tiny change and the arc method for a visible one. A straight demand curve is elastic above its middle and inelastic below it. Substitutes, need, share of income and time decide elasticity. Income elasticity and cross elasticity, with signs kept, show normal and inferior goods and substitutes and complements. A price cut raises revenue only where demand is elastic.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Steepness is not elasticity.

Many students believe that a steep curve always means inelastic demand. Slope and elasticity are different things. Slope depends on the units of the two axes. Elasticity uses percentages and changes along a straight line.

> An elasticity of 2 does not mean that quantity falls by 2 units.

Another mistake is to read an elasticity of 2 as a fall of 2 units. It means a fall of 2 per cent for each 1 per cent rise in price.

> Inelastic does not mean that buyers ignore the price.

A third mistake is to read "inelastic" as "buyers do not react". Buyers react in every case but the extreme one. They react less than in proportion. A seller facing inelastic demand also should not raise price without limit, because the rule of Chapter XI sets a stopping point.

## In History

> A fuel shock in 1973 showed that elasticity grows with time.

In October 1973, several oil-producing states cut supplies to some countries and raised prices. Within months the price of crude oil roughly quadrupled. At first, buyers cut their use only a little. Their cars, homes and factories were built to use the fuel, so demand was inelastic. Over the following years they bought smaller cars, insulated buildings and changed fuels, and demand grew more elastic. The episode taught economists and governments that the same price rise can bring a small reaction in a month and a large one in a decade.

## Sources

> Alfred Marshall defined the idea.

Alfred Marshall's *Principles of Economics* (1890) introduced the measure of the reaction of demand to price, which this chapter calls elasticity. He valued it because it compared reactions without regard to the units of goods. His work is the root of the five types and of the link between elasticity and revenue.
