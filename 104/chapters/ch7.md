:chapter VII | Supply and Its Elasticity
:mood Who sells more when the price rises, and how fast?
:desc Chapters IV and V looked at the buyer. This chapter looks at the seller. It shows why a higher price brings more goods to market, what moves the whole supply curve, where the rule fails, and how to measure the seller's reaction with a number that mirrors the elasticity of demand.

> A fisherman and a baker face the same price rise, and only one can respond today.

On the same morning the price of fish and the price of bread each rise by 10 per cent. A fisherman has landed his catch at dawn. The fish are on the table, and no more can be caught before evening. A baker has an oven standing idle and a sack of flour in store. By afternoon she has baked an extra batch. The price signal was the same for both, but the amount each could offer was very different.

> Buyers were measured in Chapters IV and V; sellers now follow the same path.

Chapter IV asked how buyers react to a price, and Chapter V measured the reaction. This chapter asks the same two questions of sellers. How much will sellers offer at each price? And how strongly does that amount react when the price moves? The answers explain why the fisherman and the baker differ, and they prepare the ground for Chapter VIII, where buyers and sellers meet.

> This chapter rests on demand and on elasticity.

*You will need* the demand curve, the idea of a shift (Chapter IV), and elasticity with its five types and two methods (Chapter V).

## What Supply Means

> A family with cows decides how much milk to sell at each price.

@! Go slowly here.

Begin with a village family that keeps cows. At Tk 50 a litre, the family finds it worth selling 20 litres a day. At Tk 60 it sells 30 litres, because the higher price makes it worth feeding the cows better and milking longer. At Tk 70 it sells 40 litres, and at Tk 80, 50 litres. The family decides how much to offer for each possible price. That list of decisions is the beginning of supply.

@ Supply | The quantity sellers are able and willing to sell at each price.
@ Quantity supplied | The amount sellers offer for sale at one price.

> Supply is the quantity sellers can and will sell at each price.
**Supply** means the quantity of a good that sellers are able and willing to sell at each price. Both words matter. A seller who is willing but has no goods or inputs cannot supply. A seller who has goods but will not sell at that price does not supply. **Quantity supplied** means the amount offered at one price. At Tk 60 the family's quantity supplied is 30 litres a day.^1

^1: Supply is a flow, measured over a period such as a day or a month. It is not the stock that a seller holds in a warehouse. A seller may hold a large stock and yet supply little at a low price.

@ Supply schedule | A table of prices and the quantities offered at each.
@ Supply curve | The line that plots a supply schedule.

> A schedule is the table; the curve is the line.
A table that lists prices and the quantities offered is a **supply schedule**. If we plot it with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, the line through the points is the **supply curve**. The family's schedule gives four points, and the curve rises from left to right. The word *schedule* names the table, and the word *curve* names the line. Both carry the same information.

> The market supply is the sum of what every seller offers at each price.

@ Individual supply | The supply of one seller.
@ Market supply | The sum of the supplies of all sellers.

One seller's supply is **individual supply**. **Market supply** is the sum of the individual supplies at each price. In the neighbourhood bun market of the case file, three bakeries bake buns. The table shows how their schedules add. Read a row: at Tk 12, Bakery A offers 60 buns, B offers 30, C offers 90, and the market offers 180.

:table Market supply of buns from three bakeries, per day
| Price (Tk) | Bakery A | Bakery B | Bakery C | Market |
| 8 | 20 | 10 | 30 | 60 |
| 10 | 40 | 20 | 60 | 120 |
| 12 | 60 | 30 | 90 | 180 |

| 16 | 100 | 50 | 150 | 300 |

> The market schedule is a rule: Qs = 30P − 180, zero below Tk 6.

The market column follows a rule: quantity supplied Qs = 30P − 180, where P is the price in taka. At Tk 12 the rule gives 30 × 12 − 180 = 360 − 180 = 180, as in the table. At Tk 6 it gives 0, and below Tk 6 no bakery offers any bun, because the price does not cover the cost of making one.

:case Case file, illustrative. In the neighbourhood bun market, supply per day is Qs = 30P − 180, with nothing offered below Tk 6. At Tk 8, 10, 12, 14 and 16 the quantity supplied is 60, 120, 180, 240 and 300. Three bakeries, A, B and C, make up this supply, with schedules 10P − 60, 5P − 30 and 15P − 90.

> A supply schedule is a table of what sellers will offer, one row for each price.

*Before moving on.* Supply is a list of quantities at each price, and market supply is the sum of the individual lists. *You should now be able to say:* "Supply means how much sellers will offer at each possible price, not how much they happen to hold."

## The Law of Supply

> Higher price, larger quantity offered: the rule mirrors the law of demand.

The pattern in the schedules above is the same in most markets. When the price of a good rises, sellers offer more. When it falls, they offer less. We call this the law of supply.

@ Law of supply | A higher price brings a larger quantity supplied, other things equal.

> The law of supply, with ceteris paribus.
The **law of supply** states that, other things remaining the same, a higher price brings a larger quantity supplied, and a lower price brings a smaller one. The phrase "other things remaining the same" is the *ceteris paribus* of Chapter II. We hold costs, technology and taxes still while we watch price and quantity. On the graph the supply curve slopes upward from left to right.

:fig The supply curve of the neighbourhood bun market. A fall in supply, caused by dearer flour, moves the whole curve to the left. | fig_supply.svg

> Rising price gives sellers both the reason and the means to offer more.

Two reasons explain the upward slope. The first is profit. A higher price makes each extra unit more profitable, so existing sellers want to sell more, and new sellers are drawn in. The second is cost. Producing more usually costs more for each additional unit, because the baker must run the oven longer, pay for extra labour and buy flour in smaller lots. Chapter X shows this carefully. A seller will produce a unit only if the price covers its extra cost, so a higher price makes more units worth producing.

> Two sellers, one price rise, different readiness to respond.

Return to the fisherman and the baker. Both want the higher price. The baker can raise the quantity she offers, because her extra cost is low and her oven is idle. The fisherman cannot, because his catch is already landed. The law of supply says that the baker's curve slopes up. It does not say how steeply, and the fisherman's curve, in this one day, is nearly vertical. The last section of this chapter measures the difference.

## Determinants of Supply

> Factors other than price move the whole supply curve.

@! Go slowly here.

The law of supply holds everything but price still. In real life these other things change. When one of them changes, the whole curve moves, and so we need to tell the two kinds of change apart. Think of the family with cows. If the price of milk rises, the family sells more milk, and we move along its curve. If the price of cattle feed rises, the family offers less milk at every price, and the curve itself moves.

@ Determinants of supply | The things other than price that decide supply.

> Determinants of supply: six things other than price.
The things other than price that decide supply are the **determinants of supply**. The main ones are the price of inputs, technology, taxes and subsidies, expectations, the number of sellers, and the weather. Take them one at a time.

> Dearer inputs lower supply; cheaper inputs and better methods raise it.

Input prices come first. If flour, wages or rent rise, the cost of each bun rises, and bakeries offer fewer buns at every price. Supply falls. If inputs become cheaper, supply rises.

@ Technology | The methods and equipment used to make a good.

> Better methods lower cost and raise supply.
**Technology** means the methods and equipment used to make a good. A faster oven or a better mixing machine lowers the cost of each bun, so supply rises. The effect is the opposite of that of dearer inputs.

@ Subsidy | Money paid by government to lower a seller's cost.

> A tax lowers supply; a subsidy raises it.
Taxes and subsidies act on cost too. A tax on each unit sold raises the seller's cost, so supply falls. A **subsidy** means money paid by the government to lower a seller's cost. It acts like a cut in input prices, so supply rises. Bangladeshi farmers receive subsidies on some fertiliser, and the effect on supply is of this kind.

> Expectations, the number of sellers and the weather also move supply.

Expectations matter as well. A baker who expects the price of buns to rise next week may hold back some buns today, and today's supply falls. The number of sellers matters because market supply is a sum: a new bakery adds a new schedule to the sum, and supply rises. The weather matters most in farming. A flood or a drought lowers the crop a farmer can offer at every price.

@ Change in quantity supplied | A move along the supply curve, caused by a change in the price of the good.
@ Change in supply | A shift of the whole supply curve, caused by something other than the price.

> Movement along the curve versus shift of the curve.
Here is the rule, which parallels the one for demand. A **change in quantity supplied** is a movement along the supply curve, and only a change in the price of the good itself causes it. A **change in supply** is a shift of the whole curve, and a change in any determinant causes it. A rise in supply shifts the curve to the right, so more is offered at every price. A fall in supply shifts it to the left.

> Case file: dearer flour shifts bun supply left.
:case Case file, illustrative. The price of flour rises, adding Tk 2 to the cost of each bun. Supply shifts to Qs = 30P − 240, which is 60 buns fewer at every price. At Tk 12 the quantity supplied is now 120 instead of 180, and nothing is offered below Tk 8. Figure 9 shows the shift. Chapter VIII uses this new supply.

> Test the rule: is it a movement or a shift?

*Pause and check.* In each case, say whether supply shifts or the quantity supplied moves along the curve. (a) The price of buns rises. (b) A new oven cuts the cost of baking. (c) The government taxes each bun sold. The answer is at the back of the book.

> Determinants shift the curve; only the good's own price moves along it.

*Before moving on.* Six determinants shift supply: input prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, expectations, the number of sellers and the weather. *You should now be able to say:* "A change in the price of the good itself moves us along the curve, and a change in anything else shifts the curve."

## Exceptional Supply Curves

> A few cases do not follow the law of supply, so their curves have other shapes.

@ Exceptional supply curve | A supply curve that does not slope upward in the usual way.


The law of supply holds for most goods, but a few cases seem to break it. These cases give **exceptional supply curves**, which means supply curves that do not slope upward in the usual way.

> Fixed-quantity goods have a vertical supply curve.
The first case is a good that cannot be made again in the short time that matters. A fisherman's catch, a day's fresh milk, and a famous painting are examples. The quantity is fixed, so the supply curve is a vertical line, whatever the price. The seller must sell what exists. A rise in price cannot call forth more.

> Labour can bend backward, and a farmer's crop can be fixed for the season.

The second case is labour. At a low wage, a higher wage may persuade a worker to work longer hours. At a high wage, the worker may prefer more leisure and work fewer hours. Suppose wages of Tk 50, 80, 110, 140 and 170 an hour bring offers of 6, 8, 9, 8 and 7 hours a day. The curve rises, then bends backward. The third case is farm output. Within a season the crop is fixed by what was sown, so supply does not respond to price. A fourth case, a poor seller who needs a fixed sum of money, may sell more when the price is low, in order to raise the sum. This is rare and is limited to cases of great need.

> The exceptions are limited: most goods follow the law.

These cases are narrow. Most goods, for most periods, follow the law of supply. Even the exceptions are explained by the way time and need limit the seller's choices, and the last section of this chapter shows that the time allowed is the main factor in how steep the supply curve is.

> Exceptions arise from fixed quantity, from leisure and from need.

*Before moving on.* The exceptional curves are vertical (fixed quantity), backward bending (labour), and in rare cases falling (a seller in need). *You should now be able to say:* "The law of supply fails where the seller cannot or will not respond to a higher price in the usual way."

## Elasticity of Supply

> How strongly sellers react to price: the mirror of the elasticity of demand.

@! Go slowly here.

Chapter V measured the reaction of buyers. The same method now measures the reaction of sellers. The fisherman and the baker differ in how strongly their quantity responds. We need a number for that difference.

@ Elasticity of supply | Percentage change in quantity supplied divided by percentage change in price.

> Elasticity of supply is the percentage change in quantity over that in price.
**Elasticity of supply** means the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. In words: elasticity of supply = percentage change in quantity supplied ÷ percentage change in price. In symbols, Es = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP. Because both quantity and price move in the same direction, the number is positive, and no sign is dropped. The point formula is as in Chapter V: Es = (ΔQs ÷ ΔP) × (P ÷ Qs).

> The same five types appear, with the same dividing line at 1.

The five types follow the same pattern as for demand. Supply is elastic when the quantity changes by a larger percentage than the price (Es above 1), inelastic when by a smaller percentage (below 1), and unit elastic when by an equal percentage (exactly 1). It is perfectly elastic (infinite) when sellers will offer any amount at one price, and perfectly inelastic (zero) when the quantity cannot change. Figure 10 shows the five curves.

:fig Five types of elasticity of supply. In each panel, price is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. | fig_stypes.svg

> The shape of a straight supply curve decides its type, wherever you measure.

A straight supply curve has a useful rule, which has no match for straight demand curves. If the line, when extended, meets the price axis above zero, supply is elastic at every point. If it passes through the origin, supply is unit elastic at every point, whatever its slope. If it meets the quantity axis to the right of zero, supply is inelastic at every point. Figure 10 shows all three lines.

> Why a line through the origin always gives one.

The reason is simple. On a line through the origin, quantity is a fixed multiple of price: Qs = kP. Then P ÷ Qs = 1 ÷ k, and the slope ΔQs ÷ ΔP is k, so Es = k × (1 ÷ k) = 1.

:table Elasticity of supply along the bun supply curve, Qs = 30P − 180
| Price (Tk) | Quantity | Elasticity |
| 8 | 60 | 4.0 |
| 10 | 120 | 2.5 |
| 12 | 180 | 2.0 |
| 14 | 240 | 1.75 |
| 16 | 300 | 1.6 |

> The bun line cuts the price axis at Tk 6, so supply is elastic everywhere.

The table shows the bun supply curve. Its slope is 30 at every point. The line meets the price axis at Tk 6, so the rule says that supply is elastic at every point, and the table agrees: every value is above 1. The value falls toward 1 as the price rises, because the intercept matters less when the price is high. To read a row, find Qs from the rule, and compute 30 × P ÷ Qs. At Tk 12: 30 × 12 ÷ 180 = 2.0.

> Case file: elasticity of supply at Tk 12 and the loaf line.
:case Case file, illustrative. At Tk 12 the elasticity of supply of buns is 30 × 12 ÷ 180 = 2.0. For loaves, supply is Qs = 15P, a line through the origin, so elasticity is 1 at every price. At Tk 20, for example, the quantity is 300 and 15 × 20 ÷ 300 = 1.

> Three factors decide how readily supply responds: time, spare capacity, ease of storing and switching.

@ Market period | A time too short to change output.
@ Short run | A time in which at least one input is fixed.
@ Long run | A time in which all inputs can change.


Three main factors decide the elasticity of supply. The first is time. Over a very short **market period**, which is a time too short to change output, the quantity is fixed, and supply is perfectly inelastic: the fisherman's day. In the **short run**, a time in which at least one input is fixed, sellers can change variable inputs such as flour and labour, but not the oven, so supply is inelastic or moderately elastic. In the **long run**, a time in which all inputs can change, sellers can build ovens, enter or leave the market, and supply is more elastic. Chapter IX gives these terms their full meaning.

> Spare capacity and ease of storage or switching raise elasticity.

The second factor is spare capacity. A baker with an idle oven can bake more at once, so her supply is elastic. A baker whose oven runs all day cannot, and her supply is inelastic until she buys another. The third factor is how easily a seller can store the good or move inputs to another use. A seller of rice can store it and release it when prices rise, so supply is elastic. A seller of fresh fish cannot, so supply is inelastic. A farmer who can switch land from jute to rice, or the reverse, has a more elastic supply of each.

> First example: small clean numbers.

*Example 1.* *Given:* the price of eggs rises from Tk 50 to Tk 55 a dozen, and a farm raises its offer from 200 to 240 dozen a week. *Find:* the elasticity of supply. *Formula:* Es = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP. *Steps.* Price change = 5 ÷ 50 × 100 = 10 per cent. Quantity change = 40 ÷ 200 × 100 = 20 per cent. Es = 20 ÷ 10 = 2.0. *Answer:* elasticity of supply is 2.0, so supply is elastic. *Check:* the quantity changed by a larger percentage than the price (20 against 10), so the value must exceed 1, and it does.

> Second example: the arc method on the bun market.

*Example 2.* *Given:* the bun market, Qs = 30P − 180. The price rises from Tk 10 to Tk 14. *Find:* the arc elasticity of supply. *Formula:* Es = (ΔQs ÷ ΔP) × (average P ÷ average Qs). *Steps.* At Tk 10, Qs = 120. At Tk 14, Qs = 240. ΔQs = 120. ΔP = 4. Average P = 12. Average Qs = 180. Es = (120 ÷ 4) × (12 ÷ 180) = 30 × 12 ÷ 180 = 360 ÷ 180 = 2.0. *Answer:* the arc elasticity between Tk 10 and Tk 14 is 2.0. *Check:* the point values at the ends are 2.5 and 1.75, and 2.0 lies between them.

> Common slips repeat those of Chapter V, with one new one.

*Common slips.* The slips are those of Chapter V: the wrong starting value, units instead of percentages, and price on top. A new slip is to call a steep supply curve inelastic. Test the curve by the point where it meets an axis, not by its steepness.

> A final test: the same price, two elasticities.

*Pause and check.* At Tk 12 in the bun market, find the elasticity of supply, and compare it with the elasticity of demand from Chapter V. The answer is at the back of the book.

> Elasticity of supply depends on time, spare capacity and ease of switching.

*Before moving on.* The longer the time allowed, the greater the spare capacity, and the easier the storage and switching, the more elastic supply is. *You should now be able to say:* "A straight supply curve through the origin has an elasticity of one at every point."

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

Supply is the quantity sellers are able and willing to sell at each price. Market supply adds the supplies of all sellers. The law of supply says that a higher price brings a larger quantity supplied, because of profit and rising cost. Input prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, expectations, the number of sellers and the weather shift the whole curve. A few supply curves are exceptional: vertical for fixed quantities, backward bending for labour. Elasticity of supply is the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. A straight supply curve through the origin has an elasticity of one, one that meets the price axis is elastic, and one that meets the quantity axis is inelastic. Time, spare capacity and ease of storage and switching decide the value.

## Where People Go Wrong

> A shift and a movement are different events.

Many students say that supply "rises" when the price rises. When the price of the good rises, the quantity supplied rises, and supply does not change. Supply changes only when a determinant other than price changes.

> Supply is not the stock held.

A second mistake is to confuse supply with the stock a seller holds. Supply is the quantity offered at each price over a period.

> Steepness is not elasticity here either.

A third mistake, as in Chapter V, is to read elasticity from steepness. A steep line that cuts the price axis can be elastic, and a flat line that cuts the quantity axis can be inelastic.

## In History

> A frost in Brazil in 1975 showed how slowly supply can respond.

In July 1975 a severe frost struck the coffee-growing regions of Brazil and destroyed a large part of the crop. The price of coffee on world markets rose sharply over the following year. Higher prices could not call forth a quick increase in supply, because a new coffee tree takes several years to bear fruit. In the market period and the short run, supply was inelastic. Only in the long run, as growers planted new trees, did supply rise and the price ease. The episode is a standard example of the importance of time in supply.
