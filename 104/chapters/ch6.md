:chapter VI | Consumers' Surplus and Indifference Curves
:mood What is a good bargain worth, and how does anyone choose?
:desc A buyer pays one price for every bun, yet values the first bun far more than the last. This chapter measures the gain a buyer keeps beyond the price paid. It then offers a second way to explain choice, one that needs no counting of utils: curves of equal liking and a line that shows what a budget can buy.

> A shopper feels that a purchase was a bargain, though everyone paid the same price.

Think of a time when you bought something and felt that you had done well. Perhaps the price was Tk 100, and you would have paid Tk 150 if you had to. The shopkeeper took Tk 100 from you, and Tk 100 from every other buyer. Where did your feeling of a good bargain come from? The price shows what you paid. It does not show what the thing was worth to you, and the difference between the two is a real gain.

> Two questions: how big is the gain, and how can choice be explained without utils?

This chapter answers two questions. First, how can that gain be measured, and what is it used for? Second, Chapter III explained choice by counting utils, which assumes that satisfaction can be measured in numbers. Can choice be explained when buyers can only rank one basket above another? The second method, called indifference curve analysis, shows that it can, and it reaches the same answer.

> This chapter rests on utility, marginal utility and the demand curve.

*You will need* total and marginal utility (Chapter III), the demand curve and the law of demand (Chapter IV), and the idea of an equilibrium in which a buyer has no reason to change (Chapter III).

## Consumers' Surplus

> The first bun is worth more to the buyer than the last bun.

@! Go slowly here.

Begin with a single customer at the bakery. In Chapter III her utility from buns fell as she ate more: the first bun was worth 20 utils, the second 16, the third 12, the fourth 8 and the fifth 4. We take one util to be worth Tk 1. The first bun is then worth Tk 20 to her, the second Tk 16, and so on. She does not pay these sums. She pays the market price, which is Tk 10 for every bun.

> Willingness to pay is the most a buyer will give for one more unit.

@ Willingness to pay | The most a buyer will give for one unit of a good.

**Willingness to pay** means the most a buyer will give for one unit of a good. For the first bun it is Tk 20. A buyer buys a unit when the willingness to pay is at least the price. At Tk 10 the customer buys the first bun (worth 20), the second (worth 16) and the third (worth 12). She does not buy the fourth, because it is worth Tk 8, and that is less than the price. She stops at three buns, which is where Chapter III said she would stop.

> On each bun bought, the buyer keeps the gap between worth and price.

@ Consumers' surplus | What a buyer would be willing to pay, minus what is actually paid.

Now count what she gains on each bun. On the first, she would pay Tk 20 and pays Tk 10, so she keeps Tk 10. On the second she keeps 16 − 10 = Tk 6. On the third she keeps 12 − 10 = Tk 2. The total is Tk 18. We call this sum **consumers' surplus**, which means what a buyer would be willing to pay for the units bought, minus what is actually paid. Here she would pay 20 + 16 + 12 = Tk 48 and pays 3 × 10 = Tk 30, so the surplus is 48 − 30 = Tk 18.

:fig Consumers' surplus of one customer at a bun price of Tk 10. The shaded parts above the price line sum to Tk 18. | fig_cs.svg

> Figure 6 shows the surplus as the gap between the bars and the price line.

Figure 6 shows the same facts. Each bar is the willingness to pay for one bun, and the dashed line is the price. Read the first bar: it reaches Tk 20, and the price line cuts it at Tk 10, so Tk 10 of it is shaded. The shaded parts of the first three bars add up to Tk 18. The fourth and fifth bars fall below the price line, so those buns are not bought.

> Case file: surplus of Tk 18 at a bun price of Tk 10.
:case Case file, illustrative. At a bun price of Tk 10 the customer of Chapter III buys 3 buns. Consumers' surplus is (20 − 10) + (16 − 10) + (12 − 10) = Tk 18.

> A whole market has a surplus too, shown as a triangle under the demand curve.

The same idea works for a whole market. In the neighbourhood bun market of Chapter IV, demand is Qd = 540 − 30P, and buyers would pay up to Tk 18 for the very first bun. If the price were Tk 12, the market would buy 180 buns a day. The surplus is the triangle between the demand curve and the price line, with base 180 and height 18 − 12 = 6. Its area is ½ × 180 × 6 = Tk 540 a day. Chapter VIII uses this figure again.

> Surplus is not profit, and it is not a perfect measure.

Two warnings follow. First, consumers' surplus is a gain in well-being, not money received, and it is not a profit. Second, the measure rests on an assumption that a taka means the same to the buyer whether she is rich or poor, and that the buyer's other purchases do not change.^1 Rich and poor buyers do not value a taka equally, and so the figure is an estimate, not an exact count.

^1: Alfred Marshall made this assumption explicit in 1890. It holds well for goods that take a small part of income, such as buns, and less well for goods that take a large part.

> Surplus guides decisions on taxes, public projects and prices.

Despite its limits, the idea is useful. A tax raises the price, and buyers lose part of their surplus. A road or a bridge may charge a toll that is lower than what users would pay, so users gain surplus beyond the toll, and that gain counts as a benefit of the project. A seller who understands surplus sees that buyers keep a gain at a single price, and that some sellers try to capture it by charging different buyers different prices (Chapter XIII).

> Test the idea on a higher price.

*Pause and check.* The customer's willingness to pay is Tk 20, 16, 12, 8 and 4 for buns one to five. If the price were Tk 14, how many buns would she buy, and what would her consumers' surplus be? The answer is at the back of the book.

> Surplus is willingness to pay minus price, summed over the units bought.

*Before moving on.* Consumers' surplus is the sum of (willingness to pay − price) over the units bought. *You should now be able to say:* "A buyer who pays one price for every unit keeps a gain on every unit except the last."

## Indifference Curves

> A buyer can say which basket is better without saying by how many utils.

@! Go slowly here.

Start with an everyday case. A student has a weekly basket of fruit and likes two fruits, mangoes and guavas. Ask her: "Would you rather have 12 mangoes and 1 guava, or 6 mangoes and 2 guavas?" She may answer, "I like them equally well." She cannot say how many utils either basket gives. She can say only that neither is better. This is enough to build a theory of choice.

> An indifference schedule lists baskets that the buyer likes equally well.

@ Indifference schedule | A table of baskets of two goods that give equal satisfaction.

The student gives these answers, and we record them. An **indifference schedule** means a table of baskets of two goods that a buyer likes equally well. Read the table row by row: each row is a basket, and the buyer is equally happy with all of them. As mangoes fall, the number of guavas needed to keep her equally happy rises.

:table Baskets that the student likes equally well
| Basket | Mangoes | Guavas | Mangoes given up per extra guava |
| A | 12 | 1 | – |
| B | 6 | 2 | 6 |
| C | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| D | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| E | 2 | 6 | 0.5 |

> Plotted on a graph, the baskets lie on a curve that slopes down and bends inward.

@ Indifference curve | A line joining baskets of two goods that give equal satisfaction.

Plot the baskets on a graph, with mangoes on the horizontal axis and guavas on the vertical axis. Join the points. The line is an **indifference curve**, which means a line joining all the baskets that give a buyer equal satisfaction. The curve in Figure 7, marked II, passes through A to E. Every point on it is as good as every other point.

:fig An indifference map. Every point on one curve is equally liked. Curve III is better than curve II, and curve II is better than curve I. | fig_ic.svg

> A set of curves, each better than the one below, forms an indifference map.

@ Indifference map | A set of indifference curves, each higher curve preferred to the lower.

The student has many such curves. A basket with more of both fruits is better, so it lies on a higher curve. A set of curves of this kind is an **indifference map**. In Figure 7, curve III shows baskets better than any on curve II, and curve I shows baskets worse. The map is like the contour lines on a hill map, except that the height is the buyer's well-being.

> The last column of the table gives the rate at which one fruit replaces the other.

@ Marginal rate of substitution | The units of one good given up for one more unit of the other.

The last column of the table needs a name. Moving from A to B, the student gives up 6 mangoes to gain 1 guava. The **marginal rate of substitution** means the number of units of one good a buyer gives up to gain one more unit of the other, while staying equally happy. Here it is 6, then 2, then 1, then 0.5. It falls as we move down the curve. This is the law of diminishing marginal utility (Chapter III) in a new form: when guavas are scarce, one more guava is worth many mangoes, and when guavas are plentiful it is worth few.

> Four properties follow: downward slope, inward bend, no crossing, higher is better.

The curves have four properties. First, they slope downward, because a buyer who gets more of one good must give up some of the other to stay equally happy. Second, they bend inward toward the origin, because the marginal rate of substitution falls. Third, they never cross. If two curves crossed at a basket, that one basket would be on two levels of well-being at once, which is impossible. Fourth, a higher curve is better than a lower one, because it holds more of at least one good.

> Test the map.

*Pause and check.* Can two indifference curves of the same buyer cross each other? Give the reason. The answer is at the back of the book.

> Indifference curves rank baskets, and the marginal rate of substitution falls along them.

*Before moving on.* An indifference curve joins baskets of equal well-being, and the marginal rate of substitution measures the rate of exchange between the two goods. *You should now be able to say:* "A higher indifference curve is better, and the curve bends inward because each extra unit of a good is worth less."

## The Budget Line and Choice

> A buyer's wants are unlimited, but the money to spend is not.

The map shows what the buyer likes. It does not show what she can afford. For that we need her money and the prices.

@ Income (of a consumer) | The money a buyer has to spend in a period.
@ Budget line | A line of all the baskets that cost exactly the whole income.

> Income and the budget line fix what a buyer can afford.
**Income** here means the money a buyer has to spend in a period. A **budget line** means a line joining all the baskets of two goods that cost exactly the whole income. Take the case of the Chapter III customer: income Tk 70, a bun at Tk 10, a cake slice at Tk 20. If she buys only buns, she can buy 70 ÷ 10 = 7. If she buys only cake slices, she can buy 70 ÷ 20 = 3.5. Between these points lie other baskets, such as 5 buns and 1 slice (50 + 20 = 70), 3 buns and 2 slices (30 + 40 = 70), and 1 bun and 3 slices (10 + 60 = 70).

> The slope of the budget line is the price ratio: one slice costs two buns.

The line has a slope that tells us something. One cake slice costs Tk 20, which is the price of two buns. So in the market, giving up 1 slice frees enough money for 2 buns. Baskets inside the line cost less than Tk 70 and are affordable. Baskets outside the line cost more and are not.

> Case file: the customer's budget line.
:case Case file, illustrative. The customer has an income of Tk 70, a bun at Tk 10 and a cake slice at Tk 20. The budget line runs from 7 buns to 3.5 slices. The basket of Chapter III, 3 buns and 2 slices, costs 30 + 40 = Tk 70, so it lies on the line.

> The best affordable basket lies where the budget line touches an indifference curve.

Now put the map and the line together in Figure 8. The buyer wants the highest curve that she can reach. Curve III lies out of reach, because no point of it is on or inside the budget line. Curve I can be reached, but it is low, and she could do better. Curve II touches the budget line at point C, with 3 buns and 2 slices. This is the buyer's equilibrium, and a touching point of this kind is called a tangent point.

:fig The choice of the customer. Curve II touches the budget line at C, 3 buns and 2 cake slices. The curves are drawn to be consistent with Chapter III. | fig_budget.svg

> At the touching point, what the buyer will swap equals what the market asks her to swap.

At C the slope of the curve and the slope of the line are equal. The slope of the curve is the marginal rate of substitution. The slope of the line is the price ratio. So at the best basket, the buyer's willingness to swap equals the market's rate of exchange. Check this with Chapter III. The second cake slice gives 24 utils and the third bun gives 12, so she values a slice at about 2 buns. The market also values a slice at 2 buns, since 20 ÷ 10 = 2. The two agree. In the language of Chapter III, utility per taka is also equal: 12 ÷ 10 = 24 ÷ 20 = 1.2.

> The two methods agree: one counts utils, the other ranks baskets.

This shows that the two methods are one idea. Counting utils gave the rule that utility per taka is equal across goods. Ranking baskets gave the rule that the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio. Both describe the same choice, and the ranking method needs weaker assumptions.

@ Price effect | The change in a buyer's best basket when the price of a good changes.

> A fall in price turns the budget line outward and raises purchases.
Now let the price of buns fall from Tk 10 to Tk 5. If she spent all her income on buns she could now buy 70 ÷ 5 = 14. The budget line swings outward along the bun axis, while the cake end stays at 3.5. She can reach a higher curve and, in the usual case, buys more buns. This change is the **price effect**, which means the change in the best basket when one price changes. If we record the number of buns she buys at each bun price, we obtain her demand curve of Chapter IV.

> Worked example: a budget line from new prices.

*Example 1.* *Given:* income Tk 120, a notebook at Tk 20 and a pen at Tk 10. *Find:* the two ends of the budget line and three baskets on it. *Formula:* price of notebooks × notebooks + price of pens × pens = income, or 20N + 10P = 120.

> The steps: find each end, then test baskets.

*Steps.* All income on notebooks: N = 120 ÷ 20 = 6, with no pens. All income on pens: P = 120 ÷ 10 = 12, with no notebooks. Test 2 notebooks and 8 pens: 20 × 2 + 10 × 8 = 40 + 80 = 120. Test 4 notebooks and 4 pens: 80 + 40 = 120. Test 5 notebooks and 3 pens: 100 + 30 = 130, which is more than 120, so it lies outside. *Answer:* the line runs from 6 notebooks to 12 pens, and the baskets (2, 8) and (4, 4) lie on it. *Check:* the slope is 12 pens for 6 notebooks, so 1 notebook is worth 2 pens, which equals the price ratio 20 ÷ 10 = 2.

> The budget line moves in two ways: income shifts it, a price turns it.

A common slip is to confuse the two ways the line can move. A change in income moves the whole line in parallel, outward if income rises. A change in the price of one good turns the line around the other end, as the bun price fall did above.

> A third test.

*Pause and check.* Could the customer, with Tk 70, buy 4 buns and 2 cake slices? The answer is at the back of the book.

> The best basket is where the line touches the highest reachable curve.

*Before moving on.* The budget line shows what can be bought and its slope is the price ratio. The best basket is at a tangent point, where the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio. *You should now be able to say:* "A buyer chooses the basket on the highest indifference curve that the budget allows."

## Measuring Consumers' Surplus with Indifference Curves

> This section is advanced and may be left for a second reading.

@! Advanced: you may defer this section.

This section shows that the surplus of the first section can be found from indifference curves. It is harder than the rest of the chapter, and the chapters that follow do not depend on it. Read it now if you wish, or return to it later.

> Put money on one axis, and baskets become buns plus money left over.

The method uses a simple trick: let money be the second good. Take the customer with an income of Tk 70 and a bun price of Tk 10. A basket is now a number of buns and the money left over. Because one util is worth Tk 1, a basket is worth the utility of the buns plus the money left, in taka. The table gives the worth of each basket she can buy.

:table Baskets for the customer at a bun price of Tk 10
| Buns | Money left (Tk) | Utility of buns (Tk) | Worth of basket (Tk) |
| 0 | 70 | 0 | 70 |
| 1 | 60 | 20 | 80 |
| 2 | 50 | 36 | 86 |
| 3 | 40 | 48 | 88 |
| 4 | 30 | 56 | 86 |
| 5 | 20 | 60 | 80 |

> The best basket is 3 buns, worth Tk 88, against Tk 70 with no buns.

Read the last column. The basket with 3 buns is worth Tk 88, which is higher than any other, so she buys 3 buns. The basket with no buns is worth Tk 70. All baskets of equal worth lie on one indifference curve. The curve through her best basket therefore meets the money axis at Tk 88. This means that she is as well off with 3 buns and Tk 40 as with no buns and Tk 88.

> The gap between Tk 88 and Tk 70 is the consumers' surplus.

She actually has Tk 70 with no buns. So the extra money that would make her give up all buns and stay equally well off is 88 − 70 = Tk 18. This is the consumers' surplus found in the first section. The two methods agree because money was used as the measure of worth.

> Case file: the second method gives Tk 18 again.
:case Case file, illustrative. For the customer of Chapter III at a bun price of Tk 10, the indifference-curve method gives the same surplus as the first method: Tk 18.

> Two methods, one answer.

*Before moving on.* The surplus is the extra money that would leave the buyer as well off as before without the good. *You should now be able to say:* "The indifference-curve method and the bar method both measure the gain from buying at one price."

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

Consumers' surplus is willingness to pay minus the price paid, summed over the units bought. For the customer in the case it is Tk 18 at a bun price of Tk 10. It is useful in judging taxes, public projects and prices, but it is an estimate. An indifference curve joins baskets of equal well-being. It slopes down, bends inward, never crosses another, and a higher curve is better. The marginal rate of substitution falls along it. The budget line shows the baskets that cost the whole income, and its slope is the price ratio. The best basket lies where the line touches the highest reachable curve, where the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio. The same method also measures consumers' surplus.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Consumers' surplus is not money in the buyer's pocket.

Many students treat consumers' surplus as a refund or a profit. It is neither. It is a measure of the gain in well-being, and no one pays it.

> Two indifference curves of one buyer cannot cross.

Some students draw indifference curves that cross, or curves that slope upward. Neither is allowed, for the reasons given in the second section.

> The budget line does not show what the buyer prefers.

A third mistake is to think that the budget line shows what the buyer likes. It shows what the buyer can afford. The map shows what she likes. Only the two together fix the choice.

## In History

> A French engineer measured the gain from a bridge in 1844.

In 1844 Jules Dupuit, a French engineer, asked how a government should judge the worth of a bridge or a road. He argued that the toll paid is not the worth of the bridge to its users, because many users would pay more than the toll. The worth is the whole area under the demand curve for crossings, not the toll revenue alone. His argument is the root of the idea of consumers' surplus. Alfred Marshall gave the idea its name and its place in the theory of demand in 1890, and cost-benefit studies of public projects still use it.

## Sources

> Three works shaped the two ideas in this chapter.

Francis Edgeworth, in *Mathematical Psychics* (1881), introduced the indifference curve. John Hicks and R. G. D. Allen, in a paper of 1934, rebuilt the theory of demand on ranking alone, without counting utils, which is the method of the third section. Alfred Marshall's *Principles of Economics* (1890) named consumers' surplus.
