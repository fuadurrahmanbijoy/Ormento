:chapter IV | Demand
:mood What makes a crowd buy more?
:desc This chapter shows how the price of a good and the quantity bought move together, and why. It then separates two kinds of change: a move along the demand curve and a shift of the whole curve. It ends with the rare cases where the usual pattern fails.

> A bakery lowers its bun price, and a festival raises sales.
A bakery lowers the price of a bun from Tk 12 to Tk 10, and sales rise. Some weeks later a festival arrives, and sales rise again, though the price is back at Tk 12. Both events brought more buyers. Yet the two are not the same kind of change, and mixing them up leads to wrong conclusions.

> The puzzle is to tell a price effect from other causes.
How can we tell a rise in sales caused by a lower price from a rise caused by something else? The answer needs a clear word for what buyers plan to buy, a picture of how that plan depends on price, and a list of the other things that can change it. This chapter provides all three.

> The chapter builds on marginal utility and on ceteris paribus.
You will need marginal utility (Chapter III) and ceteris paribus (Chapter II). Both are used on almost every page.

## What Demand Means

@! Go slowly here.
> A wish without money does not make demand.
Begin with a student who would love to own a motorcycle. The wish is strong. But the student has no money, and so no shop will ever record a sale. Now picture a family who can afford a motorcycle but has no wish for one. There is no sale here either. A sale needs three things together: the wish, the ability to pay, and the willingness to pay.

@ Demand | In daily life, a strong request or order. In economics, the quantity buyers are able and willing to buy at each price.
> Demand is the quantity buyers are able and willing to buy at each price.
Economists give the name **demand** to this combination. Demand means the quantity of a good that buyers are able and willing to buy at each price, in a given time. Notice the words at each price. Demand is not one number. It is a whole list of prices, each with a quantity, and it needs a time such as one day.

@ Quantity demanded | The amount bought at one price.
> Quantity demanded is the amount bought at one price.
Keep demand apart from **quantity demanded**. Quantity demanded means the amount bought at one particular price. If the neighbourhood buys 240 buns a day at Tk 10, the quantity demanded at Tk 10 is 240. Demand is the whole list. The quantity demanded is one line of it.

@ Demand schedule | A table of prices and quantities.
@ Demand curve | The line drawn from a demand schedule.
> A demand schedule is a table; a demand curve is its line.
A **demand schedule** is a table of prices and quantities demanded. A **demand curve** is the line that draws the schedule on a graph. The schedule and the curve say the same thing in two forms.

> The case file gives the neighbourhood bun market.

> To read the graph, find a price on the side line and go across.
Read Figure 2 as follows. The side line shows the price of a bun in taka. The bottom line shows the number of buns demanded per day. Each dot is one row of the table. The dot at price 10 and quantity 240 means that at Tk 10 buyers will take 240 buns. The straight line through the dots is the demand curve. Starting from any price, move across to the curve and then down to read the quantity.

@ Individual demand | The demand of one buyer.
@ Market demand | The sum of all buyers' demand.
> Market demand is found by adding the demand of each buyer.
**Individual demand** means the demand of one buyer. **Market demand** means the sum of all buyers' demand. To find market demand, add the quantities of all buyers at each price. Never add prices. Prices are the same for all buyers, and quantities are what differ.

> A small example adds two buyers' demand for tea.
*Example.* *Given.* At Tk 4 a cup, buyer A takes 5 cups and buyer B takes 4. At Tk 6, A takes 3 and B takes 2. At Tk 8, A takes 1 and B takes none. *Find.* The market demand at each price. *Formula.* Market quantity at a price = quantity of A + quantity of B at that price.

> The steps add at each price and the check compares totals.
*Steps.* At Tk 4: 5 + 4 = 9. At Tk 6: 3 + 2 = 5. At Tk 8: 1 + 0 = 1. *Answer.* The market demand is 9, 5 and 1 cups at Tk 4, 6 and 8. *Check.* The quantities fall as the price rises for each buyer, and the totals fall too, which is what we expect.

> Recap: demand is the whole schedule; quantity demanded is one point.
*Before moving on.* Demand needs wish, ability and willingness, and it is a schedule of prices and quantities. Quantity demanded is the amount at one price. Market demand adds the quantities of all buyers. *You should now be able to say:* demand is the whole schedule, and quantity demanded is one point on it.

> A short question tests the difference between demand and quantity demanded.
*Pause and check.* The price of tea falls from Tk 6 to Tk 4, and buyers take 9 cups instead of 5. Has the demand for tea changed, or the quantity demanded?

## The Law of Demand

> The table shows that a lower price brings a larger quantity.
Look again at the table. As the price falls from Tk 18 to Tk 8, the quantity rises from 0 to 300. The two figures move in opposite directions. This is true for most goods and most buyers, and it is the first law of the market.

@ Law of demand | A higher price brings a smaller quantity demanded, other things equal.
> The law of demand says price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions.
The **law of demand** states that, other things equal, a higher price of a good brings a smaller quantity demanded, and a lower price brings a larger quantity demanded. The demand curve therefore slopes downward from left to right. Other things equal is the ceteris paribus of Chapter II: income, tastes and the prices of other goods stay unchanged.

> A worked example fills in a schedule from the rule.
*Example.* *Given.* The market rule is Qd = 540 − 30P. *Find.* The quantity demanded at Tk 9, Tk 11 and Tk 15. *Formula.* Quantity demanded = 540 − 30 × price. *Steps.* At Tk 9: 540 − 30 × 9 = 540 − 270 = 270. At Tk 11: 540 − 330 = 210. At Tk 15: 540 − 450 = 90. *Answer.* The quantities are 270, 210 and 90 buns. *Check.* Each lies between the neighbouring rows of the table: 270 between 300 and 240, 210 between 240 and 180, 90 between 120 and 60.

> Each Tk 1 rise in price cuts quantity demanded by 30 buns.
The rule also tells us a simple fact: each rise of Tk 1 in the price lowers the quantity demanded by 30 buns. Check this against the table. From Tk 10 to Tk 12 is a rise of Tk 2, and the quantity falls from 240 to 180, a fall of 60.

> There are three reasons why the curve slopes downward.
Why does the curve slope downward? There are three reasons. The first comes from Chapter III. Marginal utility falls as a buyer takes more. A buyer will pay for each extra bun only what it is worth, so a larger quantity is taken only at a lower price. The customer of Chapter III, with a price of Tk 10, took 3 buns, because the fourth was worth only Tk 8.

@ Income effect | The change in buying power after a price change.
@ Substitution effect | Switching to a cheaper good.
> The income effect and the substitution effect add two further reasons.
The second and third reasons are two effects of a price change. When a price falls, the buyer's money buys more than before, as if income had risen. This is the **income effect**: the change in buying power after a price change. Also, the good becomes cheaper than its rivals, so buyers switch to it. This is the **substitution effect**: switching to a cheaper good. Both effects work in the same direction for most goods.

> The law holds only under stated conditions.
The law holds under conditions. Income, tastes, expectations, the number of buyers and the prices of other goods must stay unchanged. If any of them changes, the whole curve may move, as the next section shows. Section 4.4 describes the rare cases in which the law seems to fail.

> Recap: price and quantity move in opposite directions for three reasons.
*Before moving on.* The law of demand says that a higher price brings a smaller quantity demanded, other things equal. The reasons are falling marginal utility, the income effect and the substitution effect. *You should now be able to say:* a lower price brings a larger quantity, because each extra unit is worth less.

## What Moves the Curve

> A price change moves us along the curve; other changes move the curve itself.
Return to the opening puzzle. A lower bun price moves buyers along the same curve, to a larger quantity. A festival moves the whole curve, because it changes something other than the price. We need two precise terms to keep these apart.

@ Change in quantity demanded | A move along the demand curve, caused by a change in the price.
@ Change in demand | A shift of the whole demand curve, caused by something other than price.
> Only a change in price causes a move along the curve.
A **change in quantity demanded** means a move along the demand curve, caused by a change in the price of the good itself. A **change in demand** means a shift of the whole demand curve, caused by something other than the price of the good. The two names look alike, and many students mix them. Remember: price moves us along, anything else shifts the curve.

@ Determinants of demand | The things other than price that decide demand.
> The determinants of demand are the things other than price that decide demand.
The **determinants of demand** are the things other than price that decide demand. There are five main ones. The first is income: buyers with more income can buy more. The second is tastes: a fashion for a good raises demand. The third is the number of buyers: a growing town buys more. The fourth is expectations: buyers who expect a higher price next month buy more now. The fifth is the price of related goods, which has its own paragraph below.

@ Normal good | A good whose demand rises with income.
@ Inferior good | A good whose demand falls as income rises.
> Income raises the demand for normal goods and lowers it for inferior goods.
Income acts in two ways. For a **normal good**, demand rises when income rises. Most goods are normal: for example, fish and clothes. For an **inferior good**, demand falls when income rises, because buyers switch to something better. Coarse rice or the cheapest cloth may be inferior for some households. Whether a good is inferior depends on the buyer, not on the quality of the good.

@ Substitute goods | Goods used in place of each other.
@ Complementary goods | Goods used together.
> Related goods affect demand: substitutes raise it, complements lower it.
The price of related goods works in two ways. **Substitute goods** are goods used in place of each other, such as tea and coffee. If the price of coffee rises, buyers switch to tea, and the demand for tea rises. **Complementary goods** are goods used together, such as tea and sugar. If the price of sugar rises, buyers take less tea, and the demand for tea falls.

> A fuel example shows a cross effect between related goods.
Here is an application. Many vehicles in Bangladeshi cities can run on petrol or on compressed natural gas, called CNG. Petrol and CNG are substitutes for such vehicles. If the price of petrol rises, some owners switch to CNG, and the demand for CNG rises. Petrol and the vehicles that use it are complements. If petrol becomes dear, fewer people buy vehicles that run only on petrol. The price of one good thus moves the demand for another. Chapter V gives this a number, the cross elasticity.

> The case file uses a festival that raises demand by 60 buns at every price.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Suppose a festival raises the neighbourhood demand for buns by 60 at every price. The rule changes from Qd = 540 − 30P to Qd = 600 − 30P. The table compares the old and new demand. At Tk 10 the quantity demanded rises from 240 to 300, though the price has not changed. This is a change in demand. The whole curve moves to the right, as in Figure 3.

> Recap: price moves us along the curve; other determinants shift the curve.
*Before moving on.* A change in price causes a change in quantity demanded, which is a move along the curve. A change in income, tastes, the number of buyers, expectations or related prices causes a change in demand, which is a shift of the curve. Substitutes and complements, normal goods and inferior goods behave differently.

## Exceptions: Giffen, Snob and Bandwagon

> In a few cases a lower price brings a smaller quantity.
Does the law of demand ever fail? In a few cases it seems to. In each case, as the last chapter taught us to expect, one of the conditions of the law has been broken. Three cases are well known.

@ Giffen good | A good whose demand falls when its price falls.
> A Giffen good is an inferior good that takes a large part of a poor household's budget.
The first is the **Giffen good**, named after the Scottish economist Robert Giffen. A Giffen good is a good whose demand falls when its price falls. It is an inferior good that takes a very large part of a poor household's budget, for example a basic staple food. When its price falls, the household feels richer. Its income effect is strong, and it uses the saved money to buy better food and eats less of the staple. The income effect overcomes the substitution effect. The curve slopes upward over that range.

@ Snob effect | Demand falls as more people buy.
> The snob effect makes a good valued for being rare.
The second is the **snob effect**. The snob effect means that demand falls as more people buy. Some buyers want a good because few others have it, such as a rare watch or a limited edition. When the price falls and many more people buy, the good loses its appeal, and the demand of snobs falls.

@ Bandwagon effect | Demand rises as more people buy.
> The bandwagon effect makes a good valued because others buy it.
The third is the **bandwagon effect**. The bandwagon effect means that demand rises as more people buy. A fashionable clothing style or a popular mobile phone is wanted because others have it. A fall in price brings more buyers, and that attracts still more. The effect can make the demand curve flatter, or even bend upward.

> These cases are rare and each breaks a condition of the law.
How likely are these cases? They are rare. Giffen behaviour needs very poor households and a staple with no good cheap rival. The snob and bandwagon effects need buyers whose tastes depend on what others do. In each case the condition of unchanged tastes, or of a small income effect, has been broken. For most goods and most buyers, the law of demand holds.

> Recap: three exceptions, each with a reason.
*Before moving on.* A Giffen good is a staple whose strong income effect makes demand fall as the price falls. The snob effect lowers demand as more people buy. The bandwagon effect raises demand as more people buy. All three are rare.

## Summary

> Demand is a schedule; price moves along it and other things shift it.
Demand is the quantity buyers are able and willing to buy at each price. The law of demand says that a higher price brings a smaller quantity demanded, because of falling marginal utility, the income effect and the substitution effect. A change in price moves us along the curve. A change in income, tastes, the number of buyers, expectations or related prices shifts the curve. Giffen, snob and bandwagon effects are rare exceptions.

## Think It Through

> A fall in the price of tea and the demand for sugar.
The price of tea falls. Say what happens to the quantity of tea demanded, and what happens to the demand for sugar. Say whether each change is a move along a curve or a shift of a curve, and why. Then say what would happen to the demand for coffee.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Demand is not the same as quantity demanded.
Many students write that demand rises when the price falls. It is the quantity demanded that rises. Demand rises only when the whole curve shifts, and a price change cannot shift it.

> The price of the good itself never shifts its own demand curve.
Many students draw a new curve when the price changes. The price of the good moves us along the same curve. A new curve needs a change in income, tastes, numbers, expectations or the price of another good.

> The Giffen good is not common.
Many students use the Giffen good to dispute the law of demand. It is a rare case that needs special conditions. The law of demand remains true for almost all goods.

## In History

> A study in China found Giffen behaviour among very poor households.
For over a century, Giffen goods were more a puzzle than a fact. In 2008 the economists Robert Jensen and Nolan Miller reported an experiment in Hunan province in China. They gave very poor households a subsidy on rice, the staple of their diet, which lowered its price. Some households ate less rice, not more, and bought other food with the money saved. The study is cited as evidence that Giffen behaviour can occur when households are very poor and the staple takes a large part of the budget.

## Sources

> The ideas of this chapter come from three writers.
Alfred Marshall, *Principles of Economics* (1890), drew the demand curve and stated the law. Thorstein Veblen, *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899), described buying to show wealth. Harvey Leibenstein, in a paper of 1950, named the bandwagon and snob effects.
