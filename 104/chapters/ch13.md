:chapter XIII | Monopoly
:mood What happens when buyers have nowhere else to go?
:desc This chapter studies the market with a single seller. It shows how such a seller chooses a price and an output, what buyers lose when compared with competition, how a seller may charge different buyers different prices, and how public bodies try to control the power of a sole seller.

> A town with one ferry shows the question: how does a sole seller choose a price?

A wide river divides a market town from the villages on the far bank. There is no bridge. The nearest crossing is a day away by road. One family owns the only ferry. The owner could ask Tk 100 from each passenger. Few people would pay, and the boat would cross almost empty. The owner could ask Tk 2, and the boat would be full, but the day's takings would be small. Somewhere between these two fares lies the best one. How does the owner find it?

> The ferry owner chooses the fare; the baker of Chapter XII had to accept one.

Notice what is missing. There is no rival to undercut the owner and no market price to accept. In Chapter XII the baker took the loaf price of Tk 60 from the market and chose only the output. The ferry owner chooses the fare itself. That freedom is a form of power. It has limits, because every rise in the fare drives some passengers away.

> A second question follows: does the owner's best fare also serve the town?

A second question follows from the first. The fare that is best for the owner may not be best for the town. Some villagers who would gladly pay Tk 10, and whose crossing would cost the owner far less, may stay at home. Both sides lose from that missed crossing. This chapter explains how the sole seller chooses, what is lost, and what can be done about it.

> You need the profit rule of Chapter XI, the shapes of Chapter XII and surplus from Chapter VIII.

You will need three earlier ideas. First, the profit rule of Chapter XI: a firm raises output while marginal revenue is above marginal cost. Second, the competitive result of Chapter XII, which we use as a standard of comparison. Third, consumers' and producers' surplus from Chapter VIII, because the cost to buyers is measured in surplus.

## Nature and Kinds

> Chapter XI named monopoly; here we state it exactly and ask where it comes from.

In Chapter XI we met monopoly as a market with one seller. We now state the idea more exactly.

> A pure monopoly has one seller and no close substitute.

@ Pure monopoly | A sole seller of a good that has no close substitute.

A **pure monopoly** is a market with one seller and no close substitute for its good. The ferry is close to this case. A traveller who does not use it must spend a day on the road, and that is a poor substitute. The word *close* matters. A seller of a good that buyers can replace easily has little power, even if it is the only seller.

> Real sellers have more or less power, so pure monopoly is a limiting case.

@ Monopoly power | The ability to set a price above marginal cost without losing all buyers.

Pure monopoly is rare. Most sellers face some substitutes. So economists speak of degrees of **monopoly power**: the ability of a seller to set a price above marginal cost without losing all its buyers. The ferry owner has a great deal of it. A baker in a street with ten bakeries has very little.

> Monopoly power comes from barriers that keep new sellers out.

Where does monopoly power come from? From barriers to entry, which we met in Chapter XI. Four sources are common. A firm may own the only supply of a key input, such as the one mine in a region. A firm may hold a legal right, as described below. A firm may be so large that no rival could match its low cost. Or buyers may have no wish to switch, because the seller's good is the only one that works with their equipment.

> Law can create a monopoly through patents and licences.

@ Patent | A legal right to be the only seller of an invention for a fixed number of years.

@ Legal monopoly | A monopoly created by law, such as a patent or a sole licence.

Law is a strong barrier. A **patent** is a legal right to be the only seller of an invention for a fixed period, usually many years.^1 A **legal monopoly** is any monopoly that the law creates, whether by patent, by sole licence, or by giving one firm the exclusive right to serve a district. The reason is usually to reward effort or to guarantee a service.

^1: Under international trade rules the usual period is 20 years from the date of the application. The exact rules differ by country.

> Some goods are cheapest to supply through one firm, which gives a natural monopoly.

@ Natural monopoly | A market in which one firm can supply the whole demand at lower cost than two or more.

Scale can also create a monopoly. Think of water pipes in a city. If two firms each laid pipes along every road, the cost per household would double. One network is cheapest. A **natural monopoly** is a market where one firm can serve all buyers at a lower cost than two or more firms could. Electricity grids and railways are common examples. No law is needed to create it. Cost itself does so.

> The buying side can also have a single member, called a monopsony.

@ Monopsony | A market with a single buyer.

@ Bilateral monopoly | A market with one seller facing one buyer.

A market can also have one buyer. A **monopsony** is a market with a single buyer. Imagine a town whose only large employer is one factory. Workers there have nowhere else to sell their labour, so the factory can pay a lower wage than a competitive market would give. When a single seller meets a single buyer, we have a **bilateral monopoly**. A mine that sells all its coal to one power station is an example. No rule fixes the price. It lies somewhere between the lowest price the seller will accept and the highest price the buyer will pay, and bargaining settles it.

> Before moving on: sole seller, no close substitute, power from barriers.

*Before moving on.* A pure monopoly has one seller and no close substitute. Monopoly power is a matter of degree. It comes from owned inputs, patents and licences, or scale. A monopsony is one buyer; a bilateral monopoly joins one seller to one buyer.

## Demand, Revenue and Equilibrium

@! Go slowly here: four steps, one at a time.

> The sole seller's demand curve is simply the market demand curve.

We take this section in four steps. The first step is about demand. A competitive baker faces a flat demand line at the market price, because any single baker is too small to move it. A monopolist is different. It is the whole market. So the demand curve it faces is the market demand curve, and that curve slopes down. To sell more, the monopolist must lower its price.

> Everyday bridge: a lone mango seller who lowers the price must lower it for all mangoes.

The second step explains why marginal revenue is lower than price. Start with an everyday case. A sole mango seller sells 10 kg at Tk 50 a kilogram. To sell 11 kg she must lower the price to Tk 48, and she cannot lower it for the eleventh kilogram alone, because buyers would simply wait for the lower price. So all 11 kg sell at Tk 48. Revenue rises from Tk 500 to Tk 528. The extra revenue from the last kilogram is only Tk 28, far below its price of Tk 48.

> Marginal revenue is below price because the lower price applies to every unit sold.

The gain from the new kilogram is its price, Tk 48. The loss is Tk 2 on each of the ten kilograms that were selling at Tk 50. That loss is Tk 20. So the net gain is Tk 48 minus Tk 20, which is Tk 28. This is the whole reason that marginal revenue (the extra revenue from one more unit) is below price for a seller with a downward demand curve. Compare Chapter XII: the price taker lowers no price to sell more, so its marginal revenue equals its price.

> Case file: tabulating the one-seller bun market.

:case Case file, illustrative. Suppose one seller served the whole neighbourhood bun market of Chapter IV. Its demand is the market demand, P = 18 − Q/30, where P is the price in taka and Q is the number of buns a day. Total revenue is price times quantity, so TR = 18Q − Q²/30. Marginal revenue is MR = 18 − Q/15. Marginal cost is MC = 6 + Q/30, the same cost line as the supply curve of Chapter VII.

> Reading the table: each row gives price, revenue, and the two margins.

In the table, read each row from left to right. Price comes from the demand line. Total revenue is price times quantity. Marginal revenue and marginal cost are taken from their formulas at that output. Marginal revenue falls twice as fast as price. At 90 buns the price is Tk 15 but marginal revenue is only Tk 12.

:table The one-seller bun market, per day (case file, illustrative)
| Buns (Q) | Price (Tk) | Total revenue (Tk) | Marginal revenue (Tk) | Marginal cost (Tk) |
| 0 | 18 | 0 | 18 | 6 |
| 30 | 17 | 510 | 16 | 7 |
| 60 | 16 | 960 | 14 | 8 |
| 90 | 15 | 1,350 | 12 | 9 |
| 120 | 14 | 1,680 | 10 | 10 |
| 150 | 13 | 1,950 | 8 | 11 |
| 180 | 12 | 2,160 | 6 | 12 |

> The rule for a straight demand line: same starting point, twice the slope.

The pattern in the table gives a rule. When demand is a straight line, marginal revenue is also a straight line. It starts at the same price on the vertical axis and falls twice as fast. Demand P = 18 − Q/30 has slope 1/30, so marginal revenue has slope 2/30, which is 1/15. That is why MR = 18 − Q/15. The marginal revenue line reaches zero at 270 buns, where total revenue is highest.

> Marginal revenue is zero where demand has unit elasticity, so the seller avoids the inelastic range.

This links to Chapter V. At 270 buns the price is Tk 9, and we found there that elasticity is exactly one. Beyond that point marginal revenue is negative: selling more would reduce total revenue. A seller who also pays to make each bun would never go that far. So a monopolist always operates on the elastic part of its demand curve, where elasticity is above one.

> Step three: the profit rule still applies, so output is set where MR equals MC.

The third step is the choice of output. The profit rule of Chapter XI does not change. If marginal revenue is above marginal cost, one more unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so output should rise. If marginal revenue is below marginal cost, output should fall. Profit is highest where the two are equal. In the table the two meet at 120 buns, where both are Tk 10.

> Step four: the price is read from the demand curve, not from the cost curve.

The fourth step is the price, and it is where beginners often slip. The rule MR = MC gives the output only. To find the price, go up from that output to the demand curve and read the price buyers will pay. At 120 buns the demand line gives Tk 14. The price is above marginal cost (Tk 10). That gap is the monopolist's power at work. In a competitive market the price equals marginal cost, as Chapter XII showed.

:fig The one-seller bun market. The seller sets output where marginal revenue meets marginal cost (B), then reads the price from the demand curve (A). Competition would give point C. | fig_monopoly.svg

> Worked example: finding the monopoly output and price by algebra.

*Worked example.* *Given:* demand P = 18 − Q/30 and marginal cost MC = 6 + Q/30. *Find:* the profit-maximising output and price. *Formula:* in words, marginal revenue equals marginal cost; in symbols, MR = MC. We first need the MR line. Here are the steps.

:table Steps for the monopoly output
| Step | Working |
| 1. Marginal revenue: same intercept, double slope | MR = 18 − Q/15 |
| 2. Set MR equal to MC | 18 − Q/15 = 6 + Q/30 |
| 3. Move the terms in Q to one side | 18 − 6 = Q/15 + Q/30 |
| 4. Combine, using a common denominator of 30 | 12 = 2Q/30 + Q/30 = 3Q/30 = Q/10 |
| 5. Solve for Q | Q = 12 × 10 = 120 buns |
| 6. Read the price from the demand line | P = 18 − 120/30 = 18 − 4 = Tk 14 |

> The check: MR and MC agree at 120, and revenue is price times quantity.

*Answer:* the seller offers 120 buns a day at Tk 14 each. *Check:* at 120 buns, MR = 18 − 120/15 = 18 − 8 = 10, and MC = 6 + 120/30 = 6 + 4 = 10, so the two agree. Total revenue is 14 × 120 = Tk 1,680, which matches the table. Variable cost is the area under the marginal cost line, 960, so revenue above variable cost is Tk 720. This equals the producers' surplus of the next section. Fixed cost is not given, so we cannot say more about profit.

> Common slips: using the cost line for price, or forgetting to double the slope.

*Common slips.* First, reading the price from the marginal cost line instead of the demand line. Second, writing the MR slope as 1/30 instead of 1/15. Third, mixing units: P is in taka, Q in buns a day. Fourth, expecting a *supply curve*. A monopolist has none. It does not respond to a given price. It chooses price and output together from its demand and cost.

> Before moving on: demand is the market's, MR is below price, price is read from demand.

*Before moving on.* The monopolist faces the market demand curve. Its marginal revenue is below price. It picks the output where MR equals MC, then reads the price from the demand curve.

> Pause and check: should the seller raise output from 90 to 120 buns, and then from 120 to 150?

*Pause and check.* Use the table. Moving from 90 to 120 buns, total revenue rises from Tk 1,350 to Tk 1,680. What is the extra revenue per bun? Marginal cost over this step is between Tk 9 and Tk 10. Should the seller go ahead? Repeat for 120 to 150 buns. (Answer at the back.)

> You should now be able to say why a sole seller's price exceeds marginal cost.

*You should now be able to say:* "A sole seller sets output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and then charges the highest price buyers will pay for that output, which is above marginal cost."

## Compared with Competition

> We compare the one-seller market with the competitive market using the same demand and cost.

Now use the same demand and the same cost lines to compare two worlds. In the first, many bakers compete and the price settles where demand equals supply. In the second, one seller serves the whole market. Because the buyers and the costs are the same, any difference comes from the market structure alone.

> Case file: comparing the competitive and one-seller results.

:case Case file, illustrative. Under competition (Chapter VIII), P = 18 − Q/30 meets P = 6 + Q/30 at Q = 180 and P = Tk 12. Under one seller, Q = 120 and P = Tk 14. Consumers' surplus is 540 under competition. Under the single seller it is the triangle between the demand line and the price of Tk 14, which is ½ × 120 × (18 − 14) = Tk 240. Producers' surplus is revenue less the area under marginal cost: 1,680 − 960 = Tk 720.

> The table sets the two markets side by side.

The table sets the results side by side. The first column is the competitive market, the second the one-seller market.

:table Competition and one seller compared, per day (case file, illustrative)
| Measure | Competitive market | One seller |
| Output (buns) | 180 | 120 |
| Price (Tk) | 12 | 14 |
| Consumers' surplus (Tk) | 540 | 240 |
| Producers' surplus (Tk) | 540 | 720 |
| Deadweight loss (Tk) | 0 | 120 |
| Total surplus (Tk) | 1,080 | 960 |

> Buyers lose 300, the seller gains 180, and 120 vanishes.

Look at the changes. Buyers lose Tk 300 of surplus. The seller gains only Tk 180. The difference, Tk 120, is gained by nobody. Part of what buyers lose is a plain transfer: they pay Tk 2 more on each of 120 buns, which is Tk 240, and the seller receives it. The rest disappears.

> Deadweight loss is the surplus that vanishes when output is cut.

@ Deadweight loss | The surplus lost to everyone when output falls below the competitive level.

This vanished amount is the **deadweight loss**: the surplus lost to everyone when output falls below the competitive level. Consider the 60 buns between 120 and 180. Each is worth to a buyer between Tk 12 and Tk 14. Each would cost the seller between Tk 10 and Tk 12 to make. So each is worth more than it costs, yet none is made, because the seller sees that selling them would force down the price on the other 120 buns. The gain from these trades is lost.

:fig Surplus under one seller. The loss falls on buyers and, in part, on the seller. | fig_welfare.svg

> The figure shows the three areas; the lost triangle lies between the monopoly and the competitive output.

In the figure, the hatched triangle above the price is consumers' surplus (240). The crossed area between the price and marginal cost is producers' surplus (720). The small dark triangle is the deadweight loss (120). Its corners are the monopoly point, the point on marginal cost directly below it, and the competitive point.

> A fair judgement also counts what monopoly may bring.

A fair judgement does not stop there. Monopoly is not always worse in every way. A natural monopoly may have much lower cost than several small firms, and then one firm may give cheaper service even after allowing for its power. A patent gives the inventor a reward that may make the invention possible. On the other hand, a sole seller faces little pressure to keep its costs low. So the result depends on the case, and public policy tries to separate the harmful cases from the useful ones.

> Viewpoint: buyer and owner see the same market differently.

Seen from the buyer's seat, monopoly means a higher price and fewer goods. Seen from the owner's seat, it means a higher price and a larger share of the surplus. Seen from the town's seat, it means a smaller total.

## Price Discrimination

> The ferry owner might do even better by charging different people different fares.

Suppose the ferry owner could charge students less than office workers. Would that be wise? Often it is, and many sellers do it.

> Price discrimination means different prices for the same good.

@ Price discrimination | Selling the same good at different prices to different buyers, for reasons not based on cost.

**Price discrimination** means selling the same good at different prices to different buyers, where the difference is not due to cost. A cinema charges less for afternoon shows. A doctor charges richer patients more. A bus company offers lower fares to children. The key words are *same good*: if the price differs because the cost differs, as with a longer journey, that is not discrimination.

> Three conditions must hold for the plan to work.

Three conditions are needed. The seller must have some power over price, so it is not a price taker. The seller must be able to keep the groups apart, so that people who pay the low price cannot resell to those who pay the high price. And the groups must differ in how strongly they react to price, which means different elasticities. A cinema separates groups by showtime and by student card.

> The rule: charge more where demand is less elastic, so that MR is equal in each market.

The rule for the seller follows from the profit rule. Treat each group as its own market. In each market set marginal revenue equal to marginal cost. Since marginal cost is the same, marginal revenue ends up equal in both markets. The group whose demand is less elastic gets the higher price. We show this with a worked case.

> Case file: a small cinema shows two prices for one good.

:case Case file, illustrative. This example uses a small town cinema, not the bakery. Cost per seat filled is Tk 10, whatever the show. The evening show draws working adults, with demand P = 30 − Q/10, where Q is seats sold. The afternoon show draws students, with demand P = 20 − Q/20. Fixed costs are left out.

:table Steps for the discriminating seller
| Step | Working |
| 1. Marginal revenue in each market: same intercept, double slope | Evening: 30 − Q/5; afternoon: 20 − Q/10 |
| 2. Evening: set MR = MC | 30 − Q/5 = 10, so Q = 100 seats |
| 3. Evening price | P = 30 − 100/10 = Tk 20 |
| 4. Afternoon: set MR = MC | 20 − Q/10 = 10, so Q = 100 seats |
| 5. Afternoon price | P = 20 − 100/20 = Tk 15 |
| 6. Profit with two prices | (20 − 10) × 100 + (15 − 10) × 100 = 1,000 + 500 = Tk 1,500 |
| 7. Compare one price, Tk 17 | Evening 300 − 10 × 17 = 130; afternoon 400 − 20 × 17 = 60; (17 − 10) × 190 = Tk 1,330 |

> The two-price plan beats any single price, and the higher price falls on the less elastic group.

*Answer:* the cinema charges Tk 20 in the evening and Tk 15 in the afternoon, and earns Tk 1,500. *Check:* the best single price (about Tk 16.67) would earn only about Tk 1,333, so two prices do better. Elasticity at the chosen points confirms the rule: in the evening it is 10 × 20/100 = 2.0, and in the afternoon 20 × 15/100 = 3.0. The group that reacts less strongly to price, the evening audience, pays more.

> Effects are mixed: some buyers lose, others gain.

The effects on buyers are mixed. Compare a single price of Tk 17. Evening buyers now pay Tk 20 instead of Tk 17, so they lose. Students pay Tk 15 instead of Tk 17, so they gain. Total seats sold are 200, against 190. In the extreme case of *perfect price discrimination*, where each buyer pays the most he or she would pay, the seller takes all consumers' surplus, but output reaches the competitive level and no deadweight loss remains.

## Controlling Monopoly

> Governments use three tools: regulation, competition law and public ownership.

Because monopoly can harm buyers, governments act. Three tools are common.

> Regulation sets rules on price or conduct; a ceiling can raise output.

@ Regulation | Rules set by a public body on a firm's prices or conduct.

**Regulation** means rules set by a public body on a firm's prices or conduct. The usual rule is a price ceiling. A well-set ceiling can even raise output. Return to the one-seller bun market. Left alone, the seller offers 120 buns at Tk 14. Suppose the price may not exceed Tk 12. The seller can sell up to 180 buns at that price, so marginal revenue is a flat Tk 12 up to 180 buns, while marginal cost reaches Tk 12 at exactly 180 buns. Output rises to the competitive level. A ceiling set too low, however, can bring a loss that drives the firm out, so regulators set it with care. For a natural monopoly, a price equal to marginal cost may not cover total cost, so a higher price is allowed.

> Competition law and public ownership are the other two tools.

Competition law forbids abuse of power, such as agreements to fix prices, and can stop a merger that would create a dominant firm. In Bangladesh the Competition Act 2012 set up a Competition Commission for this purpose (Chapter II). The third tool is public ownership, where the state owns the firm. Railways and water supply are often run this way. Its risk is that a public firm may also face little pressure to keep costs low. Each tool has costs, so the choice depends on the market.

## Summary

> Summary of the chapter in six sentences.

A monopoly has one seller. Its power comes from barriers: inputs, law or scale. The monopolist's demand curve is the market's, and marginal revenue lies below price, so it sets MR equal to MC and reads the price from demand. In the bun market this gives 120 buns at Tk 14, against 180 at Tk 12 under competition. Buyers lose more than the seller gains, and the difference is deadweight loss (Tk 120). A seller may charge different groups different prices if it can keep them apart. Governments respond by regulation, competition law and public ownership.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common mistakes about monopoly.

*"A monopolist can charge any price it likes."* It can choose its price, but buyers then choose how much to buy. A high price loses buyers. *"The monopolist reads its price from the marginal cost line."* It reads the price from the demand line. *"Monopoly profit is the same as deadweight loss."* Profit goes to the seller; deadweight loss goes to nobody. *"Price discrimination is always unfair."* It can bring in buyers who would otherwise be left out, as the student audience shows, though it can also raise prices for the less sensitive group.

## In History

> The break-up of Standard Oil in 1911.

Standard Oil was founded in Ohio in 1870. By the 1880s it controlled most of the oil refining in the United States, and railways gave it special rates that rivals could not match. The Sherman Act of 2 July 1890 made such monopolising unlawful (Chapter XI). On 15 May 1911 the United States Supreme Court ruled that Standard Oil was an unlawful monopoly and ordered it divided into separate companies. Economists still debate how much the break-up helped buyers, but the case shows a state using competition law to dissolve a monopoly.
