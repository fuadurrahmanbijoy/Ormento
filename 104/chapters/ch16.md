:chapter XVI | Interest and Profit
:mood The reward for waiting, and the reward for daring.
:desc A bank lends money and asks for more back. A baker risks savings and may gain or lose. This chapter shows why money earns interest, how the rate is set, what profit really is, and why it appears at all.

> A lender is paid for waiting, and an owner for running a business; the chapter asks why.

At the end of one month the baker's books show Tk 48,000 of profit. A friend who works in an office says, "You have earned almost nothing. I earn Tk 40,000 and do not risk my savings." Who is right?

> The same figure looks large to the books and small to the friend.

A second puzzle sits beside it. The baker wants a second oven and asks a bank for Tk 400,000. The bank agrees, but it will ask for more than Tk 400,000 back. Why should anyone pay for the use of money? Money does not bake bread.

> A second puzzle: why is a price paid for the use of money.

These two puzzles lead to the last two rewards in our list of inputs. In Chapter XV we met rent for land and wages for labour. Here we study interest for capital and profit for the entrepreneur.

> Interest and profit complete the four rewards of production.

## 16.1 Interest

> In daily speech interest means curiosity; in economics it means a payment for money.

@ Interest | The payment made for the use of money for a period of time.

In daily speech, *interest* means curiosity: "an interest in cricket". In economics, **interest** is the payment made for the use of money for a period of time. A borrower pays it; a lender receives it. It is usually stated as a percentage of the sum for a year, called the *rate of interest*.

> A short example shows the arithmetic of simple interest.

*Given.* A loan of Tk 400,000 at 12% a year, for one year. *Find.* The interest. *Formula.* Interest = sum lent x rate x years. *Steps.* 400,000 x 0.12 x 1 = 48,000. *Answer.* The interest is Tk 48,000 a year, which is Tk 4,000 a month. *Check.* Ten per cent of 400,000 is 40,000, and a further 2% is 8,000, so the total is 48,000.

> Why does money earn interest? Because the lender gives up something.

@ Time preference | The tendency to value present goods more than the same goods in the future.

The answer begins with **time preference**, the tendency to value goods in the present more than the same goods in the future. Most people would rather have Tk 1,000 today than Tk 1,000 a year from now. To give up money today, a lender must be paid. The Austrian economist Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk made this idea central to the theory of interest.^1

> A second reason is that money can be used to make more goods.

A second reason concerns the borrower. Money lets the baker buy an oven that makes more loaves. If the oven earns Tk 60,000 a year, the baker can afford to pay part of that sum for the loan. The borrower's gain from using the money sets the highest interest he would pay. The lender's wish to be paid for waiting sets the lowest he would accept.

> Two forms of interest: what the lender receives, and what is left for waiting alone.

@ Gross interest | The whole payment a lender receives, including pay for risk and for the cost of lending.

@ Net interest | The part of gross interest that pays only for waiting.

The total payment is **gross interest**. It contains more than pay for waiting. Suppose a lender charges 14%. Of this, 8% pays for waiting, 4% covers the chance that the borrower will not repay, and 2% covers the cost of handling the loan. The 8% left for waiting is **net interest**.

## 16.2 How Interest Rates Are Determined

> The rate of interest is a price, so demand and supply set it.

Interest is the price of using money, so we can apply demand and supply. Economists give two views. They look at different sides of the same market and should be read together.

> The first view: the rate balances the demand for and supply of loans.

@ Loanable funds | Money offered for lending and money sought for borrowing.

In the **loanable funds** view, the rate balances the money offered for lending against the money sought for borrowing. Firms borrow to build and buy. Households borrow to buy homes. The government borrows to pay for projects. Savers, and banks creating credit, supply the funds. A higher rate makes borrowers want less and lenders offer more.

> A table shows the demand and supply of loans at five rates.

:table Loanable funds at different rates (Tk million, illustrative)
| Rate of interest (%) | Funds sought | Funds offered | Position |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 | 90 | 20 | Excess demand |
| 8 | 70 | 35 | Excess demand |
| 10 | 50 | 50 | Equilibrium |
| 12 | 30 | 65 | Excess supply |
| 14 | 10 | 80 | Excess supply |

> The rate settles where funds sought equal funds offered.

At 6% borrowers want Tk 90 million but lenders offer only Tk 20 million. Competition among borrowers pushes the rate up. At 14% lenders offer more than borrowers want, and the rate is pushed down. The balance comes at 10%, where both are Tk 50 million. A rise in saving moves the supply to the right and lowers the rate. A rise in business hope moves the demand to the right and raises it.

> The second view: people choose how to hold their wealth, in cash or in loans.

@ Liquidity preference | The wish to hold wealth in cash instead of in forms that earn interest.

John Maynard Keynes proposed the second view in 1936. Holding cash earns no interest, yet people hold it. They need it for daily purchases, for emergencies, and sometimes because they expect to buy something cheaper later. This wish to hold cash is **liquidity preference**. To persuade people to part with cash, the rate of interest must be high enough. The supply of money is set by the central bank, here Bangladesh Bank, and the rate settles where the demand for cash equals that supply.

> Both views agree that the rate is a price set by two sides.

The two views agree on the main point: interest is a price, and it changes when demand or supply changes. They differ on which changes matter most. For a beginner, the loanable funds view is the easier first step.

> A nominal rate hides the effect of rising prices.

@ Nominal interest rate | The rate of interest stated in money, before allowing for rising prices.

@ Real interest rate | The rate of interest after the effect of rising prices is removed.

The **nominal interest rate** is the rate stated in the loan. The **real interest rate** is the rate after removing the rise in prices. As a rule, real rate is about nominal rate minus the rate of price rise. A loan at 12% when prices rise 7% a year pays a real rate of about 5%.^2 If prices rose by 12%, the lender would gain nothing in real terms.

> Check the real-rate rule with an example.

*Given.* Nominal rate 12%; prices rise 7% a year. *Find.* The real rate. *Formula.* Real rate = nominal rate - rate of price rise (approximately). *Steps.* 12 - 7 = 5. *Answer.* About 5% a year. *Check.* A lender who is repaid Tk 112 for each Tk 100 lent can buy only about 5% more goods than before.

## 16.3 Profit

> Profit is the difference between what a firm earns and what it costs.

@ Accounting profit | Revenue minus explicit costs, as recorded in the firm's books.

@ Economic profit | Revenue minus all costs, including implicit costs.

In everyday talk, profit is what is left after paying the bills. An accountant calls this **accounting profit**: revenue minus explicit costs, the costs actually paid. An economist asks for more. **Economic profit** is revenue minus *all* costs, including implicit costs, the costs of owned resources that nobody pays in cash (Chapter X). The owner's own time and money are resources, and they could have earned money elsewhere.

> The bakery's month shows both kinds of profit.

The case file of Chapter I gives the bakery's figures. Revenue is Tk 252,000 a month and explicit cost is Tk 204,000. Accounting profit is therefore Tk 48,000. The owner's forgone pay is Tk 40,000 and forgone interest is Tk 3,000, both implicit costs.

:table Accounting and economic profit of the bakery, one month (illustrative)
| Item | Tk |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue | 252,000 |
| Explicit costs | 204,000 |
| Accounting profit | 48,000 |
| Less owner's forgone pay | 40,000 |
| Less forgone interest on savings | 3,000 |
| Economic profit | 5,000 |

> The friend in the opening puzzle was partly right: much of the profit pays for the owner's own time and money.

Now the opening puzzle has an answer. Of the Tk 48,000, Tk 43,000 only repays the owner for what he gave up. The friend was almost right: only Tk 5,000 is a gain beyond the owner's best alternative.

> Normal profit is the minimum reward that keeps an entrepreneur in the business.

In Chapter X we called the owner's minimum reward *normal profit*. It is a cost of production, because a firm that cannot pay it will lose its entrepreneur. When economic profit is above zero, the firm earns **supernormal profit** (Chapter XII) in addition to normal profit. When economic profit is below zero, the owner would do better elsewhere.

## 16.4 Why Profit Occurs

> Economists give several answers to the question of why profit exists.

If competition drives economic profit to zero in the long run (Chapter XII), why does profit appear at all? Economists have given five main answers. Each explains a different part of the picture.

> The oldest answer: profit pays for taking a risk.

The first is the *risk-bearing* view. Every business may fail. The owner puts money in, and may lose it. Profit is the reward for taking this risk. An American economist, Frederick Hawley, put this view in 1893. Many risks, such as fire, can be insured, and an insurance premium is simply a cost. So the pay for risk alone cannot explain all profit.

> Knight's view: the reward is for facing what cannot be calculated.

@ Uncertainty | A situation in which the chances of outcomes cannot be known.

Frank Knight drew a sharp line in 1921.^3 A risk whose chances can be counted, like fire, can be insured. **Uncertainty** is a situation in which the chances cannot be known: will customers like a new product? Nobody can insure against this. The entrepreneur bears it, and profit is the reward. In Knight's view profit is the reward of uncertainty, not of risk.

> Schumpeter's view: profit comes from doing something new.

@ Innovation | The introduction of a new product, method or market.

Joseph Schumpeter wrote in 1911 that profit comes from **innovation**, the introduction of something new. A baker who first sells a milk bun, or who first delivers by bicycle, earns more than the rest, until others copy. Competition then removes the gain, and the next innovator earns a new profit. On this view profit is temporary but never disappears for long.

> Another view treats the entrepreneur as one more input with a marginal product.

The *marginal productivity* view treats the entrepreneur like any input. His reward equals the extra output his skill adds. The idea has a weakness: there is usually one entrepreneur in a firm, so it is hard to say what one *extra* entrepreneur would add. It is still useful, because it links profit to the value the entrepreneur creates.

> Some profit arises by chance, and some from market power.

@ Windfall profit | Unexpected profit that comes by chance, not by the owner's effort or skill.

Not every profit is earned. A **windfall profit** comes by chance. If floods destroy a neighbour's stock, a baker with sacks of flour in store may sell at a much higher price. And a monopoly (Chapter XIII) may earn profit through market power, by keeping price above cost. The table sets out the theories side by side.

:table Five views of profit
| View | Source of profit | Main thinker | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Risk-bearing | Taking risk | Hawley, 1893 | Insurable risks are only a cost |
| Uncertainty | Facing the unknowable | Knight, 1921 | Hard to separate from risk |
| Innovation | New products and methods | Schumpeter, 1911 | Not all profit is innovative |
| Marginal productivity | Entrepreneur's own product | Early marginalists | One entrepreneur: no "extra" |
| Windfall or market power | Chance or monopoly | Various | Not a reward for effort |

> No single view is complete; each fits some cases.

No single view is complete. A farmer whose crop depends on rain fits the uncertainty view. A firm with a new product fits the innovation view. A firm with a sole licence fits the market-power view. Good analysis picks the theory that fits the case.

## 16.5 The Whole Picture

> Four inputs, four rewards: this is the summary of Chapters XV and XVI.

We can now put the four rewards of production together. Chapter I named four kinds of resource. Each has its own reward and its own price.

:table The four inputs and their rewards
| Input | Reward | Where studied |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Rent | Chapter XV |
| Labour | Wages | Chapter XV |
| Capital | Interest | Chapter XVI |
| Entrepreneur | Profit | Chapter XVI |

> The rewards depend on one another: a firm must pay the first three before profit remains.

The rewards depend on one another. Revenue pays rent, wages and interest first, and what remains is profit. Profit can be negative: the entrepreneur bears the loss. That is why profit is called the *residual* reward. A rise in wages, for example, lowers profit unless the price rises.

> Microfinance applies the theory of interest to poor borrowers.

@ Microfinance | The supply of small loans and other financial services to poor people.

**Microfinance** is the supply of small loans and other financial services to poor people. Bangladesh is the home of a famous example. The Grameen project began in 1976 with small loans to villagers near Chittagong, and became the Grameen Bank by law in 1983.^4 Poor borrowers could not offer a bank anything to secure a loan, and small loans cost much to manage. In the loanable-funds view, funds did not reach them. Microfinance brought the lenders and these borrowers together. Debate continues over the rates charged and the risk of too much debt.

## Worked Case: The Second Oven

> The bakery borrows to buy a second oven and sees what interest does to profit.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* In a month of 30 days the bakery bakes 140 loaves a day. Revenue is Tk 252,000 and cost is Tk 204,000 (Tk 6,800 x 30). Accounting profit is Tk 48,000. The owner's forgone pay is Tk 40,000 and forgone interest is Tk 3,000, so economic profit is Tk 5,000 a month.

> The loan adds a new explicit cost.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* The owner borrows Tk 400,000 for a second oven at 12% a year. Interest is Tk 48,000 a year, or Tk 4,000 a month. This is an explicit cost, paid in cash. Before the oven earns anything, accounting profit falls to 48,000 - 4,000 = Tk 44,000, and economic profit falls to 5,000 - 4,000 = Tk 1,000.

> The oven is worth buying only if it earns more than it costs in interest.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* The oven is worth buying only if it adds more than Tk 4,000 a month to revenue, after its running costs. *Check.* If it added Tk 3,000, the net effect would be a fall of Tk 1,000, and economic profit would drop from Tk 5,000 to Tk 4,000. The rule is to compare the gain with the interest.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in one place.

Interest is the payment for the use of money. It arises from time preference and from the use of money to produce more. Gross interest includes pay for risk and cost; net interest pays for waiting. The rate is set by loanable funds, or by liquidity preference and the supply of money; the real rate is about the nominal rate minus the rate of price rise. Accounting profit counts explicit costs only; economic profit counts all costs, and normal profit is the minimum reward of the entrepreneur. Profit is explained by risk, uncertainty, innovation, marginal productivity, windfall and market power. Rent, wages, interest and profit are the four rewards of production.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common mistakes, each corrected.

*"Interest is only paid on borrowed money."* An owner's own money also has an interest cost, the interest forgone. This is an implicit cost.

> The second mistake, with its correction.
*"A high nominal rate is always a good return."* If prices rise faster, the real rate is low or negative.

> The third mistake, with its correction.
*"Accounting profit is the true profit."* It leaves out implicit costs. A firm with accounting profit may have an economic loss.

> The fourth mistake, with its correction.
*"Profit is only a reward for hard work."* Profit may come from risk, uncertainty, innovation, chance or market power.

## In History

> A true account from Bangladesh.

In 1976 the economist Muhammad Yunus lent small sums to villagers near Chittagong who had been unable to borrow from banks. Many repaid. The project grew, and in 1983 it became the Grameen Bank. The idea spread to many countries. Its lesson for this chapter is that interest and risk depend on who can reach the loanable funds, and that new ways of lending can change that.
