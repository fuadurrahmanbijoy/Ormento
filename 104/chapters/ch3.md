:chapter III | Utility and Consumption
:mood Why does the third bun please less than the first?
:desc This chapter explains why a buyer values each extra unit of a good a little less than the one before. It shows how this simple fact can be written as a table and a law, where the law seems to fail, and how a buyer with a fixed budget decides what to buy.

> A hungry customer finds each bun less welcome than the last.
A customer walks into a bakery, hungry, and eats a bun. It is delicious. A second bun is still good. By the third, the pleasure is weaker. By the fifth, the customer leaves half of it on the plate. Yet each bun cost the same Tk 10. The buns did not change. Something in the customer did.

> The puzzle is how anyone decides how many to buy.
This raises a puzzle. If each extra bun is worth less to the customer, how does the customer decide when to stop? And if the customer has money for other things too, such as a slice of cake, how is the money shared between the two? This chapter answers both questions, step by step.

> The chapter builds on choice, opportunity cost and ceteris paribus.
You will need three earlier ideas: scarcity and choice (Chapter I), opportunity cost (Chapter I), and ceteris paribus (Chapter II). All three return here.

## The Idea of Utility

@! Go slowly here.
> A hungry student's samosas show satisfaction falling with each one.
Begin with a hungry student at a tea stall. The first samosa is a great relief. The second is still welcome. The third is pleasant, but the hunger is gone. A fourth would be too much. Each samosa was the same size and the same price. The pleasure from each one fell as the number eaten rose. Notice that the student never stopped to count this pleasure. It is felt, not calculated.

@ Utility | In daily life, the usefulness of a tool. In economics, the satisfaction a person gets from a good.
> Utility is the satisfaction a person gets from a good.
Economists need a word for this pleasure. The word is **utility**. Utility means the satisfaction a person gets from using a good. A samosa gives utility to a hungry person. A bus ride gives utility to a person late for work. A good gives utility if someone wants it, whether or not it is good for that person.

@ Total utility | All the satisfaction from the units consumed.
@ Marginal utility | The extra satisfaction from one more unit.
> Total utility is all the satisfaction; marginal utility is the extra from one more unit.
Two kinds of utility must be kept apart. **Total utility** means all the satisfaction from all the units consumed. **Marginal utility** means the extra satisfaction from one more unit. If three samosas give a total of 21 units of pleasure and four samosas give 22, then the marginal utility of the fourth samosa is 22 − 21 = 1.

@ Margin | The edge: the next or last unit.
@ Util | A made-up unit of satisfaction.
> Margin means the edge; a util is a made-up unit of satisfaction.
The word **margin** means the edge, or the next step. Marginal means at the edge: the one more unit that is added, or the last unit taken. To put numbers on satisfaction we use a made-up unit, the **util**. A util is a made-up unit of satisfaction. It has no existence outside the book, as a metre has outside a ruler. It lets us write a feeling as a number.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* A customer at the bakery eats buns. We count one util as worth Tk 1. The marginal utility of buns 1 to 5 is 20, 16, 12, 8 and 4 utils. The price of a bun at opening is Tk 10. The table gives these figures, and adds total utility.

> We find total utility by adding the marginal utilities one by one.
*Given.* Marginal utility of buns 1 to 5: 20, 16, 12, 8, 4 utils. *Find.* The total utility after each bun. *Formula.* Total utility of n units = total utility of (n − 1) units + marginal utility of the nth unit. In symbols, TU(n) = TU(n − 1) + MU(n), where TU is total utility and MU is marginal utility.

> The steps show total utility rising while marginal utility falls.
*Steps.* After 1 bun: 20. After 2 buns: 20 + 16 = 36. After 3: 36 + 12 = 48. After 4: 48 + 8 = 56. After 5: 56 + 4 = 60. *Answer.* The totals are 20, 36, 48, 56 and 60 utils. *Check.* Going backwards, 60 − 56 = 4, which is the marginal utility of the fifth bun. Notice that the total keeps rising, though more slowly, while the marginal utility falls.

> A common slip is to confuse total utility with marginal utility.
*Common slips.* Students often confuse the two columns. Total utility is the running sum. Marginal utility is the step added at each line. A fall in marginal utility does not mean that total utility falls.

> Recap: total is all the satisfaction, and marginal is the extra.
*Before moving on.* Utility is satisfaction. Total utility is all of it. Marginal utility is the extra from one more unit. *You should now be able to say:* total utility is the whole, marginal utility is the last step added.

> A short question tests marginal and total utility.
*Pause and check.* A buyer's total utility from 4 mangoes is 30 utils and from 5 mangoes is 33 utils. What is the marginal utility of the fifth mango?

## Can Satisfaction Be Measured?

> Satisfaction cannot be seen, so we must decide how to count it.
Satisfaction is a feeling inside a person. No instrument can read it. How, then, can we write it as numbers, as the table did? Economists have two answers, and the book will use both.

@ Cardinal | Measured in numbers.
@ Ordinal | Ranked as first, second, third.
> Cardinal measures satisfaction in numbers; ordinal only ranks it.
The first answer is **cardinal** measurement. Cardinal means measured in numbers, such as 20 utils and 16 utils. It tells us how much more satisfaction one thing gives than another. The second answer is **ordinal** measurement. Ordinal means ranked as first, second and third. It says only that a bun is preferred to a biscuit, without saying by how much.

> The money measure is used first, because money is a number we can observe.
Most of this chapter uses the cardinal method, with a money measure. We take one util as worth Tk 1. A buyer who will pay at most Tk 20 for the first bun is saying that it gives 20 utils. Money is useful because it is a number that we can see. The method assumes that a taka means the same to the buyer at each step. This is reasonable when a purchase is small compared with the buyer's whole income.

> The method has limits, which is why ranking returns in Chapter VI.
The cardinal method has limits. Satisfaction differs from person to person, so one person's utils cannot be added to another's. And people can rank things more easily than they can score them. For these reasons Chapter VI introduces the ordinal method, which needs only ranking. The two methods lead to the same main results.

## The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility

> The customer's fifth bun is worth only 4 utils against a price of Tk 10.
Return to the customer. The fifth bun gave 4 utils, which is worth Tk 4. The price of the bun is Tk 10. A customer who weighs this will not buy the fifth bun. The first bun gave 20 utils, worth Tk 20, and was a bargain at Tk 10. What happened in between? The answer is one of the oldest laws of economics.

@ Law of diminishing marginal utility | Each extra unit adds less satisfaction than the one before.
> The law states that each extra unit adds less satisfaction.
The **law of diminishing marginal utility** states that, as a person consumes more units of a good one after another, the marginal utility of each extra unit becomes smaller, other things unchanged. Put in plain words, each extra unit adds less satisfaction than the one before. The bun schedule shows the law: 20, 16, 12, 8, 4.

:fig Marginal utility of each bun for the customer in the case file | fig_mu.svg

> The diagram shows the law as a staircase that goes down.
Read Figure 1 as follows. The bottom line shows the number of the bun. The side line shows the marginal utility in utils. Each bar is one bun, and its height is the satisfaction that bun adds. The bars become shorter from left to right. This staircase going down is the law in a picture.

> A second example shows how to find marginal utility from totals.
*Example.* *Given.* A thirsty cyclist drinks glasses of sugarcane juice. The total utility after 1 to 7 glasses is 14, 26, 35, 41, 44, 44 and 41 utils. *Find.* The marginal utility of each glass. *Formula.* MU(n) = TU(n) − TU(n − 1), with TU(0) = 0.

> The steps show marginal utility reaching zero and then turning negative.
*Steps.* Glass 1: 14 − 0 = 14. Glass 2: 26 − 14 = 12. Glass 3: 35 − 26 = 9. Glass 4: 41 − 35 = 6. Glass 5: 44 − 41 = 3. Glass 6: 44 − 44 = 0. Glass 7: 41 − 44 = −3. *Answer.* The marginal utilities are 14, 12, 9, 6, 3, 0 and −3. *Check.* Adding them again, 14 + 12 + 9 + 6 + 3 + 0 − 3 = 41, the last total.

> Total utility is greatest where marginal utility is zero.
The example shows two more points. When marginal utility is zero, as at the sixth glass, total utility stops rising and has reached its highest value. Beyond that point marginal utility is negative, and the extra glass brings discomfort. A buyer who thinks carefully never goes past the point of zero marginal utility, and usually stops sooner, because goods have a price.

> The law has three reasons behind it.
Why does marginal utility fall? There are three reasons. First, each want has a limit. A person who is hungry has a limited need for food, and each unit meets part of that need. Second, the person uses the first unit for the most important purpose. The second unit goes to a less important use. Water, for example, serves first to drink, then to wash, and only later to water plants. Third, units of the same good are partial substitutes for one another, so the more one has, the less any single unit matters.

> The law holds only under stated conditions.
Like every economic law, this one comes with conditions. The units must be of the same size and quality. They must be consumed one after another within a short time, not spread over years. The buyer's tastes must not change. The quantities of other goods the buyer has must not change. And the money measure assumes that a taka means the same at each step. When these conditions hold, the law holds. When they are broken, it seems to fail, as the next section shows.

> Recap: marginal utility falls as more units are consumed, under stated conditions.
*Before moving on.* The law of diminishing marginal utility says that each extra unit adds less satisfaction than the one before. It rests on limited wants and on using the best units first. It holds when units, tastes and other goods stay the same. *You should now be able to say:* each extra unit adds less than the one before.

> A short question tests the shape of the law.
*Pause and check.* A buyer's marginal utility from tea is 10, 8, 6, 4 and 2 utils for cups 1 to 5. What is the total utility from 3 cups?

## Limits of the Law

> Some cases seem to break the law, though each has an explanation.
Does the law hold for everything? Several cases look like exceptions. In each, a closer look shows that one of the conditions of the law has been broken. This is the pattern for every exception in economics: ask which condition has changed.

> A collector's pleasure may rise with each new item.
The first case is the hobby or the collection. A stamp collector may feel more pleasure with each new stamp, because the collection is nearer to completion. The explanation is that the collector is not buying separate units of one good. The unit of the good is the whole collection, and the last stamp completes it. For the collector, the whole set is one unit.

> The wish for money may seem to have no limit.
The second case is money. A person who loves money seems always to want more. But money is the means of buying many goods, and the marginal utility of each extra taka falls more slowly than for a single good, since there are always more uses. For a person with a very large amount, each extra taka does add little.

> Very small units give too little to feel.
The third case is the very small unit. A thirsty person given a single drop of water does not feel more satisfaction from a second drop. The units are too small to matter. The explanation is that the useful unit is a glass, not a drop, and the law applies to the useful unit.

> A habit can change tastes while the units are consumed.
The fourth case is the habit. A person who drinks a harmful drink may want more with each glass, because the habit has changed the person's tastes. The law assumes that tastes stay the same. Here they have changed, so the condition is broken.

> Recap: each exception breaks one condition of the law.
*Before moving on.* The apparent exceptions are collections, money, very small units and habits. Each breaks one condition: the unit, the measure, the size of the unit, or unchanged tastes. The law remains true under its conditions.

## Consumption: How a Buyer Spends

> A customer with Tk 8 must split it between tea and biscuits.
Think of a customer at a tea stall with Tk 8. A cup of tea costs Tk 2. A biscuit costs Tk 1. How should the customer share the Tk 8 between them? Eight biscuits would be too many. Four cups of tea would also be too many. The best choice lies somewhere between, and we now find a rule to locate it.

@ Consumption | Using a good to meet a want.
@ Consumer | A person who uses a good.
> Consumption is using a good to meet a want; a consumer does it.
**Consumption** means using a good to meet a want. A **consumer** is a person who uses a good. Eating a samosa, riding a bus and attending a lesson are all consumption. A consumer has wants, a limited budget, and goods with prices. The consumer's task is to turn the budget into the greatest satisfaction.

> A buyer should compare the satisfaction from each taka, not from each unit.
Comparing units can mislead, because goods have different prices. A cup of tea may give more utility than a biscuit, but it also costs more. The fair comparison is the marginal utility per taka spent: the marginal utility of the unit divided by its price. The buyer should spend the next taka where it brings the most satisfaction.

@ Equilibrium | A state of balance with no reason to change.
@ Equi-marginal rule | Spend so that marginal utility per taka is equal across goods.
> The buyer is in balance when each good gives equal satisfaction per taka.
Suppose the buyer spends the next taka on the good with the highest marginal utility per taka. As more of that good is bought, its marginal utility falls. At some point the other good becomes the better use. The buyer is then in **equilibrium**. Equilibrium means a state of balance with no reason to change. The rule that gives this balance is the **equi-marginal rule**: spend so that marginal utility per taka is equal across goods, until the budget is used.

> A worked example finds the best bundle for the tea-stall customer.
*Example.* *Given.* Budget Tk 8. Tea costs Tk 2 a cup, and its marginal utility for cups 1 to 4 is 12, 10, 8 and 6. A biscuit costs Tk 1, and its marginal utility for biscuits 1 to 4 is 5, 4, 3 and 2. *Find.* The bundle that gives the greatest total utility. *Formula.* Marginal utility per taka = marginal utility ÷ price.

> The steps list the utility per taka and buy in order, best first.
*Steps.* Tea per taka: 12 ÷ 2 = 6, 10 ÷ 2 = 5, 8 ÷ 2 = 4, 6 ÷ 2 = 3. Biscuit per taka: 5, 4, 3, 2. Buy in order of the highest figure, as long as the budget lasts: tea 1 (6) costs Tk 2, total Tk 2. Tea 2 (5) costs Tk 2, total Tk 4. Biscuit 1 (5) costs Tk 1, total Tk 5. Tea 3 (4) costs Tk 2, total Tk 7. Biscuit 2 (4) costs Tk 1, total Tk 8. The budget is spent.

> The answer is 3 cups and 2 biscuits, and the check compares other bundles.
*Answer.* The customer buys 3 cups of tea and 2 biscuits. The marginal utility per taka is 4 for the last cup and 4 for the last biscuit, which is equal. Total utility is 12 + 10 + 8 + 5 + 4 = 39 utils. *Check.* Try 2 cups and 4 biscuits, which also cost Tk 8: 22 + 14 = 36 utils. Try 4 cups and no biscuits: 36 utils. Both are lower than 39.

> The rule has limits: a fixed budget, given prices and independent goods.
The rule has limits. The budget must be fixed and fully spent. Prices must be given, since one buyer cannot change them. The marginal utility of one good must not depend on how much of another is bought. And it must be possible to buy goods in small steps. Chapter IV builds the demand curve from this idea.

> Recap: a buyer is in equilibrium when each good gives equal utility per taka.
*Before moving on.* A consumer spends a budget on several goods. The equi-marginal rule says to equalise marginal utility per taka across goods. At that point the consumer is in equilibrium and has no reason to change.

## Summary

> Satisfaction falls with each extra unit, and buyers spend by equalising utility per taka.
Utility is the satisfaction from a good. Total utility is all of it, and marginal utility is the extra from one more unit. Marginal utility falls as more units are consumed, under stated conditions, and this is the law of diminishing marginal utility. Apparent exceptions break one of the conditions. A consumer with a budget is in equilibrium when marginal utility per taka is equal across goods.

## Think It Through

> A family declines a sixth kilogram of rice at the same price.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Total utility keeps rising while marginal utility falls.
Many students think that when marginal utility falls, total utility falls too. It does not. Total utility rises as long as marginal utility is positive. It falls only when marginal utility becomes negative.

> Utility does not mean usefulness or goodness.
Utility is the satisfaction a person feels. A good that harms a person can still give utility, since the person wants it. The word does not judge whether a want is wise.

> The law is about one good, one buyer, a short time and unchanged tastes.
Many students apply the law to the whole of a person's wealth, or over a long time. It is about successive units of one good, bought one after another, with tastes and other things unchanged.

## In History

> The puzzle of water and diamonds was solved by marginal thinking.
In 1776 Adam Smith pointed to a puzzle: water is very useful but costs little, while diamonds are of little use but cost much. For nearly a century the puzzle stood. In the early 1870s, William Stanley Jevons in England and Carl Menger in Austria, and soon after Léon Walras in Switzerland, each showed that the value of a good depends on its marginal utility, not on its total usefulness. Water is plentiful, so the marginal utility of one more glass is small. Diamonds are scarce, so the marginal utility of one more diamond is large. Hermann Gossen, a German, had stated the law earlier, in 1854, but his book was little noticed.

## Sources

> The law has a short and well-documented history.
Hermann Gossen, *Entwickelung der Gesetze des menschlichen Verkehrs* (1854), stated the law first. William Stanley Jevons, *The Theory of Political Economy* (1871), and Carl Menger, *Grundsätze der Volkswirtschaftslehre* (1871), made it the base of the subject. Léon Walras, *Éléments d'économie politique pure* (1874), set it in a wider system. Alfred Marshall, *Principles of Economics* (1890), gave it the form used in this book.
