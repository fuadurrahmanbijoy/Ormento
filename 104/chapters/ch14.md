:chapter XIV | Monopolistic Competition and Oligopoly
:mood Many sellers, each a little different; or a few, each watching the others.
:desc This chapter studies the two markets that lie between perfect competition and monopoly. In one, many firms sell goods that differ a little. In the other, a few firms watch each other closely. It shows how price and output are set in each, and then compares all four kinds of market.

> Two puzzles: why small differences matter, and why a few big sellers rarely cut prices.

Walk down a busy lane in any Bangladeshi town and count the bakeries. There may be a dozen. No two sell exactly the same bun. One bakes early and sells warm. One uses more butter. One stands beside the bus stop. Their prices are close, yet not equal. A baker who charges Tk 1 more than the neighbour does not lose every customer. Why not? And yet no baker on that lane grows rich for long. Why not?

> The second puzzle concerns markets with a few big sellers.

Now move to a different market: mobile phone service. A few large firms supply nearly everyone. When one firm announces a cheaper plan, the others often reply within days. When one firm raises its price, rivals often leave theirs unchanged. Yet prices in such markets do not fall every week. What holds them?

> The two puzzles share one answer: the seller is neither alone nor lost in a crowd.

Both puzzles have one root. In each market the seller is neither alone, as the ferry owner was in Chapter XIII, nor lost in a crowd of identical sellers, as the baker was in Chapter XII. This chapter studies the two middle cases.

> You need the profit rule, the cost curves and the shapes of Chapters XI to XIII.

You will need the profit rule of Chapter XI, the cost curves of Chapter X, and the equilibrium ideas of Chapters XII and XIII. We use them again here, with one change: now the seller's demand curve slopes down, but rivals are close.

## The Idea

> Monopolistic competition mixes a little monopoly with plenty of competition.

Take the lane of bakeries again. Each baker has a small monopoly over a bun that buyers think is a little different. But each baker also has many rivals whose buns are close substitutes. Monopolistic competition is the market that results.^1 It has three features. First, there are many sellers. Second, the sellers' goods are differentiated: they are alike, but not the same. Third, entry is free, so new sellers can join when profits tempt them.

^1: Economists call any market in which sellers face downward-sloping demand *imperfect competition*. That includes monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly.

> The name shows the mix: monopoly because of the difference, competition because of the rivals.

The name explains the mix. The word *monopoly* points to each firm's power over its own variety: it is the sole seller of that variety. The word *competition* points to the many close rivals and the free entry. Two books of 1933 set out the idea: Edward Chamberlin, *The Theory of Monopolistic Competition*, and Joan Robinson, *The Economics of Imperfect Competition*. Before them, textbooks treated markets as either perfectly competitive or monopolistic.

> Many everyday markets fit this description.

Many everyday markets in Bangladesh fit. Tailors, restaurants, hair salons, private tutors and pharmacies each have many sellers who differ in place, service or quality. Compare the market with the earlier two. A perfectly competitive seller faces a flat demand line, since all goods are alike. A monopolist faces the whole market demand. A monopolistic competitor sits in between: its demand slopes down, but because rivals are close, buyers react strongly to its price. That demand is elastic.

*Before moving on.* Monopolistic competition has many sellers, goods that differ a little, and free entry. Each firm has a downward demand curve, which is elastic because rivals are close.

## Product Differentiation and Selling Costs

> Differences make buyers loyal; loyalty gives the seller some power.

Why does a customer pass a cheaper bakery and walk on to another? Because the goods are not quite alike in the buyer's eyes.

> Product differentiation makes buyers see one seller's good as different.

@ Product differentiation | Making a good different from rivals' goods, in fact or in the buyer's mind.

**Product differentiation** means making a good different from rivals' goods, in fact or in the buyer's mind. The difference may be real: more butter, a better fit, a faster repair. It may be one of place: a shop close to home. It may be one of service: free delivery, longer hours. It may even be a matter of belief, when the goods are alike but the buyer thinks otherwise. Whatever its source, the difference makes some buyers loyal, and loyalty gives the seller some power over price.

> A brand names a seller's good and fixes the difference in the buyer's mind.

@ Brand | A name or sign that identifies one seller's good and sets it apart from others.

Sellers use a brand to fix the difference in the buyer's mind. A **brand** is a name or sign that identifies one seller's good. The milk bun, sold in a printed paper bag with the bakery's name, is a brand. A brand helps a buyer remember the good and find it again. It also lets the seller build a reputation that is worth money.

> Selling costs are paid to win buyers, not to make goods.

@ Selling cost | Money spent to promote a seller's goods, such as advertising.

Sellers also spend money to tell buyers about the difference. **Selling cost** means money spent to promote sales, such as signboards, flyers, advertisements and free samples. It differs from the cost of production, which is the cost of making the good. A Tk 2,000 signboard is a selling cost. The flour in the bun is a production cost. Selling costs push the demand curve to the right, since more buyers learn about the good. They also add to the seller's average cost.

> Advertising informs and persuades; both effects can occur at once.

Advertising does two jobs. It informs: a buyer learns that the warm bun is on sale at 7 a.m. It persuades: a buyer comes to prefer one bun to another because of what an advertisement says. Economists disagree about which job matters more. The information is useful. The persuasion can be wasteful if every seller advertises and no one gains in the end.

@ Non-price competition | Rivalry among sellers through quality, brand, service or advertising, not through price.

> Non-price competition is rivalry through quality, service and brand.

Sellers in these markets often compete in ways other than price. **Non-price competition** is rivalry through quality, brand, service or advertising, not through price. A baker who adds a new filling is competing in this way. Such competition gives buyers more variety, but it costs money, and that cost is paid by buyers through prices.

> Before moving on: differences, brands and selling costs.

*Before moving on.* Product differentiation creates loyalty. A brand fixes the difference in the mind. Selling cost is money spent to promote sales. Non-price competition is rivalry by quality or service.

## The Theory

> The firm's demand slopes down but is elastic; the profit rule gives its output.

Now we set out how the monopolistic competitor chooses. Its demand curve slopes down, as a monopolist's does, so its marginal revenue lies below price (Chapter XIII). It follows the same profit rule: it raises output until marginal revenue equals marginal cost. The new element is entry, which acts in the long run.

> Worked example one: the short run.

*Worked example.* *Given:* milk bun demand P = 20 − Q/10 and TC = 60 + 8Q + Q²/20, so MC = 8 + Q/10. *Find:* output, price and profit. *Formula:* MR = MC; profit = total revenue − total cost. The steps follow.

:table Short-run equilibrium of the milk bun line (case file, illustrative)
| Step | Working |
| 1. Marginal revenue: same intercept, double slope | MR = 20 − Q/5 |
| 2. Set MR equal to MC | 20 − Q/5 = 8 + Q/10 |
| 3. Collect terms | 12 = Q/5 + Q/10 = 3Q/10 |
| 4. Solve for Q | Q = 12 × 10/3 = 40 buns |
| 5. Price from the demand line | P = 20 − 40/10 = Tk 16 |
| 6. Revenue and cost | TR = 16 × 40 = 640; TC = 60 + 320 + 80 = 460 |
| 7. Profit | 640 − 460 = Tk 180 a day |

> The answer and its check: the profit equals the price-minus-average-cost gap times output.

*Answer:* the bakery sells 40 milk buns a day at Tk 16 and earns Tk 180 a day above all costs. *Check:* average cost at 40 is 460/40 = Tk 11.50, and (16 − 11.50) × 40 = 180. This is supernormal profit, a profit above normal (Chapter XII).

> Profit attracts entry: rivals copy the milk bun, and each firm's demand shifts left.

A profit of Tk 180 does not go unnoticed. Entry is free, so rivals bake their own milk buns. Each new variety takes buyers from the others. For our bakery, demand falls: at every price, fewer buns sell. The demand curve shifts left. It also becomes more elastic, because there are now more close substitutes. Entry continues as long as a profit remains. It stops when the demand curve just touches the average cost curve, so that price equals average cost and profit is zero.

> Worked example two: after entry, demand is P = 14 − Q/10 and the firm just breaks even.

*Worked example.* *Given:* after entry, demand is P = 14 − Q/10; cost is unchanged. *Find:* output, price and profit. *Formula:* MR = MC again.

:table Long-run equilibrium of the milk bun line (case file, illustrative)
| Step | Working |
| 1. New marginal revenue | MR = 14 − Q/5 |
| 2. Set MR equal to MC | 14 − Q/5 = 8 + Q/10 |
| 3. Collect terms | 6 = 3Q/10, so Q = 20 buns |
| 4. Price | P = 14 − 20/10 = Tk 12 |
| 5. Average cost at 20 buns | AC = 60/20 + 8 + 20/20 = 3 + 8 + 1 = Tk 12 |
| 6. Compare | P = AC = 12, so profit = 0 |

> The check: the AC curve has the same slope as demand at 20 buns, so the curves just touch.

*Answer:* 20 buns at Tk 12, with no profit above normal. *Check:* total revenue 12 × 20 = 240 and total cost 60 + 160 + 20 = 240. The slope of the average cost curve at 20 buns is −60/400 + 1/20 = −0.10, the same as the slope of demand, so the two curves just touch.

:fig Long-run equilibrium of the milk bun line. Demand touches average cost at T; output is 20, below the output of lowest average cost. | fig_mcomp.svg

> The figure shows tangency: price equals average cost, so entry stops.

In the figure, the demand line touches the average cost curve at T, directly above the point E where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. A firm that earns zero supernormal profit still earns *normal profit*, since all costs, including the owner's time and money, have been counted (Chapter X). It does not close. If profits had turned into losses, firms would leave, and demand for those who remain would shift right again.

> Excess capacity: the firm makes less than the output of lowest average cost.

@ Excess capacity | Output that lies below the level at which average cost is lowest.

There is a second result. The lowest average cost is at 34.6 buns, where average cost is about Tk 11.46. The firm makes only 20. The gap, about 15 buns, is **excess capacity**: output below the level of lowest average cost. The firm is not idle. Its equipment could produce more at lower cost per bun, but the firm cannot sell more at a price that covers cost, because its demand curve slopes down.

> Compared with perfect competition, price is higher than marginal cost but buyers gain variety.

Compare this with perfect competition (Chapter XII). A competitive firm ends at the lowest point of average cost, and price equals marginal cost. Here price (Tk 12) is above marginal cost (Tk 10), and output is below the lowest-cost level. Some economists call this waste. Others reply that buyers value the variety: they could have a single standard bun made at lower cost, but they prefer a choice. The truth depends on how much buyers value the variety.

> Before moving on: marginal revenue meets marginal cost; entry removes profit; excess capacity remains.

*Before moving on.* The firm sets MR equal to MC. Profit draws in new firms until demand just touches average cost. Then profit above normal is zero and capacity is unused.

## Oligopoly

> Oligopoly has few sellers and high barriers, so each firm's choice affects the others.

@ Interdependence | Each firm's best choice depends on the choices of its rivals.

Now the second puzzle. Oligopoly is a market with a few sellers, and its key feature is **interdependence**: each firm's best choice depends on what rivals do. Barriers to entry keep new firms out, so the few stay few. Mobile phone service and air travel on a busy route are examples. A firm that cuts its price cannot treat the market as given, because rivals may cut too, and the gain disappears.

> Interdependence means no single rule fixes the price.

Because of interdependence, economists have several models of oligopoly, not one. We study the four main ideas: price war, kinked demand, collusion and price leadership.

@ Price war | A series of price cuts, each rival cutting below the last.

> A price war leaves every firm worse off.

A **price war** is a series of price cuts in which each rival cuts below the last. Everyone's profit falls. Firms fear this, and so they often avoid price cuts and compete in other ways, such as brand, service and advertising.

@ Kinked demand curve | A demand curve with a bend at the present price.

> Kinked demand: rivals follow a cut but not a rise.

The **kinked demand curve** is a simple model of this fear. A firm believes that if it raises its price, rivals will not follow, so it loses many buyers: demand is elastic above the present price. If it cuts its price, rivals will follow, so it gains little: demand is inelastic below. The demand curve has a bend at the present price.

> Case file: a kinked-demand variant of the milk bun.

:case Case file, illustrative. A variant of the milk bun. Suppose the four bakeries are the only sellers of branded buns and each sells at Tk 16, with this bakery selling 40 a day. Above Tk 16, demand is P = 20 − Q/10, because rivals hold their prices. Below Tk 16, demand is P = 24 − Q/5, because rivals match. Marginal cost at 40 buns is taken as Tk 10; only the demand lines are borrowed from the milk bun above.

> The kink gives a break in marginal revenue, so costs can change without a change in price.

The bend creates a break in the marginal revenue line. Above the kink, marginal revenue is 20 − Q/5, which is Tk 12 at 40 buns. Below the kink, it is 24 − 2Q/5, which is Tk 8 at 40 buns. So between Tk 8 and Tk 12 there is a vertical gap. Marginal cost at 40 buns is Tk 10, inside the gap. So the firm has no reason to change its output or price. If marginal cost rose to Tk 11 or fell to Tk 9, nothing would change.

:fig The kinked demand curve. Marginal revenue breaks at 40 buns; any marginal cost cutting the gap leaves price and output unchanged. | fig_kink.svg

> The model explains why prices stay stuck but not how the price was set.

The model explains why prices in oligopoly are often sticky. It does not explain how the price of Tk 16 came about in the first place. It also fits poorly when all firms face the same cost rise and all raise prices together. So it is one idea, not a full theory.^2

^2: The kinked demand idea was published by Paul Sweezy in 1939, and independently by Hall and Hitch in the same year.

@ Collusion | An agreement among rivals to fix a price or limit output.

@ Cartel | A group of firms that agree together on prices or output.

> Collusion turns rivals into a single seller, but each member is tempted to cheat.

Rivals may avoid risk by agreeing. **Collusion** means an agreement among rivals to fix a price or limit output. A group of firms that do this openly is a **cartel**. If the firms agree, they behave like one monopolist and share its profit. But each member is tempted to cheat. By selling slightly more at the agreed price, one firm gains, while the others lose. If all cheat, the cartel breaks down. Many countries forbid cartels by law (Chapter XIII).

@ Price leadership | One firm in the market sets the price and the others follow.

> Price leadership is a quiet way to agree without meeting.

A quieter route is **price leadership**: one firm, often the largest, announces a price and the others follow it. No agreement is signed. The rivals simply learn that following is safer than leading. Price leadership can give much the same result as a cartel.

> Before moving on: interdependence, price war, kinked demand, collusion, price leadership.

*Before moving on.* Oligopoly has few sellers and interdependence. Four ideas describe the outcome: price war, kinked demand, collusion and price leadership. No single rule fixes the price.

## Price Determination Compared

> We now compare how price is set in the four kinds of market.

We now have all four markets. Their differences can be shown in a table. Read each column as one kind of market and each row as one feature. The last row gives an example from the case file or from daily life.

:table The four markets compared
| Feature | Perfect competition | Monopoly | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly |
| Number of sellers | Very many | One | Many | A few |
| Product | Identical | One, no close substitute | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated |
| Entry | Free | Blocked | Free | Hard |
| Control over price | None | Large | Some | Some, shared with rivals |
| Price against marginal cost | Equal | Above | Above | Above |
| Long-run profit above normal | None | Can persist | None | Can persist |
| Example | Loaves at Tk 60 (Chapter XII) | One-seller bun market | The milk bun | Mobile phone service |

> Each market has its own way of setting the price.

The way price is set also differs. In perfect competition, market demand and supply set the price, and each firm takes it. In monopoly, the seller sets output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost and reads the price from demand. In monopolistic competition, the firm uses the same rule, but entry then brings price down to average cost. In oligopoly, the price depends on the replies of rivals, so no single rule exists.

> The bakery's own markets show all four ideas on one street.

:case Case file, illustrative. Place the bakery's products on this scale. Loaves sell at the market price of Tk 60 (perfect competition). If one seller served the bun market, the price would be Tk 14 against Tk 12 under competition (monopoly). The milk bun settles at Tk 12 with no profit above normal (monopolistic competition). In the kinked variant, price holds at Tk 16 (oligopoly).

> Before moving on: the four markets differ in power over price and in long-run profit.

*Before moving on.* As we move from perfect competition to monopoly, control over price rises. Price lies above marginal cost in every market except perfect competition. Entry decides whether profit lasts.

## Summary

> A short summary of the chapter.

Monopolistic competition has many sellers of differentiated goods and free entry. Each firm's demand slopes down and is elastic. In the short run it earns a profit; entry removes it, leaving price equal to average cost and some excess capacity. Oligopoly has a few sellers and interdependence, so firms may fight price wars, face a kinked demand, collude, or follow a leader. The four markets differ in power over price and in how long profit lasts.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common mistakes about these markets.

*"Differentiated means monopoly."* The firm has power over its own variety only; rivals are close. *"Zero profit means the firm is failing."* It earns normal profit, which covers the owner's own time and money. *"Excess capacity means idle workers."* It means output below the level of lowest cost. *"The kinked curve shows how price is chosen."* It shows only why price tends to stay put.

## In History

> OPEC, a cartel of oil exporting countries, began on 14 September 1960.

On 14 September 1960, representatives of five oil exporting countries met in Baghdad and founded the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries. Its aim was to coordinate the oil policies of its members. In later years it tried to hold prices up by agreeing on output quotas. Members have often produced more than their quotas, and this shows the weakness of a cartel: each member gains by cheating. The case shows both the power of a group of sellers and the difficulty of keeping them together.

## Sources

> Where the ideas of this chapter began.

Chamberlin (1933) and Robinson (1933) wrote the first full theories of markets between competition and monopoly. They are the basis of this chapter. Sweezy (1939) and Hall and Hitch (1939) set out the kinked demand curve. These sources are listed with full details at the back of the book.
