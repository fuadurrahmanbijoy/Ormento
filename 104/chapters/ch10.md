:chapter X | Cost
:mood What does a loaf really cost?
:desc This chapter counts every cost of production, including those that no one is paid in cash. It builds the cost curves of a firm from its product table, shows why average cost is shaped like a U, and asks what happens to cost when the firm grows.

> A puzzle: a shop can show a profit and still be a poor bargain.

At the end of its first full month, the bakery's books show that it paid rent, flour, wages and the electricity bill. They show no wage for the owner, who works every day, and no interest on the savings that were used to open the shop. A friend studies the books and asks a hard question. "You left a job that paid Tk 40,000 a month, and your savings would have earned Tk 3,000 a month in a bank. Do your books count these?" They do not. So is the shop's profit a true profit? To answer, we must decide what counts as a cost. Then we must see how cost changes as the bakery bakes more loaves.

> What you should know before reading on.

*You will need:* opportunity cost from Chapter I, and the bakery's product table from Chapter IX (total, average and marginal product, and the law of diminishing returns).

## Cost and Opportunity Cost

> *Word in use.* Cost. In daily life, the price you pay for a thing. In economics, the value of what you give up to get it.
> Cost means the value of what is given up.
@ Cost | The value of what is given up to make or get something.
Chapter I taught that every choice gives something up. We can now put a money value on the loss. A **cost** is the value of what is given up to make or get something. For the bakery owner, the best alternative to opening the shop was to keep the job and deposit the savings: Tk 40,000 + Tk 3,000 = Tk 43,000 a month. That sum is the opportunity cost of using her time and savings in the shop. To measure any opportunity cost, find the best alternative and write down its money value.

> Some costs are paid in money; others are not.

@ Explicit cost | A cost paid in money to someone outside the firm.
@ Implicit cost | The cost of using resources the owner already has; no money is paid.
Costs come in two kinds. An **explicit cost** is a cost paid in money to someone outside the firm. The bakery's rent of Tk 25,000 a month is explicit, as are flour and wages. An **implicit cost** is the cost of using resources the owner already has, where no money is paid. The owner's own time, valued at Tk 40,000, and her own savings, valued at Tk 3,000, are implicit costs. A family that farms its own land has an implicit cost too: the rent the land could have earned if it had been let to others.

> Accountants count explicit costs; economists count all costs.

@ Accounting cost | The explicit costs recorded in the firm's books.
@ Economic cost | All costs of the firm, explicit and implicit together.
The two kinds of cost give two totals. The **accounting cost** is the explicit costs recorded in the firm's books. The **economic cost** is all costs, explicit and implicit together. In words: economic cost equals explicit cost plus implicit cost. For the bakery, the implicit cost is Tk 43,000 a month, which is about Tk 1,433 a day in a month of 30 days. The books leave this out, so accounting cost is smaller than economic cost.

> A cost already paid and lost cannot guide a new choice.

@ Sunk cost | A cost already paid that cannot be recovered.
A **sunk cost** is a cost already paid that cannot be recovered. Suppose a tailor pays Tk 5,000 for a licence that cannot be refunded or sold. The money is gone whatever she does next. When she decides whether to open the shop this month, the Tk 5,000 should not weigh on the choice, because no choice can bring it back. Only costs that still depend on the decision belong in it.

> A normal profit is the owner's own opportunity cost.

@ Normal profit | The least profit that keeps an entrepreneur in the business.
The owner of a firm gives up something too, so her reward is also a cost. **Normal profit** is the least profit that keeps an entrepreneur in the business. It equals what her time and money could earn in the best other use: for the bakery, Tk 43,000 a month. Economists therefore count normal profit as a cost. A firm that earns only normal profit is doing as well as the owner's best alternative. A firm that earns less will, in time, lose its owner. Chapter XVI returns to this when it compares economic and accounting profit.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Cost is the value of what is given up. Explicit costs are paid in money; implicit costs are not. Economic cost counts both; accounting cost counts only the explicit kind. Normal profit is a cost, because it is the owner's opportunity cost. A sunk cost cannot be recovered and should not guide a new choice.

## Kinds of Cost

> Some costs stay the same whatever the output; others change.

Costs also differ in how they behave when output changes. This difference is the base of everything that follows in this chapter. We use the short run of Chapter IX, in which one input (the oven and the shop) is fixed.

> Fixed, variable and total cost.

@ Fixed cost | Cost that does not change with output.
@ Variable cost | Cost that changes with output.
@ Total cost | Fixed cost plus variable cost.
A **fixed cost** is a cost that does not change with output. The shop rent is paid even on a day when no loaf is baked. A **variable cost** is a cost that changes with output. Flour, fuel and bakers' wages all rise when more bread is made. The **total cost** is fixed cost plus variable cost. In symbols, TC = FC + VC.^1

> An older name for a similar idea.

@ Supplementary cost | An older name for overhead cost, which does not change with output.
Older books use the name **supplementary cost**. It means overhead cost, the cost that does not change with output. In this book we say fixed cost for the same idea. When you meet "supplementary cost" in another book, read it as fixed cost.

> Cost can be seen as money or as sacrifice.

@ Money cost | Cost measured in money, such as taka paid.
@ Real cost | The effort and sacrifice of the people who supply the inputs.
Cost can be seen in two ways. **Money cost** is cost measured in money. **Real cost** is the effort and sacrifice of the people who supply the inputs: the baker's tiring work and the saver's waiting. Money cost is what we can count in a table, so the rest of this chapter uses it. Real cost explains why people want a wage at all.

> The bakery's cost table follows from the product table.

:case Case file, illustrative. Step 10. Fixed cost is Tk 1,000 a day, which is Tk 30,000 a month (rent Tk 25,000 and other costs Tk 5,000) divided by 30 days. A baker costs Tk 600 a day, and materials cost Tk 20 for each loaf. The table counts explicit costs only. The owner's implicit cost of Tk 43,000 a month is not in it.

> A worked example: setting out the problem.

*Given.* The product table of Chapter IX: 1 to 6 bakers make 20, 50, 90, 120, 140 and 150 loaves. FC = Tk 1,000 a day; baker Tk 600 a day; materials Tk 20 a loaf. *Find.* Variable cost and total cost at each output. *Formula.* VC = bakers × Tk 600 + loaves × Tk 20; TC = FC + VC.

> A worked example: the arithmetic and the answer.

*Steps.* Take 90 loaves, made by three bakers. VC = 3 × 600 + 90 × 20 = 1,800 + 1,800 = Tk 3,600. TC = 1,000 + 3,600 = Tk 4,600. Repeat for each row. *Answer.* The table shows the result. *Check.* TC minus FC must equal VC in every row. For 150 loaves, 7,600 − 1,000 = 6,600, which is 6 × 600 + 150 × 20 = 3,600 + 3,000.

| 50 | 2 | 1,000 | 2,200 | 3,200 |
| 90 | 3 | 1,000 | 3,600 | 4,600 |

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Fixed cost does not change with output; variable cost does; total cost is their sum. Money cost is counted in taka. Real cost is the sacrifice behind it. The cost table follows from the product table.

## Average and Marginal Cost

@! Go slowly here.
> Start with pens: an average and a new unit.

Buy four pens for Tk 40. The cost of each pen on average is Tk 10. Now buy a fifth pen for Tk 20. The total is Tk 60, and the average becomes Tk 12. The new pen cost more than the old average, so it pulled the average up. Keep this small case in mind: an extra unit that costs more than the average raises the average, and an extra unit that costs less lowers it. A firm's cost curves follow the same rule.

> Four measures of cost per loaf.

@ Average fixed cost | Fixed cost divided by output.
@ Average variable cost | Variable cost divided by output.
@ Average cost | Total cost divided by output; the cost of one unit on average.
@ Marginal cost | The extra cost of making one more unit.
The **average fixed cost** is fixed cost divided by output. The **average variable cost** is variable cost divided by output. The **average cost** is total cost divided by output. The **marginal cost** is the extra cost of making one more unit. In symbols: AFC = FC ÷ Q; AVC = VC ÷ Q; AC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC; MC = change in TC ÷ change in Q. Here Q is output.

> A worked example: setting out the problem.

*Given.* The cost table of section 10.2. *Find.* AFC, AVC, AC and MC at each output. *Formula.* As above.

> A worked example: the arithmetic.

*Steps.* Take 90 loaves. AFC = 1,000 ÷ 90 = Tk 11.11. AVC = 3,600 ÷ 90 = Tk 40.00. AC = 4,600 ÷ 90 = Tk 51.11. MC is measured from the row before, where TC was Tk 3,200 at 50 loaves: MC = (4,600 − 3,200) ÷ (90 − 50) = 1,400 ÷ 40 = Tk 35. The first row has no row before it, so we measure from zero output, where TC equals the fixed cost of Tk 1,000: MC = (2,000 − 1,000) ÷ (20 − 0) = Tk 50.

:table Cost per loaf at the bakery (taka)
| Loaves | AFC | AVC | AC | MC |
| 20 | 50.0 | 50.0 | 100.0 | 50 |
| 50 | 20.0 | 44.0 | 64.0 | 40 |
| 90 | 11.1 | 40.0 | 51.1 | 35 |
| 120 | 8.3 | 40.0 | 48.3 | 40 |
| 140 | 7.1 | 41.4 | 48.6 | 50 |
| 150 | 6.7 | 44.0 | 50.7 | 80 |

> The answer, and two checks on it.

*Answer.* Average cost falls from Tk 100 to Tk 48.3 at 120 loaves, then rises. Marginal cost falls to Tk 35 at 90 loaves, then rises sharply. *Check.* In every row AC equals AFC plus AVC, apart from a difference of Tk 0.1 caused by rounding. The gap between AC and AVC is AFC, and it shrinks as output grows.

> Marginal cost is the mirror image of marginal product.

Why does marginal cost first fall and then rise? The product table answers. Each step in the table adds one baker, who costs Tk 600, and the baker's marginal product tells how many loaves that Tk 600 buys. So MC = (Tk 600 ÷ MP) + Tk 20 for materials. For the third baker, MP is 40 and MC = 15 + 20 = Tk 35. For the sixth baker, MP is 10 and MC = 60 + 20 = Tk 80. When marginal product rises, marginal cost falls. When marginal product falls, marginal cost rises. The law of diminishing returns is therefore the cause of rising marginal cost.

> The rule linking average and marginal cost.

The pen case gives the rule. When marginal cost is below average cost, average cost falls. When marginal cost is above average cost, average cost rises. So the marginal cost curve cuts the average cost curve at the lowest point of the average cost curve. The table agrees. At 120 loaves, MC is Tk 40, below AC of Tk 48.3, so AC is still falling. At 140 loaves, MC is Tk 50, above AC of Tk 48.6, so AC has begun to rise. The true lowest point lies between 120 and 140 loaves. Among the rows of the table, the lowest average cost is at 120 loaves. The same rule links marginal cost to average variable cost.

:fig Cost curves drawn through the points of the bakery's cost table. Marginal cost cuts average cost near its lowest point. | fig_cost.svg

> Why average cost has the shape of a U.

Average cost is U-shaped because two forces act on it. First, the fixed cost is spread over more loaves, so AFC falls all the time. This force dominates at low output. Second, diminishing returns raise AVC after some output. At small output, the first force wins and AC falls. At large output, the second wins and AC rises. Since AFC keeps falling after AVC has started to rise, the lowest point of AC lies at a larger output than the lowest point of AVC.

> How the totals and the averages connect.

We can also read the totals together. Fixed cost is a flat line. Variable cost and total cost have the same shape, and the gap between them is always the fixed cost. Marginal cost is the rate at which total cost rises. Average cost is total cost shared among the loaves. If you know any one of these curves, you can find the others by the formulas above.

> Four slips to avoid.

*Common slips.* Do not divide by the wrong output: AC divides by the output of its own row. Do not measure marginal cost from the same row; use the row before. Do not forget that total cost is not zero when output is zero: it equals the fixed cost. Do not say that MC and AC are lowest at the same output; MC is lowest earlier.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* AFC falls all the time. AVC, AC and MC fall and then rise. MC rises first because marginal product falls. MC cuts AC and AVC at their lowest points.

> The sentence to test yourself against.

*You should now be able to say:* when marginal cost is below average cost, average cost falls; when it is above, average cost rises; so the two curves meet where average cost is lowest.

## Short-Run and Long-Run Cost Curves

> In the long run the firm may choose its plant.

The curves above belong to the short run, with one oven. In the long run, the owner can choose any size of plant: one oven, two or five. Each plant size has its own short-run average cost curve. A small plant has low cost at small output but high cost at large output, because its fixed oven is crowded. A large plant costs more at small output, because its fixed costs are spread over few loaves, but less at large output.

> The long-run curve is the lowest cost over all plant sizes.

@ Long-run average cost | The lowest average cost of each output when every input can change.
The **long-run average cost** is the lowest average cost of each output when every input can change. For each output, the owner picks the plant that makes it most cheaply. The long-run curve therefore runs along the lower edge of all the short-run curves. For this reason it is called an envelope: it wraps around them from below. In the long run there are no fixed costs, since every input can be changed.

:fig Short-run average cost curves for three plant sizes, and the long-run average cost curve that wraps around them. | fig_lrac.svg

> Where the curves touch.

Each short-run curve touches the long-run curve at one point and nowhere lies below it. The firm can always choose that plant, so the long run can never cost more than any one plant. Only for the medium plant, at the bottom of the long-run curve, is the touching point also the lowest point of the short-run curve. For smaller plants the long-run curve is falling, so the touching point is on the falling side of the short-run curve. For larger plants it is on the rising side. The long-run curve is also flatter than the short-run curves, because in the long run the firm can adjust every input.

## Economies and Diseconomies of Scale

> What makes cost per loaf fall as the firm grows.

@ Economies of scale | Average cost falls as the scale of the firm grows.
When the long-run curve falls, the firm enjoys **economies of scale**, which means that average cost falls as the scale grows. There are several sources. Technical economies come from bigger machines and division of labour. Managerial economies come from hiring a specialist for each task. Marketing economies come from buying flour in bulk and spreading the cost of advertising over more loaves. Financial economies come from borrowing at lower rates, which banks offer to large firms. Risk-bearing economies come from selling several products, so a loss on one can be met from another.

> Some economies arise outside the firm.

Economies may also be *internal* or *external*. Internal economies arise inside one firm, as above. External economies arise outside the firm, when the whole industry grows. For example, when many bakeries gather in one town, trained bakers, flour merchants and repair shops settle nearby. All the bakeries then enjoy lower costs, though no single one has grown.

> Growth beyond a point raises cost again.

@ Diseconomies of scale | Average cost rises as the scale of the firm grows.
When the long-run curve rises, the firm suffers **diseconomies of scale**, which means that average cost rises as the scale grows. The chief cause is management strain: orders pass through many people, delays grow, and it is harder to see waste. Buying very large amounts of an input may also raise its price. The bottom of the long-run curve is the scale at which cost is lowest. Between falling and rising parts, there is often a flat stretch where average cost hardly changes.

> Costs and returns to scale are linked but not the same.

How does this link to returns to scale in Chapter IX? Returns to scale compare output with inputs, without prices. Economies of scale compare cost with output, in taka. If input prices do not change, the two move together. In the bakery's plan, output per scale unit was 50, 55, 60, 60 and 56. If each unit of scale costs the same, the cost per loaf falls from scale 1 to 3, is level from 3 to 4, and rises at 5. That agrees with increasing, constant and decreasing returns to scale. The two differ when input prices change, as when bulk buying lowers the price of flour.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Economies of scale make average cost fall as the firm grows; diseconomies make it rise. Sources of economies include technical, managerial, marketing, financial and risk-bearing gains. Returns to scale describe quantities; economies of scale describe cost.

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

Cost is the value of what is given up. Economic cost counts explicit and implicit costs; accounting cost counts only explicit ones; normal profit is a cost. In the short run, total cost is fixed plus variable cost. Average cost is U-shaped, and marginal cost cuts it at its lowest point. In the long run the firm chooses its plant, and the long-run average cost curve wraps around the short-run curves. It falls with economies of scale and rises with diseconomies.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common errors, each corrected.

*Error:* "Fixed cost is zero when output is zero." *Correction:* fixed cost must still be paid; only variable cost is zero. *Error:* "Money already spent should guide the choice." *Correction:* a sunk cost cannot be recovered, so it should not. *Error:* "Marginal cost and average cost are lowest at the same output." *Correction:* marginal cost reaches its lowest point first and cuts average cost at the average's lowest point. *Error:* "Unpaid resources have no cost." *Correction:* the owner's time and savings have an opportunity cost, which economists count.

## In History

> A fall in cost made a car cheap.

The Ford Motor Company began making the Model T in 1908. In 1913 it introduced a moving assembly line at its Highland Park factory, and each worker repeated one task as the car moved past. Output rose, and the cost per car fell. Ford passed part of the saving to buyers: the price fell from about 850 US dollars in 1908 to about 260 dollars in the mid-1920s. The story shows economies of scale at work, from division of labour and from large output spread over a single design.

## Sources

> Where the ideas first appeared.

Alfred Marshall, *Principles of Economics* (1890), discussed real cost and money cost. Jacob Viner's article of 1931 on cost curves and supply curves set out the long-run curve as an envelope of short-run curves.
