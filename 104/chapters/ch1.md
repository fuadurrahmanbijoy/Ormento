:chapter I | What Economics Is About
:mood Why can no one have everything?
:desc This chapter starts from a plain fact of daily life: wishes are many and means are few. It shows why every person, family and country must choose, and what each choice costs. It then explains what economics is, and the three questions every society must answer.

> A small family budget shows the problem that the whole book studies.
Imagine a family with Tk 20,000 to spend this month. The family wants rice, fish, school books, a bicycle for the eldest child, new roof sheets, and a journey to visit relatives. Added together, these wishes cost far more than Tk 20,000. The family cannot buy everything. Someone must decide what to buy now and what to leave for later.

> The same problem faces farms, shops and governments.
Now widen the picture. A farmer has one field. It can grow rice or jute, but not both in the same season. A government has one budget. It can build a road or a school, but it cannot use the same taka for both. A baker has one oven and twelve working hours in a day. Each of them wants more than they can have. Each of them must choose.

> The chapter asks why choice is forced on us and what a choice really costs.
This chapter asks three plain questions. Why is choice forced on everyone, even on rich people and rich countries? What does a choice cost, when no money is paid? And what is the subject that studies these questions? You need no earlier knowledge to begin. Everyday experience of spending, saving and choosing is enough.

## The Problem of Wanting More

@! Go slowly here.
> A student's small purse shows the gap between wishes and money.
Begin with a student who has Tk 100 to last the week. The student would like a notebook for Tk 60, a lunch for Tk 70, a bus ticket for Tk 40 and a cinema seat for Tk 80. Add the four prices and the total is Tk 250. The purse holds Tk 100. The student can have some of these things, but not all of them. This gap between what is wished and what is possible is the root of the whole subject.

@ Want | A wish for something.
> A want is any wish, wise or not, and new wants keep arriving.
We need a word for the wishes in this picture. The word is **want**. A want means a wish for something. The student's wish for a cinema seat is one want. A family's wish for a better roof is another. A country's wish for a new bridge is a third. Economists do not judge whether a want is wise or foolish. They note only that wants exist and that they are very many. When one want is met, another often takes its place. A person who has a bicycle may soon want a motorcycle.

@ Resource | Anything used to make goods.
> Resources are the things used to make goods, and they are limited.
Wants are met by goods, and goods must be made. Making them needs materials, tools and people. Each such thing is a **resource**. A resource means anything used to make goods. Soil, water, human work and machines are all resources. A baker uses flour, an oven and hours of work to make bread. If the flour runs out or the hours end, no more bread can be made.

@ Good | In daily life, good means nice or kind. In economics, a good is a thing that satisfies a want.
@ Service | Work done for others, such as teaching.
> A good satisfies a want; a service is work done for others.
What do resources make? They make things that satisfy wants. We call such a thing a **good**. Rice, a bicycle and a pair of shoes are goods. Some wants are met by work done for others, and not by a thing that can be held. A teacher's lesson and a barber's haircut are examples. This book calls such work a **service**. To save words, the book often writes goods for both, and it says so when the difference matters.

@ Factors of production | Land, labour, capital and entrepreneur.
@ Land | Gifts of nature, such as soil and water.
@ Labour | Human work of the body or the mind.
> Resources fall into four groups; the first two are land and labour.
Economists sort resources into four groups. Together the groups are called the **factors of production**. The first is **land**, which means all gifts of nature, such as soil, water, forests, minerals and fish.^1 The second is **labour**, which means human work, whether of the body or of the mind. A farmer who ploughs and a clerk who keeps accounts both supply labour.

^1: Economists use land in a wide sense. A river and the fish in it, and the oil under the sea bed, are all land.

@ Capital | Made things used to make other goods.
@ Entrepreneur | A person who starts and runs a business and bears its risk.
> The other two groups are capital and the entrepreneur.
The third is **capital**, which means made things that are used to produce other goods, such as ovens, tools and buildings. In this book capital does not mean money. The fourth is the **entrepreneur**. An entrepreneur is a person who starts and runs a business and bears its risk. If the business fails, the entrepreneur loses the most.

> A table gives one bakery example of each group.
The table below gives one example of each factor, taken from a bakery. Read each row from left to right: the factor, its meaning, and the example.

:table The four factors of production
| Factor | Meaning | Example in a bakery |
| Land | Gifts of nature | The wheat grown in the soil; the water for the dough |
| Labour | Human work | The baker who kneads and bakes |
| Capital | Made things used in production | The oven and the baking trays |
| Entrepreneur | The person who starts the business and bears its risk | The owner who decides to open the shop |

> Each factor earns a reward, which later chapters will study.
Every factor earns a reward when it is used. Later chapters study these rewards and how they are set.^2 For now, remember only that all four are resources, and that all four are limited.

^2: Chapters XV and XVI study the rewards of the four factors.

@ Scarcity | Too little exists to satisfy all wants.
> Scarcity means resources are too few to meet all wants.
We can now name the gap seen in the student's purse. **Scarcity** means that too little exists to satisfy all wants. Resources are limited, and wants are not, so some wants must go unmet. The student has Tk 100 against wants worth Tk 250. A country has a limited amount of land, labour and capital, against a long list of wants. Scarcity is the starting point of economics.

> Scarcity is not poverty, and it differs from a shortage.
Two confusions should be removed at once. First, scarcity is not the same as poverty. A rich family also has limited money and limited time, and it too must choose. Second, scarcity is not the same as a shortage. A shortage is a short-lived lack of one good, such as no rice in the shop today because the lorry is late. A shortage may end next week. Scarcity applies to nearly all goods, and it does not end.

@ Free good | A good that exists in unlimited amount.
> A free good exists in unlimited amount, so no one must choose.
Is anything not scarce? A **free good** means a good that exists in unlimited amount. Sea air on an empty beach is an example. Anyone may breathe as much as they like, and no one's use leaves less for another person. Free goods are rare, and they raise no problem of choice. Notice that the word free here is not about price. A lesson in a government school costs the student nothing, yet it is not a free good, because teachers, buildings and books were used to provide it.

> Recap: wants are many, resources are few, and the gap is scarcity.
*Before moving on.* Wants are many. Resources are few, and they come in four groups. The gap between them is scarcity. A free good is the rare thing that is not scarce. *You should now be able to say:* wants are many, resources are limited, and this gap is called scarcity.

> A short question tests the idea of scarcity.
*Pause and check.* A friend says that a shop in the town has no sugar this week. Is this scarcity or a shortage? Give a reason.

## Choice and Opportunity Cost

@! Go slowly here.
> Every choice gives something up, even a free evening.
Return to the student, now with an evening free on Friday. A cousin has asked for help at the family shop and will pay Tk 200 for the evening. A friend has invited the student to a film, and the ticket is free. The student cannot do both, because both take the same evening. The student chooses the film. What did the film cost? No money was paid, so we might say that it cost nothing. But the student gave up Tk 200. That is the real cost of the evening.

@ Choice | Picking one use of limited resources.
> A choice means picking one use of limited resources.
The student made a **choice**. A choice means picking one use of limited resources, such as money or time, and leaving the others. Choice exists only because of scarcity. If resources were unlimited, the student could help at the shop and see the film as well. No one would need to choose.

@ Opportunity cost | The best alternative given up.
> Opportunity cost is the best alternative given up.
Economists give the cost of a choice a special name. The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the best alternative given up. In our example the film evening has an opportunity cost of Tk 200, because helping at the shop was the best alternative. The word opportunity is a reminder: the cost is an opportunity that was lost.

> Only the single best alternative counts, not all of them.
The word best matters. Take a farmer with one field. Planted with rice, the field would give a net income of Tk 40,000 this season. Net income means income after all costs are paid. Planted with jute, it would give Tk 30,000. Planted with pulses, it would give Tk 25,000. The farmer plants rice. The opportunity cost is Tk 30,000, the net income from jute. It is not Tk 55,000, the sum of jute and pulses, because the field can hold only one of them. The farmer gives up the best of the other choices, and that one counts.

> Recap: the cost of a choice is the best thing given up.
*Before moving on.* Choice follows scarcity. The cost of a choice is the best alternative given up, even when no money is paid. Only one alternative counts: the best of those not chosen. *You should now be able to say:* the cost of a choice is the best thing I give up to make it.

> A short question tests the idea of opportunity cost.
*Pause and check.* A student has one hour. The student can tutor a child for Tk 150, or sell snacks for Tk 100. The student chooses tutoring. What is the opportunity cost of the hour?

> Opportunity cost can be counted in money, in time or in goods.
Opportunity cost is not always money. For a student, an hour on a phone game is an hour not spent on study. For a country, a taka spent on a road is a taka not spent on a school. Money gives a convenient way to measure the cost, but the cost itself is the alternative that was lost.

> The bakery owner meets the same cost, in larger amounts.
Now view the same idea from the owner's seat. A person who opens a bakery pays money for rent, flour and wages. These payments are easy to see. But the owner also gives up things that no one is paid for: the wage that a job would have brought, and the interest that the savings would have earned in a bank. These forgone rewards are real costs. The worked case at the end of this chapter measures them. Chapter X returns to the idea and shows how a firm counts such costs.^3

^3: Chapter X, section 10.1, measures opportunity cost and separates costs paid in money from costs not paid.

> Opportunity cost needs a real alternative to exist.
Some limits keep the idea clear. If there is truly no alternative, nothing is given up, and the opportunity cost is zero. The cost is judged at the time of the choice, by what could have been done then. And opportunity cost is not regret. A choice may turn out well even when its opportunity cost was high.

## What Economics Is

> Thinkers have answered the question "what is economics?" in different ways.
Ask what economics is, and the answer depends on whom you ask and when. Over two hundred years, three well-known answers were given. Each was a step forward, and each left something out. Looking at them in order shows why the modern answer rests on the two ideas you have met: scarcity and choice.

### Wealth: Adam Smith, 1776

> The earliest famous answer says economics studies wealth.
Adam Smith was a Scottish thinker who taught at the University of Glasgow. In 1776 he published a book about the nature and causes of the wealth of nations. Many readers took from it a plain definition: economics is the study of wealth, meaning how a nation produces goods of value and grows richer. This is called the wealth definition.

> The wealth definition is too narrow, because it puts goods before people.
The wealth definition has two limits. It places goods at the centre and the people who need them at the side. It also makes the subject seem a study of money-making only, which many readers found too narrow. People want wealth for what it does for their lives.

### Well-being: Alfred Marshall, 1890

> Marshall moved people to the centre and treated wealth as a means.
Alfred Marshall was a professor at Cambridge, in England. In 1890 he published *Principles of Economics*. He described economics as a study of people in the ordinary business of life. He held that wealth matters because it serves human well-being, and he spoke of material well-being, meaning the well-being that comes from goods that can be bought and sold. This is called the welfare definition.

> The welfare definition has limits: it divides life into material and other parts.
The welfare definition put people first, yet it too has limits. It seems to count only material things, so the work of a teacher or a doctor sat uneasily inside it. It also says little about choice, which is the activity that economics actually studies. Many lives mix material and other aims in one decision.

### Scarcity and Choice: Lionel Robbins, 1932

> Robbins defined economics by scarcity and choice.
Lionel Robbins taught at the London School of Economics. In 1932 he published *An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science*. He said that economics studies human behaviour as a relation between wants and scarce resources that have alternative uses.^4 In plain words, economics studies choice under scarcity. This is the scarcity and choice definition.

^4: Robbins wrote ends where this book writes wants, and means where it writes resources. The meaning is the same.

> The scarcity definition fits every choice, but it says little about whole economies.
This definition fits any choice, in any society, whether or not money is used. The student's evening, the farmer's field and a government's budget all fall inside it. Its weakness is that it says little about why a whole economy may produce less, or why many jobs may vanish. Later writers treated those questions as a separate branch, as Chapter II shows.

> A table sets the three definitions side by side.
The table sets the three answers side by side. Read it row by row. Each row gives one thinker, the central idea, one strength and one limit.

:table Three definitions compared
| Thinker and date | Central idea | Strength | Limit |
| Adam Smith, 1776 | Wealth: how nations grow rich | Focus on producing goods | Puts goods before people |
| Alfred Marshall, 1890 | Well-being: wealth serves people | People come first | Seems to count only material things |
| Lionel Robbins, 1932 | Scarcity and choice | Fits any choice | Says little about whole economies |

### The Central Theme

@ Economics | The study of choice under scarcity.
> Modern books join scarcity, choice and well-being in one definition.
Today most textbooks keep the idea of Robbins as the core and keep the concern of Marshall for people. This book therefore uses a working definition. **Economics** means the study of choice under scarcity. It asks how individuals, firms and governments use limited resources to meet wants, and what follows for the well-being of people.

:box IN SHORT
Economics is the study of how people and societies choose among uses of scarce resources. Its theme is scarcity and choice.

> Everything in the book grows from one chain of ideas.
The central theme can be written as a chain. Wants are many. Resources are limited. So there is scarcity. Scarcity forces choice. Every choice has an opportunity cost. Each later chapter takes one link of this chain and studies it closely: how buyers choose, how sellers choose, and how markets bring the two together.

> Economics explains, predicts and guides choice.
Knowing what economics studies, we can ask what it is for. It does three jobs. It *explains*: why did the price of onions rise in one month? It *predicts*: if a tax is placed on fuel, buyers will probably use less. And it *guides* choice: it shows a family, a firm or a government what each option costs, so that the choice is made with open eyes. Economics cannot choose for anyone, because the final choice depends on values. Chapter II returns to this point.

> Recap: three definitions led to one working definition.
*Before moving on.* Smith looked at wealth, Marshall at well-being, and Robbins at scarcity and choice. This book defines economics as the study of choice under scarcity. Its three jobs are to explain, to predict and to guide choice.

## The Three Basic Problems

> Because of scarcity, every society must answer three questions.
Think of a village after a poor harvest. There is not enough rice for everyone. The villagers must decide what to plant next year, how to work the fields, and who will receive the rice that exists. These questions are not special to villages. Every society, large or small, poor or rich, meets the same three problems, because every society has scarce resources and many wants.

> What to produce: which goods, and how much of each.
The first problem is *what* to produce. Land, labour and capital can make rice or jute, schools or roads, shirts or mobile phones. Using them for one purpose takes them from another. The society must decide which goods to make, and how much of each. This is the choice of the farmer's field, now made for a whole country.

> How to produce: which method and which mix of resources.
The second problem is *how* to produce. The same output can be made in different ways. Rice can be harvested by many workers with sickles, or by a few workers with a machine. The first method uses much labour and little capital. The second uses much capital and little labour. The society must choose a method, and the choice depends on which resources it has in plenty and which are scarce.

> For whom to produce: who receives the goods that are made.
The third problem is *for whom* to produce. When goods are made, someone receives them. They may go to those who pay the most, to those in greatest need, or in equal shares. This question is about sharing the output of a society among its people.

@ Economic system | The way a society answers what, how and for whom.
> An economic system is the way a society answers the three problems.
A society's answers to the three problems form its **economic system**. An economic system means the way a society answers what, how and for whom. Some writers call it an economic organisation. This book uses the term economic system throughout.

> Four broad kinds of system answer the problems differently.
Real economies differ in who decides. In a traditional system, custom decides: sons farm as their fathers did. In a market system, prices and the free choices of buyers and sellers decide. In a command system, the government decides by plan. In a mixed system, markets and government share the work. Most countries today, Bangladesh among them, are mixed. The table shows the main answers. Read each row as one kind of system.

:table How four systems answer the three problems
| System | What to produce | How to produce | For whom |
| Traditional | What custom has always made | By inherited methods | By family and custom |
| Market | What buyers will pay for | By methods that cost least | To those who can pay |
| Command | By government plan | By government orders | By government rule or ration |
| Mixed | Partly by prices, partly by government | Mostly by firms, under rules | By prices, with taxes and public services |

> No system removes scarcity; each handles the problems in its own way.
No system removes scarcity. Each handles the three problems in its own way, and each has strengths and costs. Later chapters study the market system closely, because prices and markets are the subject of microeconomics. Notice also that the three problems are choices, and so each carries an opportunity cost: more of one good means less of another.

> Recap: scarcity forces every society to answer what, how and for whom.
*Before moving on.* Every society must decide what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. The pattern of answers is its economic system. The four kinds are traditional, market, command and mixed.

## A Worked Case: The Owner's Choice

> The case file begins on 2 February 2026 with a choice.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Situation. On 2 February 2026 an owner has savings of Tk 600,000 and a job that pays Tk 40,000 a month. There are two options. Option A is to keep the job and deposit the savings in a bank, at an assumed 6% a year. Option B is to open a bakery.

> The question is what Option B costs besides money paid out.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Question. Apart from money paid to others, what does Option B cost the owner each month?

> Reasoning: Option A would pay interest of Tk 3,000 a month, plus the wage.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Reasoning. Option A gives two rewards. The wage is Tk 40,000 a month. The interest is 6% of Tk 600,000, which is Tk 36,000 a year, or Tk 3,000 a month. Choosing the bakery gives up both. The opportunity cost of the bakery is therefore Tk 40,000 + Tk 3,000 = Tk 43,000 a month. Rent comes on top of this, because it is a money payment. On 1 March 2026 the bakery opens, with shop rent of Tk 25,000 a month and a loaf price of Tk 60.

> The owner must also settle the three basic problems.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* The three problems. What to produce: loaves and buns. How to produce: with one oven and hired bakers. For whom: the households of the neighbourhood.

> Conclusion and check: the bakery is worth opening only if it earns more than Tk 43,000 a month above its running costs.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Conclusion. The bakery is a good choice only if it can earn more than Tk 43,000 a month above what it pays out in running costs. Later chapters test this. Check. 6% of Tk 600,000 is 36,000, and 36,000 divided by 12 is 3,000. Then 40,000 + 3,000 is 43,000.

## Summary

> Scarcity forces choice, and choice has a cost.
Wants are many and resources are limited, so scarcity exists. Resources are land, labour, capital and the entrepreneur. Scarcity forces choice, and the cost of a choice is the best alternative given up. Economics is the study of choice under scarcity, and it explains, predicts and guides choice. Every society must decide what to produce, how, and for whom. Its answers form its economic system.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Scarcity does not mean poverty or a shortage.
Many students write that scarcity means poor people have too little. It does not. Scarcity means that wants exceed resources, and it applies to the richest family and the richest country. A shortage is a temporary lack of one good. Scarcity is lasting.

> Opportunity cost is not the money price, and it is not the sum of alternatives.
Many students give the price paid as the cost of a choice. The price is only part of it, and for a free film it is zero while the cost is not. Others add up every alternative given up. Only the single best alternative counts.

> Free in price is not the same as a free good.
A good that costs the user nothing, such as a government school lesson, is not a free good. Resources were used to provide it, and they could have been used elsewhere. A free good exists in unlimited amount, with no resources used up.

## In History

> Wartime food rationing in Britain answered the question "for whom".
During the Second World War, Britain was short of imported food. Rationing began on 8 January 1940, first with bacon, butter and sugar, and later it covered many other foods. The last food controls ended in 1954. Each person received a ration book that gave a fixed weekly share. The government answered the question of *for whom* by shares, and not by price and money. The rule did not remove scarcity. It changed who received the scarce food, and it showed that every society must answer the question in some way.
