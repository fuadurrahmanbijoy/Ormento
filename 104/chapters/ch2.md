:chapter II | How Economists Think
:mood One event, seen from two places.
:desc This chapter separates the close-up view of economics from the wide view, and shows how each depends on the other. It explains how economists build simple pictures and state laws, how facts differ from values, and where the subject is used in Bangladesh.

> A rise in bus fares can be seen from two places.
On a Monday in March, bus fares in a city rise by Tk 5 a ride. A student at a bus stop does the sum: the two daily rides now cost Tk 10 more. The student decides to walk the shorter half of the journey and ride the longer half. At the same moment, a watcher in a government office looks at the whole city. The watcher sees that fares, food prices and house rents have all risen together, and that the number of passengers across the city has fallen.

> The two pictures answer different questions, and both are true.
Both pictures are true. The student's picture is small and close: one person, one choice, one price. The watcher's picture is wide: the whole city, many prices, total travel. Economists use both. This chapter shows how the two differ, and how they depend on each other. It then shows how economists build simple pictures of the world, how they separate facts from opinions, and where the close-up picture is used in Bangladesh.

> This chapter builds on scarcity and choice from Chapter I.
You will need two ideas from Chapter I: scarcity, and choice with its opportunity cost. Both appear here again, in new settings.

## Two Ways of Looking

> Economics has a close-up branch and a wide branch.
Look again at the two viewers in the opening story. The student studied one household's choice. The watcher studied a whole economy. Economics has a name for each way of looking. The two names come from Greek words: *mikros*, which means small, and *makros*, which means large.

@ Microeconomics | The study of single households, firms and markets.
> Microeconomics studies the choices of single households, firms and markets.
**Microeconomics** means the study of the choices of single households, firms and markets, and of the prices that result. A family choosing what to eat is a micro subject. So is a tailor choosing the price of a shirt, and so is the market for fish in one town. The unit is small, and the questions are about choice and price.

@ Household | People who live together and spend together.
@ Firm | An organisation that makes goods for sale.
> Households and firms are the two main decision makers in micro study.
Two words need clear meaning. A **household** means people who live together and spend together, such as a family sharing one kitchen. A **firm** means an organisation that makes goods for sale, such as a bakery, a garment factory or a bus company. Households buy goods and supply labour. Firms hire labour and make goods. Chapter III onwards studies how each chooses.

@ Macroeconomics | The study of the whole economy.
> Macroeconomics studies the whole economy.
**Macroeconomics** means the study of the whole economy: its total output, the general level of prices, and the total number of jobs. The watcher in the government office was asking macro questions. Why did the total output of the country grow more slowly this year? Why did prices in general rise? The unit is the whole, and the questions are about totals and averages.

> A table sets the two branches side by side.
The table below compares the two branches. Read down a column to see what one branch studies. Read across a row to compare the branches on one feature.

:table Microeconomics and macroeconomics compared
| Feature | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
| Unit of study | A household, a firm, a market | The whole economy |
| Typical question | Why did the price of onions rise? | Why did prices in general rise? |
| Example of a subject | The price a bakery sets for a loaf | The total number of jobs in the country |
| Main idea | Choice and price in one market | Totals and averages over all markets |
| Viewpoint | Close up | Wide |

> The two branches depend on each other.
The two branches are not rivals. They depend on each other. The total output of a country is built from the output of its firms, so macro results rest on micro choices. Yet the whole can behave in a way that no single part shows. Suppose every family decides to save more and spend less. Each family acts sensibly. But shops then sell less, some firms cut jobs, and family incomes fall. This result can be seen only from the wide view. In the other direction, general conditions, such as the level of prices, form the surroundings in which each firm chooses.

> Recap: micro is close up, macro is wide, and each needs the other.
*Before moving on.* Microeconomics studies single households, firms and markets. Macroeconomics studies the whole economy. Each depends on the other. This book is about microeconomics.

## Scope, Importance and Purposes

> Microeconomics covers three large areas.
What does microeconomics cover? Its scope has three parts. The first is the pricing of products: how demand, supply and cost decide the price of bread, cloth or bus rides. Chapters III to XIV study this. The second is the pricing of resources: what rent, wages, interest and profit are paid, and why. Chapters XV and XVI study this.

@ Welfare | The well-being of people.
> The third part is welfare, the well-being of people.
The third part is **welfare**. Welfare means the well-being of people. Here we ask whether the way markets work serves people well, and who gains or loses when something changes. A new tax may raise the money a government needs. It may also leave some households with less to spend. Welfare study keeps both effects in view.

> Microeconomics helps consumers, firms and governments choose.
Why does it matter? For consumers, it explains how prices arise and how to spend limited money with care. For firms, it shows how costs, demand and rivals affect the price and output to choose. For governments, it shows who gains and who loses from a tax, a price limit or a subsidy. A government that sets a price limit on rice without knowing how buyers and sellers react may bring about the very shortage that it fears.

> The purposes are to explain, to predict and to guide choice.
Chapter I named three jobs of economics. Microeconomics does each job for single units. It explains why a price is what it is. It predicts what may happen when a price, a tax or an income changes. It guides choice by showing what each option costs. In this way it serves the student, the shopkeeper and the official alike.

> The student also gains a habit of questions.
A student of microeconomics also gains a habit of asking three questions. What is scarce here? What is the best alternative? Who bears the cost? These questions help in running a shop, in reading a news report, and in judging a public plan.

> Recap: three areas, three kinds of user, three purposes.
*Before moving on.* The scope is product pricing, resource pricing and welfare. The users are consumers, firms and governments. The purposes are to explain, to predict and to guide choice.

## Models and Economic Laws

@! Go slowly here.
> A bus route map is wrong in detail and still useful.
Think of the route map pinned inside a bus. It shows lines and stops. It leaves out the shape of the roads, the shops, the trees and the hills. A map with all of these would be too crowded to read. The route map is useful because it leaves things out. It shows only what a traveller needs in order to find the way.

@ Economic model | A simple picture of how something works.
> A model is a simple picture that keeps only what matters.
An **economic model** is the same kind of thing for the economy. An economic model means a simple picture of how something works. It keeps a few important features and sets the rest aside. A model of the market for tea may keep only the price of tea and the number of cups bought, and ignore the colour of the cups. Because the model is simple, we can reason through it step by step.

> A model is judged by whether it helps, not by whether it is complete.
No model is complete. A model is judged by a plain test: does it help us explain what happens and predict what may happen? A model that fails the test is changed or dropped.

@ Ceteris paribus | All other things held unchanged.
> Ceteris paribus means all other things held unchanged.
To use a model we must change one thing at a time. Suppose the price of tea rises, and in the same month the weather turns hot and incomes fall. The number of cups bought changes, and we cannot tell which cause did what. So economists use an old Latin phrase, **ceteris paribus**, which means all other things held unchanged. We then ask: if only the price of tea changes, what happens to the cups bought? This is a tool of thought, like testing one switch at a time. It does not claim that the real world stands still.

@ Economic law | A statement of a regular tendency.
> An economic law is a statement of a regular tendency.
Models help us find regular patterns. When a pattern holds in most cases, under stated conditions, we call it an **economic law**. An economic law means a statement of a regular tendency. One example, met in Chapter IV, is that buyers tend to buy less of a good when its price rises, other things unchanged. The word law here does not mean a rule made by a government. No one is punished for breaking an economic law.

> Economic laws have five features.
Five features mark an economic law. First, it is a statement of tendency and not of certainty: most buyers, most of the time. Second, it holds under conditions, usually ceteris paribus. Third, it rests on the observed behaviour of people. Fourth, it can be tested by facts, though not in a laboratory where everything is controlled. Fifth, it is less exact than a law of nature, because people differ and change their minds.

> A table compares economic laws with laws of nature.
A law of nature, such as the law that a dropped stone falls, is a useful comparison. The table below sets the two kinds of law side by side. Read each row to see one point of difference.

:table Laws of nature and economic laws
| Feature | Law of nature | Economic law |
| Subject | Matter and forces | Human choices |
| Statement | Holds exactly | Holds as a tendency |
| Test | Controlled experiment | Observation of events |
| Exactness | High | Lower, because people vary |
| Example | A dropped stone falls | A price rise lowers purchases |

> A law does not fail where its conditions are absent.
If the stated conditions are absent, the law does not apply. Most laws of economics come with exceptions, and later chapters name and explain them. An exception is not a defeat for the law. It tells us where the conditions have changed.

> Recap: models simplify, ceteris paribus isolates one cause, and laws state tendencies.
*Before moving on.* A model keeps what matters. Ceteris paribus holds other things unchanged. An economic law states a tendency under conditions. *You should now be able to say:* an economic law tells what usually happens when other things stay the same.

> A short question tests the idea of an economic law.
*Pause and check.* A friend says: "The law of demand is false, because I bought more tea last month even though the price rose." What has the friend left out?

## Positive and Normative; Science or Art

> Some statements can be tested by facts, and some cannot.
Compare two statements. One: the price of lentils was higher in March than in February. Two: the price of lentils should be lowered for poor families. The first can be checked by looking at prices in the two months. The second cannot be checked in that way, because it expresses a view about what is right.

@ Positive statement | A statement that can be tested by facts.
> A positive statement can be tested by facts.
A **positive statement** means a statement that can be tested by facts. It says what is, what was, or what will be. It may be true or false, but the way to settle it is by evidence. "A rise in bus fares reduces the number of passengers" is a positive statement. Evidence may show that it is wrong, and it is still positive.

@ Normative statement | A statement of what ought to be.
> A normative statement says what ought to be.
A **normative statement** means a statement of what ought to be. It rests on values, such as fairness. "Bus fares should be kept low for students" is a normative statement. Evidence about costs may help the debate, but evidence alone cannot settle it, because people hold different values.

> Disagreements about facts and about values are different disagreements.
When two people disagree, ask which kind of disagreement it is. If they disagree about a positive matter, such as whether a price limit causes shortages, they can look at evidence. If they disagree about a normative matter, such as whether poor families should receive cheap rice, they must debate values. Much confusion comes from mixing the two. Economics helps with the first kind directly. It helps with the second kind by showing what each choice costs.

> Economics is both a science and an art.
Is economics a science or an art? A science is organised knowledge built by observation and reasoning. It tells us what is. An art is the skilled use of knowledge to reach an aim. It tells us how to do something. In 1891 John Neville Keynes, an English economist, described three parts of the subject: a positive science, a normative science of ideals, and an art of rules for reaching goals.^1 By this view, economics is a science when it explains, and an art when it advises a government or a firm. It also uses normative judgements when it asks what ought to be done. The honest answer is that it is both.

^1: John Neville Keynes was the father of John Maynard Keynes, who appears in the history section of this chapter.

> Recap: positive means testable, normative means ought, and economics is science and art.
*Before moving on.* A positive statement can be tested by facts. A normative statement says what ought to be. Economics is a science when it explains and an art when it advises.

## Microeconomics in Bangladesh

> Microeconomic questions appear in daily life in Bangladesh.
Microeconomics is not only a classroom subject. Many ordinary events in Bangladesh are micro questions. Why does the price of vegetables change between a good harvest and a flood season? How does a farmer decide between boro rice and another crop? Why do garment factories in one district compete for the same workers, and what does this do to wages? What rules stop sellers from agreeing together to fix a price?

> The subject has growing uses in business, farming, public work and research.
These questions point to the prospects of the subject. Firms need people who can judge costs, prices and demand. Farmers and farm advisers choose crops and methods. Public bodies set rules for markets. Researchers use data to study households and firms. There are also difficulties: many markets are informal, and good data on small firms is hard to obtain. A student who learns the method can help with both the use and the difficulty.

> Several kinds of institution teach, study and apply microeconomics.
Several kinds of institution teach, research and apply the subject. The table lists six, with one example of each and its main role. The names and duties should be checked against current official sources, because public bodies are renamed and reorganised.^2

*Before moving on.* Microeconomic questions arise in prices, farm output, wages and competition rules. Universities, research institutes, the central bank, the statistics office, the planning body and the competition commission teach, study or apply the subject.

## A Worked Case: One Firm, One Economy

> The bakery is a micro unit, and a general price rise is a macro event.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Situation. The bakery opened on 1 March 2026 and sells loaves at Tk 60. It is one firm in the neighbourhood market for bread. Suppose that the price of flour rises as part of a general rise in prices across the economy.

> The question is which of the bakery's concerns are micro and which are macro.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Question. Which concerns of the owner belong to microeconomics, and which to macroeconomics?

> Reasoning: choices inside the market are micro; the general price rise is macro.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Reasoning. The owner decides the price of a loaf, the number of bakers to hire and the hours of work. These are choices of one firm in one market, and so they are micro decisions. The general rise in prices comes from outside the bakery. It affects all firms and households, and it is measured for the whole economy, so it is a macro event. The owner cannot change it, but must respond to it.

> Conclusion and check: ask whether the question is about one unit or the whole economy.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Conclusion. The price the bakery sets is micro. The general price rise is macro. Check. Ask of any question: is it about one household, firm or market, or about totals for the whole economy? No new figures are added in this chapter.

## Summary

> Economics has two views and uses models and laws.
Microeconomics studies single households, firms and markets. Macroeconomics studies the whole economy, and each depends on the other. Microeconomics covers product pricing, resource pricing and welfare. Economists use simple models and the idea of ceteris paribus to state laws of tendency. Positive statements can be tested by facts, and normative statements say what ought to be. Economics is both a science and an art, and it is applied in Bangladesh in many ways.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Micro does not mean only small businesses.
Many students think microeconomics studies small firms and macroeconomics studies large ones. The difference is in the question, not in the size. A large company's price is a micro matter. The total jobs in a country is a macro matter.

> Ceteris paribus does not claim that nothing else changes.
Many students think ceteris paribus means that nothing else changes in the real world. It is a way of thinking. We hold other things unchanged in the model, in order to see one cause at work.

> A positive statement is not a true statement, and a law is not a rule of government.
A positive statement can be false. It is called positive because it can be tested. An economic law is a statement of tendency. It is not a rule that anyone enforces.

## In History

> The Great Depression of the 1930s showed the need for a wide view.
In the early 1930s many countries suffered a long fall in output and a great rise in unemployment, called the Great Depression. Thinking about single markets did not explain why so many jobs vanished at once. In 1936 John Maynard Keynes published *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, which argued that total spending in the economy decides total output and jobs. The wide view became a branch of its own. The Norwegian economist Ragnar Frisch is often credited with using the words micro and macro for the two views in a paper of 1933.

## Sources

> The ideas of this chapter come from two writers on method.
John Neville Keynes, *The Scope and Method of Political Economy* (1891), separated the positive, the normative and the art. Milton Friedman, *The Methodology of Positive Economics* (1953), argued that a model should be judged by the quality of its predictions.
