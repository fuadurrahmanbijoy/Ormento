:chapter XV | Rent and Wages
:mood Two payments, one question: what is a shop, or a pair of hands, worth?
:desc A bakery pays Tk 25,000 for a shop and Tk 600 a day for a baker. This chapter shows how the market sets such payments for land and for labour, why they differ from place to place and person to person, and what a payment really buys.

> A bakery owner must pay for a shop and for a baker, and wonders how such prices arise.

Look at two shops in one town. One stands on a quiet lane and costs Tk 25,000 a month. The other stands on the main road and costs Tk 40,000. The land under them is the same size. Why does the second owner ask Tk 15,000 more?

> Two equal shops carry different prices, and the chapter must explain the gap.

Now look at two workers. One weaves cloth in a mill. The other repairs the mill's machines. The second earns far more, though both work eight hours. Nobody sets these payments by a rule of fairness. The market sets them, and this chapter shows how.

> Two workers with equal hours earn unequal pay, and the market explains it.

We study rent and wages together because both are prices of inputs. In Chapters VIII to XIV we studied the prices of goods. Now we turn to the prices of the things used to make them.

> Rent and wages are prices of inputs, not of finished goods.

## 15.1 Pricing the Factors

> A baker is wanted for the loaves, so the demand for bakers depends on loaf demand.

A bakery buys flour, a shop and the work of bakers. Each purchase happens in a market, but this market is not the one where loaves are sold. It is the market for inputs.

@ Factor market | A market where inputs are bought and sold.

> The two terms: the market for inputs and the price paid in it.
A **factor market** is a market where inputs such as land and labour are bought and sold. The price paid in it is the **factor price**. Rent is the price of land, and a wage is the price of labour. (Chapter XVI treats the prices of capital and of the entrepreneur.)

> Demand for an input exists only because buyers want the goods the input makes.

@ Derived demand | Demand for an input that comes from the demand for the good it makes.

Nobody wants a baker for his own sake. The owner wants a baker because customers want loaves. We therefore say that the demand for bakers is a **derived demand**. It is derived from, that is, it follows from, the demand for loaves. If customers stopped buying loaves, the bakery would stop hiring bakers.

> The owner compares what one more worker adds to revenue with what that worker costs.

How many bakers should the owner hire? In Chapter IX we met marginal product, the extra output from one more worker. The owner now asks a money question: how much extra revenue does that output bring?

@ Marginal revenue product | The extra revenue a firm gains from one more unit of an input.

> The name for the extra revenue is marginal revenue product.
This extra revenue is the **marginal revenue product** of the input. For a seller who must accept the market price (Chapter XII), it equals marginal product multiplied by the price of the good. When the firm also pays for materials, we subtract that cost, and the result is the extra money the worker truly brings in.

@! Go slowly here: this rule is used for the whole chapter.

> The hiring rule says: hire another worker while the worker adds at least as much as the wage.

The rule is simple. Hire one more worker while the marginal revenue product is at least the wage. Stop when it falls below the wage. A worker who adds Tk 500 to revenue and costs Tk 400 raises profit by Tk 100. A worker who adds Tk 300 and costs Tk 400 lowers it by Tk 100.

> One clean example fixes the method.

*Given.* One more worker adds 10 units of output. Each unit sells at Tk 50. Materials cost nothing extra. The wage is Tk 400 a day. *Find.* Should the firm hire the worker? *Formula.* Marginal revenue product = marginal product x price. *Steps.* 10 units x Tk 50 = Tk 500. Compare Tk 500 with Tk 400. *Answer.* Hire: the worker adds Tk 100 a day to profit. *Check.* If the wage were Tk 600, the same worker would cost Tk 100 more than he adds, so the firm would not hire.

> Marginal revenue product usually falls as more workers are hired, which is why the firm stops.

The marginal revenue product falls as the firm hires more. The reason is diminishing returns (Chapter IX): each added worker adds less output. So the demand curve for an input slopes downward, like the demand curve for a good. A lower wage means that more workers are worth hiring.

> The supply of an input also matters, and it differs between land and labour.

The other side of the market is supply. More people will work in a trade if it pays more, so the supply of labour to one trade rises with the wage. Land is different. A society cannot make more land when rent rises. Next we see what this means.

## 15.2 Rent

> In daily speech rent is a payment for a house; in economics it means something wider.

@ Economic rent | A payment to an input above the least needed to keep it in use.

In daily speech, *rent* means the money paid to use a house, shop or machine. In economics the word has a second, sharper meaning. **Economic rent** is the part of a payment to an input that is above the least payment needed to keep that input in use. It is a surplus: more than the owner needs to be paid.

> Ricardo's question is why fertile land earns a surplus while poor land earns none.

* * *

@! Go slowly here: the three-grade table is the heart of the theory.

> Ricardo explained rent by differences in the land itself.

David Ricardo wrote about rent in 1817.^1 He asked a plain question. Farmers pay rent for good land. Why do they pay nothing for the worst land they use? His answer rests on two facts. Land differs in fertility and in position, and the amount of land is limited.

> Three plots of equal size and cost give different harvests.

Suppose three plots of land, each one acre, are used to grow rice. The same work and seed on each plot cost Tk 6,000, and this sum includes the farmer's normal profit. The plots differ in fertility. Plot A is the best, plot B is average, and plot C is the poorest that anyone farms.

:table Three grades of land, one acre each (illustrative)
| Plot | Harvest (sacks) | Value at Tk 300 a sack | Cost (Tk) | Rent (Tk) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 40 | 12,000 | 6,000 | 6,000 |
| B | 30 | 9,000 | 6,000 | 3,000 |
| C | 20 | 6,000 | 6,000 | 0 |

> The price is fixed by the poorest plot in use, which earns no rent.

How do we find the price of Tk 300? Rice from plot C costs Tk 6,000 for 20 sacks, which is Tk 300 a sack. Farmers will use plot C only if the price covers that cost. So the price settles at Tk 300. Plot C just pays its way and earns no rent. It is the *no-rent land*, the margin of use.

@ Differential rent | The extra return of better land over the worst land in use.

> Better plots earn a differential rent.
Plots A and B cost the same to farm but give more rice. Their extra return is **differential rent**: rent that comes from a difference. Plot A gives 20 more sacks than plot C, worth 20 x Tk 300 = Tk 6,000. Plot B gives 10 more sacks, worth Tk 3,000. These are the rents in the last column.

> Rent follows the price: when demand brings poorer land into use, every rent rises.

Now let the demand for rice grow. Farmers must bring in a fourth plot, D, which gives only 10 sacks. It will be used only if the price rises to Tk 600 a sack (Tk 6,000 divided by 10). At that price every rent rises. Plot A earns 40 x 600 - 6,000 = Tk 18,000. Plot B earns Tk 12,000 and plot C earns Tk 6,000. Plot D now earns none.

> A short example shows the steps for a plot of one's own.

*Given.* A plot gives 35 sacks. The worst plot in use gives 20 sacks. Both cost Tk 6,000 to farm. *Find.* The rent of the better plot. *Formula.* Rent = harvest x price - cost, where price = cost of worst plot / its harvest. *Steps.* Price = 6,000 / 20 = Tk 300. Value = 35 x 300 = Tk 10,500. Rent = 10,500 - 6,000. *Answer.* Rent is Tk 4,500. *Check.* The extra 15 sacks over the worst plot are worth 15 x 300 = Tk 4,500. The two methods agree.

> Ricardo's famous claim: rent does not make the price, the price makes the rent.

Ricardo drew a bold conclusion. The price of rice is set by the cost on the worst land. So rent is *the result* of a high price, not its cause. For the farming community as a whole, he said, a landlord's demand for rent does not raise the price of rice.

> The theory has limits that later writers pointed out.

The theory has limits. Few lands are truly free of rent. Land can be used for other crops, so a farmer who rents it must pay at least what another crop would pay. For one farmer, then, rent is a real cost. Also, land can be improved by drainage and fertiliser, and part of what looks like rent is a return on such work. Still, the central idea survives: a payment fixed by demand alone, on an input that cannot be increased.

> Contract rent is what the tenant actually pays, and it may differ from economic rent.

@ Contract rent | The sum agreed between owner and user for the use of a thing.

**Contract rent** is the sum that the owner and user agree for the use of a thing, such as the Tk 25,000 paid for the lane shop. It may include more than economic rent. It can contain interest on a building the owner put up, and pay for the owner's upkeep. Economic rent is only the surplus part.

> The location of a shop shows differential rent in a town.

Return to the two shops. The main road brings more customers, so the same bakery would earn more there. The extra earning is the reason the owner can ask more. The gap of Tk 15,000 is a differential rent, a rent of position instead of fertility.

> The modern view says any input in fixed supply can earn economic rent.

The modern view widens the idea. Economic rent is the payment to any input minus its *transfer earnings*, the least payment that would keep it in its present use. A singer paid Tk 100,000 for an evening, whose best other offer is Tk 10,000, earns an economic rent of Tk 90,000. If the supply of an input is fixed and cannot move elsewhere, all its payment is rent. When supply can move easily, rent is small.

> Man-made things earn a temporary surplus that economists call quasi-rent.

@ Quasi-rent | A temporary surplus earned by a man-made asset whose supply is fixed in the short run.

A bakery's oven is made by people, so more ovens can be built in time. In the short run, however, the number of ovens is fixed. If loaf prices are high, the oven earns a surplus above its running costs. This is **quasi-rent**, *quasi* meaning "almost": it looks like rent but lasts only until new ovens appear. In the bakery of Chapter XII, revenue is Tk 8,400 a day and variable cost is Tk 5,800, so the fixed assets earn Tk 2,600 a day. After paying their Tk 1,000 fixed cost, Tk 1,600 remains. Other bakers will notice this, build ovens, and the surplus will shrink.^2

## 15.3 Wages: Meaning

> A wage is the pay for work, and what matters is what it can buy.

@ Wage | The payment made for the use of labour.

A **wage** is the payment made for the use of labour. In everyday speech some people say "wages" for hourly or daily pay and "salary" for monthly pay. Economics uses *wage* for all of them. Pay may be by time (so much a day) or by piece (so much for each shirt sewn).

> Money wages may rise while buying power falls.

@ Nominal wage | The wage measured in money.

@ Real wage | What the wage can buy, shown by dividing the money wage by a price index.
@ Price index | A number showing the average level of prices compared with a base period set at 100.

The **nominal wage** is the wage in money: Tk 600 a day. The **real wage** is what that money can buy. If prices double and the nominal wage stays the same, the worker is poorer. To compare, we use a **price index**, a number that shows the average level of prices against a base of 100.

> A real wage is found by dividing the money wage by the index and multiplying by 100.

*Given.* A wage is Tk 500 a day. Prices rise so that the index goes from 100 to 125. *Find.* The real wage in old prices. *Formula.* Real wage = nominal wage / price index x 100. *Steps.* 500 / 125 = 4. 4 x 100 = 400. *Answer.* The real wage is Tk 400 in old prices. *Check.* Prices rose by a quarter, so the same money buys one-fifth less (400 is four-fifths of 500).

> A second reading of the real wage includes non-money benefits.

Real wages also include non-money gains: free meals, housing, shorter hours. Two jobs with the same money pay may differ in real reward. We use this idea in section 15.5.

## 15.4 How Wages Are Determined

> The wage in a trade comes from the demand for and supply of labour.

In a competitive market for labour, the wage is set as the price of any good is set in Chapter VIII. Employers demand labour, and workers supply it. Where the two quantities are equal, we have equilibrium in the labour market.

> Employers demand labour according to what a worker adds.

The demand for labour is the marginal revenue product curve of section 15.1. Each employer hires up to the point where the extra worker adds as much as the wage. At a high wage few workers are worth hiring; at a low wage many are. This is *marginal productivity theory*: the wage tends to equal the marginal revenue product of the last worker hired.

> Workers supply more labour to a trade when it pays more.

The supply of labour to one trade rises with the wage, because workers move in from poorer trades. Supply in a trade also depends on the cost and time of learning the skill. Where the wage settles, the quantity of labour wanted equals the quantity offered.

> A change in either side moves the wage.

A rise in the demand for loaves raises the demand for bakers and pushes the wage up. More people learning to bake raises the supply of bakers and pushes it down. These are the same shifts we saw in Chapter VIII, applied to labour.

> Bargaining can move a wage away from the market value.

@ Trade union | A group of workers who act together on pay and conditions.

@ Collective bargaining | Talks in which a union deals with employers on behalf of its members.

A **trade union** is a group of workers who act together. In **collective bargaining** the union deals with employers for all its members, instead of each worker making his own deal. A strong union may win a wage above the market level, though employers may then hire fewer workers. A strong employer, one of few buyers of labour, may hold wages below the level of marginal product. The result depends on who has more bargaining power.

> A minimum wage is a floor on the price of labour.

@ Minimum wage | The lowest wage that the law permits an employer to pay.

A **minimum wage** is the lowest wage that the law permits. It is a price floor (Chapter VIII). If the floor lies below the market wage, it changes nothing. If it lies above, workers who keep jobs earn more, but employers may want fewer workers, so some may lose work. How large that loss is depends on how strongly employers cut hiring, a question of elasticity (Chapter V). Careful studies of real cases have found different results, which is why economists still debate it.^3

## 15.5 Why Wages Differ

> The same market forces give different wages to different jobs.

In one economy a surgeon, a bus driver and a field worker all earn different wages. If labour were one uniform thing, competition would equalise pay. It is not. Workers differ, and so do jobs. Adam Smith named several of the causes in 1776.

> Hard, risky or unpleasant work pays more.

@ Compensating difference | Extra pay given for work that is unpleasant, risky or insecure.

A **compensating difference** is extra pay that makes up for the unpleasant side of a job. A worker in a dangerous shipbreaking yard, or one who works at night, is paid more than one with a safe daytime job. Without the extra, few would take the work. The wage evens out the total reward between jobs.

> Skill that takes long and costly training must be paid for.

Training costs money and years. A student who spends five years learning to be an engineer gives up earnings meanwhile, and may also pay fees. The wage after training must repay this, or no one would train. So skilled work pays more than unskilled work. Fewer people also have the skill, so supply is small.

> Workers in different grades do not compete with each other.

@ Non-competing groups | Groups of workers whose skills differ so much that one cannot easily move into the other.

A fitter cannot become a doctor next month. Workers form **non-competing groups**, layers of labour with little movement between them. Each layer has its own demand and supply, so each has its own wage. The gap between layers lasts for as long as training or entry is difficult.

> Bargaining power, place and other causes also play a part.

Other causes add to the list. Strong unions can lift pay in a trade. Wages differ between regions where living costs differ. Some jobs offer secure work for life and others do not. The table collects the main causes.

:table Main causes of wage differences
| Cause | Effect on wage | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk or discomfort | Higher | Night shift in a factory |
| Cost and time of training | Higher | Engineer or doctor |
| Scarce skill | Higher | Machine fitter in a mill |
| Strong bargaining power | Higher | Workers in a strong union |
| Easy entry by many workers | Lower | Unskilled day labour |

## Worked Case: The Bakery Hires

> The bakery uses these ideas to choose a shop and a number of bakers.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* The owner can rent a shop on the lane for Tk 25,000 a month or a main-road shop for Tk 40,000. The difference is Tk 15,000 a month. The extra rent is justified only if the main road brings extra revenue of more than Tk 15,000 a month.

> The hiring rule is applied to the product table of Chapter IX.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* The bakery bakes loaves at Tk 60. Materials cost Tk 20 for each loaf, so each loaf adds Tk 40 after materials. The product table of Chapter IX shows the marginal product of each baker. The fourth baker adds 30 loaves, worth Tk 1,200. The fifth adds 20 loaves: 20 x (60 - 20) = Tk 800. The sixth adds 10 loaves: 10 x 40 = Tk 400.

> With a wage of Tk 600 a day, the owner stops at the fifth baker.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* The wage is Tk 600 a day. The fifth baker adds Tk 800 and costs Tk 600, so hiring raises profit by Tk 200. The sixth adds Tk 400 and costs Tk 600, so hiring would lower profit by Tk 200. The bakery therefore hires five bakers. *Check.* The sixth baker still adds loaves, yet the owner does not hire him, because he adds less than he costs.

> A rise in prices lowers the buying power of a fixed wage.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Suppose prices rise by 5%, so the index is 105. The nominal wage of Tk 600 is unchanged, but the real wage is 600 / 1.05 = Tk 571.43 in old prices. The baker is poorer by about Tk 28.57 a day, though the pay slip shows no change.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in one place.

Rent and wages are prices of inputs, set in factor markets. Demand for an input is derived from demand for the good, and a firm hires while marginal revenue product is at least the price of the input. Economic rent is a surplus above the least payment needed; Ricardo explained it by differences in land, with the no-rent land fixing the price. Contract rent is the sum agreed; quasi-rent is a temporary surplus on man-made assets. A wage is the payment for labour; the real wage is the nominal wage divided by a price index. Wages are set by demand and supply, and by bargaining, and they differ because jobs and workers differ.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common mistakes, each corrected.

*"Rent is what a tenant pays."* This is contract rent. Economic rent is only the surplus part of it.

> The second mistake, with its correction.
*"High rent makes the price of rice high."* In Ricardo's theory the cause runs the other way: the price, set by the worst land, creates the rent.

> The third mistake, with its correction.
*"A higher money wage means a better life."* Not if prices rise faster. Compare real wages.

> The fourth mistake, with its correction.
*"A minimum wage always helps workers"* or *"always harms them"* are both too simple. The effect depends on the level of the floor and on how employers react.

## In History

> A true account from Bangladesh.

After the collapse of the Rana Plaza building on 24 April 2013, wages in the garment industry became a matter of national debate. A wage board set a new minimum for garment workers in 2013, and raised it in 2018 and again in 2023, to Tk 12,500 a month. Workers argued that rising prices had cut the real value of their pay. Owners argued that higher pay would raise their costs against foreign rivals. Both sides used the ideas of this chapter: real wage, marginal productivity and bargaining power.
