:chapter VIII | Markets and Equilibrium
:mood Who decides the price, when no one is in charge?
:desc Buyers want low prices and sellers want high ones, yet markets settle on a price at which what is offered matches what is wanted. This chapter brings demand and supply together, shows how the balance is found and restored, how it moves when conditions change, and who gains from it.

> A vegetable market's prices fall through the afternoon with no one ordering it.

In the morning, tomatoes at a town market cost Tk 60 a kilogram. By the evening the same tomatoes cost Tk 40, and almost nothing is left on the stalls. No official has ordered the fall, and no seller has announced a meeting. Each seller has simply watched the buyers, noticed the unsold heap, and lowered the price a little. Each buyer has noticed the lower price and bought a little more. By closing time sellers have sold nearly all they brought, and buyers have found what they wanted.

> How does a price find the level that clears a market?

Here is the puzzle. Buyers act for themselves and so do sellers. Yet the result is a price at which the amount wanted is close to the amount offered. How does this happen? This chapter answers the question with the demand curve of Chapters IV and V and the supply curve of Chapter VII. It also asks what happens when something changes, and who gains from the trade.

> This chapter combines demand, elasticity, surplus and supply.

*You will need* demand and its shifts (Chapter IV), consumers' surplus (Chapter VI), and supply and its shifts (Chapter VII).

## What a Market Is

> In economics, a market is any dealing between buyers and sellers, not only a place.

@ Market | Word in use: in daily life, a place to shop; in economics, buyers and sellers dealing with each other.

The word **market** has two meanings. In daily life it means a place where people shop, such as the market at the end of the road. In economics, a market means the dealing between the buyers and sellers of a good, wherever they are. It need not be one place. There is a market for mobile phones, for jute and for taxi rides, and these buyers and sellers may be scattered over a whole country, or linked only by telephone and the internet. What matters is that buyers and sellers are in contact, and that the same good is being bought and sold.

> A competitive market has many buyers and sellers, none strong enough to set the price.

@ Competitive market | A market with so many buyers and sellers that none can change the price alone.

The simplest kind of market is the competitive market. A **competitive market** means a market with so many buyers and sellers that no single one can change the price alone. Each bakery in the neighbourhood bun market is small. If one bakery raised its price a lot, its customers would walk to another. Each customer is also small, and one customer's choice cannot move the price. In such a market the price is set by all buyers and sellers together, and demand and supply explain it.

> Other kinds of market exist and are studied in Chapter XI.

Not every market is competitive. In some a single seller controls the supply, and in others a few large sellers watch one another. Chapter XI describes these types, and Chapters XII to XIV study them. This chapter treats the competitive market, because it is the clearest case and the basis of the others.

> A market connects buyers and sellers of one good; a competitive market has too many to matter singly.

*Before moving on.* A market is the dealing between buyers and sellers of a good, and a competitive market is one in which no one can set the price alone. *You should now be able to say:* "A market need not be a place."

## Equilibrium Price and Quantity

> This is the main idea of the chapter, and it repays slow reading.

@! Go slowly here.

The meeting of demand and supply is the central idea of this chapter. It can be seen in three ways: in a table, on a graph and in algebra. All three give the same answer, and each helps to understand the other two.

### Balance in a Table

> Where the quantity wanted equals the quantity offered, the market balances.

Take the neighbourhood bun market of the case file. Demand is Qd = 540 − 30P a day (Chapter IV) and supply is Qs = 30P − 180 (Chapter VII). The table sets them side by side. Read a row: at Tk 10, buyers want 240 buns and sellers offer 120. The last column subtracts quantity supplied from quantity demanded.

| 6 | 360 | 0 | 360 |
| 8 | 300 | 60 | 240 |
| 10 | 240 | 120 | 120 |
| 12 | 180 | 180 | 0 |

| 16 | 60 | 300 | −240 |
| 18 | 0 | 360 | −360 |

> Only at Tk 12 do the two quantities match.

@ Market equilibrium | A state in which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
@ Equilibrium price | The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

Look at the last column. It is positive when the price is low, negative when the price is high, and zero at Tk 12. At that price buyers want 180 buns and sellers offer 180. We say that the market is in **market equilibrium**, which means a state in which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. You met the word *equilibrium* in Chapter III, where a buyer had no reason to change her purchases. The meaning is the same here: nobody has a reason to change. The price at which it happens, Tk 12, is the **equilibrium price**, and the quantity, 180, is the equilibrium quantity.

### Balance on a Graph

> On a graph, equilibrium is the point where the two curves cross.

Now draw both curves on one graph, with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. The demand curve slopes down and the supply curve slopes up, so they must cross, and they cross once. In Figure 11 the crossing is at E, with price Tk 12 and quantity 180. Every other price gives a gap between the curves. At Tk 10 the supply curve is to the left of the demand curve, and at Tk 16 it is to the right. The gaps are the quantities in the last column of the table.

### Balance in Algebra

> To find the price algebraically, set Qd equal to Qs and solve.

The graph and the table suggest a method. At equilibrium, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so we put the two rules equal and solve for the price. Then we put that price into either rule to find the quantity. The first example uses very small numbers, so that the method is the only new thing.

> First example: small clean numbers.

*Example 1.* *Given:* demand Qd = 10 − P and supply Qs = 2P − 2. *Find:* the equilibrium price and quantity. *Formula:* equilibrium where Qd = Qs. *Steps.* Set 10 − P = 2P − 2. Add P to both sides: 10 = 3P − 2. Add 2: 12 = 3P. Divide by 3: P = 4. Quantity: Qd = 10 − 4 = 6, and Qs = 2 × 4 − 2 = 6. *Answer:* equilibrium price 4 and quantity 6. *Check:* both rules give 6 at a price of 4, so the two sides match.

> Second example: the bun market.

*Example 2.* *Given:* Qd = 540 − 30P and Qs = 30P − 180. *Find:* the equilibrium price and quantity. *Steps.* Set 540 − 30P = 30P − 180. Add 30P to both sides: 540 = 60P − 180. Add 180: 720 = 60P. Divide by 60: P = 12. Quantity: Qd = 540 − 30 × 12 = 540 − 360 = 180, and Qs = 30 × 12 − 180 = 180. *Answer:* the price is Tk 12 and the quantity is 180 buns. *Check:* the table shows a gap of zero at Tk 12, and the graph shows the crossing at E.

> Common slips: the wrong equation, and the idea that equilibrium means fair.

*Common slips.* Do not set price equal to quantity. Set one quantity rule equal to the other. Do not forget to find the quantity after the price. Do not take "equilibrium" to mean that the price is fair or that everyone is pleased. It means only that the quantities match and nobody can gain by changing the price on his or her own.

> A test of the balance idea.

*Pause and check.* In the table, at what price would buyers want 120 buns, and how many would sellers offer at that price? What does the gap tell you? The answer is at the back of the book.

> Equilibrium is the price at which Qd equals Qs; solve by setting the rules equal.

*Before moving on.* The equilibrium price is where the two curves cross, and where Qd = Qs. *You should now be able to say:* "At the equilibrium price, the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer."

## Forces Above and Below Equilibrium

> What happens at a wrong price, and why the price moves toward Tk 12.

@! Go slowly here.

The equilibrium price is the one the market tends toward, but a market may begin at any price. Suppose the bun price is Tk 10 on a certain morning. Buyers want 240 buns and bakeries offer 120. There is a gap of 120.

@ Excess demand | The quantity demanded is greater than the quantity supplied at the price.
@ Excess supply | The quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded at the price.

When the quantity demanded is greater than the quantity supplied, there is **excess demand**. When the quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded, there is **excess supply**. At Tk 10 there is excess demand of 120 buns. At Tk 16 buyers want 60 and sellers offer 300, so there is excess supply of 240 buns. This book uses these two words for a market imbalance. It keeps the word *surplus* for the gains of buyers and sellers, which Section 8.5 describes.

> Excess demand pushes the price up, step by step.

Follow the market from Tk 10. First, the bakeries sell out early, and some customers go home without buns. Second, some customers offer a little more to be served, and bakeries notice that they can raise the price without losing sales. Third, the price rises. Fourth, as it rises, buyers want fewer buns and bakeries offer more, so the gap narrows. The process continues until the price reaches Tk 12, where the gap is zero and nothing pushes the price further.

> Excess supply pushes the price down.

At Tk 16 the story runs the other way. Bakeries find unsold buns at the end of the day. They cut prices to sell them. As the price falls, buyers want more and bakeries offer fewer, so the gap narrows until the price is Tk 12. Whether the price starts too high or too low, the market moves toward the equilibrium.

@ Price mechanism | The way changes in price bring quantity demanded and supplied into balance.

This self-correcting process is the **price mechanism**, which means the way changes in price bring the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied into balance. It also answers the opening puzzle. The tomato sellers did not need an order. Excess supply of tomatoes pushed the price down, and the lower price drew in the buyers.

:case Case file, illustrative. At a bun price of Tk 10, quantity demanded is 240 and quantity supplied is 120, so there is excess demand of 120. At Tk 16, quantity demanded is 60 and quantity supplied is 300, so there is excess supply of 240.

> A government may hold the price away from equilibrium, with predictable results.

@ Price ceiling | The highest price allowed by law.
@ Price floor | The lowest price allowed by law.

Sometimes the government does not want the market to find its own price. A **price ceiling** is the highest price that the law allows. A **price floor** is the lowest price that the law allows. Suppose a ceiling of Tk 10 is placed on buns. The ceiling is below the equilibrium price, so it binds. At Tk 10, sellers offer only 120 buns, though buyers want 240. The quantity traded is the smaller of the two, 120, which is less than the 180 that would have been traded without the ceiling. Excess demand of 120 remains. In practice it leads to queues, to sales to favoured customers, and to illegal sales at higher prices. Sellers may also cut quality. A ceiling set above the equilibrium price, such as Tk 14, changes nothing, because the market price of Tk 12 is already below it.

> A floor above equilibrium leaves goods unsold.

A floor works the other way. A floor of Tk 16 is above the equilibrium price. Buyers want only 60 buns at that price, and sellers offer 300. Excess supply of 240 remains, and only 60 are sold. Governments sometimes set floors to help sellers, such as farmers or workers. The benefit goes only to those who sell, and some sellers cannot find buyers at all.

> The price mechanism restores balance; controls prevent it.

*Before moving on.* Excess demand raises the price and excess supply lowers it, until the market balances. A ceiling or a floor that binds stops this. *You should now be able to say:* "A price ceiling below the equilibrium price leaves excess demand, and the quantity traded is the quantity supplied."

## Shifts and New Equilibria

> When demand or supply shifts, the equilibrium moves to a new place.

Chapters IV and VII showed that determinants shift the curves. When a curve shifts, the crossing point moves, and the market finds a new equilibrium. Three steps find the new equilibrium on a graph. First, decide which curve is affected. Second, decide whether it moves right or left. Third, read the new crossing.

> A rise in the cost of flour moves supply left, raising the price and cutting the quantity.

Take flour first. A rise in the price of flour adds Tk 2 to the cost of each bun, and supply shifts left, as in Chapter VII. The new supply rule is Qs = 30P − 240. Demand is unchanged. Set 540 − 30P = 30P − 240. Then 780 = 60P, so P = 13. The quantity is 540 − 30 × 13 = 150, and the supply rule agrees: 30 × 13 − 240 = 150. The price rises from Tk 12 to Tk 13, and the quantity falls from 180 to 150. Buyers pay Tk 1 more of the Tk 2 cost rise, and the bakeries absorb the rest.

> A rise in demand moves demand right, raising both price and quantity.

Now take a festival. In Chapter IV, demand rose by 60 buns at every price, to Qd = 600 − 30P. Supply is unchanged at Qs = 30P − 180. Set 600 − 30P = 30P − 180. Then 780 = 60P and P = 13. The quantity is 600 − 390 = 210. Both the price and the quantity rise. Notice that the price is Tk 13 in both stories, but the quantity is 150 in one and 210 in the other. The direction of the quantity tells us which curve moved.

:case Case file, illustrative. After the flour price rise, supply is Qs = 30P − 30 × 8, that is 30P − 240, and the new equilibrium is Tk 13 and 150 buns. After the festival rise in demand, to Qd = 600 − 30P, the equilibrium is Tk 13 and 210 buns.

> The table gives the four cases and one bun example for each.

The table gives the four cases. Read a row: if demand rises, price and quantity both rise, and the bun market moves to Tk 13 and 210. If supply rises, the price falls but the quantity rises. Check the last row against the flour example above.

> When both curves shift, one of price or quantity is certain, but not both.

What if both curves shift at once? Then one of the two results is certain and the other depends on the sizes. If demand rises (to 600 − 30P) and supply falls (to 30P − 240), the price rises for certain, to Tk 14. The quantity stays at 180, but it could rise or fall with other sizes. If demand rises (to 600 − 30P) and supply rises (to 30P − 120), the quantity rises for certain, to 240. The price stays at Tk 12, but it could rise or fall with other sizes. Check each case by solving the two rules.

> Third example: a shift of supply.

*Example 3.* *Given:* demand Qd = 540 − 30P, and supply after a rise in flour prices Qs = 30P − 240. *Find:* the new equilibrium. *Steps.* Set 540 − 30P = 30P − 240, so 780 = 60P, and P = 13. Then Q = 540 − 30 × 13 = 150. *Answer:* Tk 13 and 150 buns. *Check:* the supply rule gives 30 × 13 − 240 = 150, which equals the demand quantity.

> A shift moves the crossing point; read the new one.

*Before moving on.* A rise in demand lifts price and quantity, a fall in supply lifts the price and lowers the quantity, and so on. *You should now be able to say:* "To find the effect of a change, decide which curve shifts, in which direction, and read the new crossing."

## Gains From Trade

> A trade benefits both sides, and the gains can be measured.

@! Go slowly here.

Why do buyers and sellers trade? Because both gain. Chapter VI measured the buyer's gain as consumers' surplus. The seller's gain has a matching measure.

@ Producers' surplus | The price a seller receives minus the lowest price the seller would accept.

**Producers' surplus** means the price a seller receives minus the lowest price the seller would have accepted. The lowest price for each unit is read from the supply curve, because it shows the least that will bring that unit to market. In the bun market, sellers will offer nothing below Tk 6, so the first bun would have been sold for as little as Tk 6. At the market price of Tk 12, a seller of that first bun gains Tk 6 above the minimum. Added over all units, the gains form a triangle between the price line and the supply curve.

> The two triangles at Tk 12 each have an area of Tk 540.

At the equilibrium, 180 buns are sold. Consumers' surplus is the triangle below the demand curve and above the price. Its base is 180 and its height is 18 − 12 = 6, so its area is ½ × 180 × 6 = Tk 540. Producers' surplus is the triangle above the supply curve and below the price. Its base is 180 and its height is 12 − 6 = 6, so its area is ½ × 180 × 6 = Tk 540. Figure 13 shows both.

:fig The gains from trade in the bun market. Consumers' surplus (above the price) and producers' surplus (below the price) are each Tk 540 a day. | fig_gains.svg

@ Total surplus | Consumers' surplus plus producers' surplus.

The sum of the two is the **total surplus**, which means consumers' surplus plus producers' surplus. Here it is 540 + 540 = Tk 1,080 a day. This is the total gain of the market from the trade of 180 buns. Any change in the market that reduces the total surplus is a loss to society, even if some people gain.

:case Case file, illustrative. At the equilibrium, consumers' surplus is Tk 540 a day, producers' surplus is Tk 540, and total surplus is Tk 1,080.

> Surplus lets us measure what a tax does.

The two surpluses help to judge public policy. Take a tax of Tk 3 on every bun sold, paid by the bakeries. Supply becomes Qs = 30(P − 3) − 180 = 30P − 270. This is the fourth example.

> Fourth example: a tax on buns.

*Example 4.* *Given:* Qd = 540 − 30P, and after a Tk 3 tax Qs = 30P − 270. *Find:* the new equilibrium, and the change in the surpluses. *Steps.* Set 540 − 30P = 30P − 270. Then 810 = 60P, so P = 13.5 and Q = 540 − 405 = 135. Buyers pay Tk 13.50. Bakeries keep 13.50 − 3 = Tk 10.50. Tax revenue = 3 × 135 = Tk 405. Consumers' surplus = ½ × 135 × (18 − 13.5) = ½ × 135 × 4.5 = Tk 303.75. Producers' surplus = ½ × 135 × (10.5 − 6) = Tk 303.75. *Answer:* total surplus plus tax revenue is 303.75 + 303.75 + 405 = Tk 1,012.50, against Tk 1,080 before. *Check:* the loss is 1,080 − 1,012.50 = Tk 67.50, which equals ½ × 3 × 45, where 45 is the fall in quantity from 180 to 135. Chapter XIII gives this loss a name.

> A tax shifts the price by less than the tax, and both sides pay.

The result shows that the buyers pay Tk 1.50 more (13.50 − 12) and the bakeries receive Tk 1.50 less (12 − 10.50). Both share the tax, though the law taxed only the bakeries. A subsidy works in the opposite way: it lowers the cost, shifts supply right, and raises the surpluses of both sides, though the government pays for it. Trade between countries can be judged the same way, by asking whether the gains to buyers and sellers outweigh the losses to others.

> Two surpluses add to a total, and policy is judged by its effect on the total.

*Before moving on.* Consumers' surplus plus producers' surplus makes the total surplus, and a tax or a control reduces it. *You should now be able to say:* "Producers' surplus is what a seller gains above the lowest price the seller would accept."

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

A market is the dealing of buyers and sellers of a good. In a competitive market no one can set the price alone. Market equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, found in a table, on a graph or by solving Qd = Qs. For the bun market it is Tk 12 and 180 buns. Excess demand pushes the price up and excess supply pushes it down, which is the price mechanism. A price ceiling below equilibrium leaves excess demand, and a floor above it leaves excess supply. A shift of demand or supply moves the equilibrium: a rise in demand lifts price and quantity, and a fall in supply lifts the price and lowers the quantity. Consumers' surplus and producers' surplus add to total surplus, which a tax reduces.

## Where People Go Wrong

> A movement and a shift can be confused.

Students often say that the price rise caused "a fall in demand". A price rise caused by a fall in supply moves us up the demand curve, and demand itself has not changed.

> Equilibrium is not fairness, and it is not permanent.

Another mistake is to think that equilibrium means a fair price or one that lasts. It is only the price at which the quantities match, and it moves whenever a determinant changes.

> A ceiling that does not bind does nothing.

A third mistake is to think that every ceiling lowers the price. A ceiling above the equilibrium price has no effect.

## In History

> American petrol queues in 1973-74 showed the cost of a binding ceiling.

In 1971 the United States government placed controls on prices, and later extended limits on the price of petrol. After October 1973, when oil supplies were cut and world prices rose sharply, the controlled price of petrol stayed below the level at which demand matched supply. In the winter of 1973-74 drivers queued for hours at filling stations, and some stations ran out of fuel. Some states allowed purchases only on alternate days, depending on the last digit of the number plate. The episode is a standard example of excess demand under a ceiling.

## Sources

> Marshall's picture of demand and supply as two blades of a pair of scissors.

Alfred Marshall's *Principles of Economics* (1890) set out the equilibrium of demand and supply. He compared the two to the blades of a pair of scissors: it is idle to ask which blade cuts the paper, because both do. The comparison explains why a price depends on both buyers and sellers.
