:chapter IX | Production
:mood How do flour, fire and labour become bread?
:desc This chapter follows inputs into a workshop and output out of it. It shows why each extra worker may add less than the one before, and what happens when a firm grows all its inputs together. It ends with the choice of the cheapest mix of inputs.

> A puzzle: more bakers bring more bread, until they do not.

The bakery has one oven and one small kitchen. At first the owner works with a single baker, and the loaves come slowly. A second baker arrives and the work speeds up, because one can shape the dough while the other watches the oven. A third baker helps again. Then the kitchen fills. Bakers wait for the oven and block each other's way to the trays. By the seventh baker, no extra bread is made at all. Yet every baker is paid the same wage each day. Why does more labour first help and then stop helping? And what would change if the owner bought a second oven as well? This chapter answers both questions, and in doing so it explains how any firm turns inputs into output.

> What you should know before reading on.

*You will need:* the four kinds of resource from Chapter I (land, labour, capital, entrepreneur); the idea of the margin from Chapter III; and the words short run and long run, which Chapter VII previewed. This chapter teaches those two words in full.

## Production and the Production Function

@! Go slowly here.
> Start at home: inputs go into a kitchen and a meal comes out.

Think of cooking rice at home. You use rice, water, fuel, a pot and your own time. At the end there is a meal. The things you used are the inputs, and the meal is the output. Nobody calls a home kitchen a factory, yet the process is the same one that a bakery follows, only smaller. Keep this picture in mind, because every term in this section can be read in the kitchen first.

> Production means making goods and services.

@ Production | Making goods and services that satisfy wants.
Economists give the name **production** to any activity that makes goods or services that satisfy wants. A baker who bakes bread produces a good. A bus driver who carries passengers produces a service. Moving, storing and selling a good also count as production, because each of them makes the good more useful to the buyer.

> Inputs go in; output comes out.

@ Input | Any resource used to make a good or service.
@ Output | The goods and services that production makes.
An **input** is any resource used in production. Chapter I sorted resources into land, labour, capital and entrepreneur. For the bakery, the flour, the oven, the baker's work and the owner's planning are all inputs. The **output** is what comes out. For the bakery, it is loaves. We write output as a number of units in a period, such as 120 loaves a day.

> Fixed inputs cannot change quickly; variable inputs can.

@ Fixed input | An input whose amount cannot be changed in the period studied.
@ Variable input | An input whose amount can be changed in the period studied.
Some inputs are easy to change and some are not. A baker can be hired next week. An oven cannot be built next week. A **fixed input** is an input whose amount cannot be changed in the period we are studying. The oven is fixed. A **variable input** is an input whose amount can be changed in that period. The number of bakers is variable. Notice that "fixed" describes a period of time, not the nature of the thing. Over a longer time, the oven too can be replaced.

> The short run keeps something fixed; the long run does not.

@ Short run | The period in which at least one input is fixed.
@ Long run | The period in which every input can be changed.
This idea gives us two periods. The **short run** is the period in which at least one input is fixed. The **long run** is the period in which every input can be changed. These names do not count days or months. For a bakery the long run may be a few weeks, since it can install another oven in that time. For a steel mill it may be many years. Sections 9.2 and 9.3 are about the short run. Section 9.4 is about the long run.

> The production function links inputs to the greatest output.

@ Production function | The link between amounts of inputs and the greatest output they can make.
We now need one rule that joins the two sides. The **production function** is the link between the amounts of inputs used and the greatest output they can make. In words: output depends on inputs. In symbols, Q = f(L, K). Here Q is output, L is labour, K is capital, and f means "depends on". A table that lists the loaves made by one baker, two bakers, three bakers and so on, with one oven, is a production function in its plainest form. The words "greatest output" matter. The function assumes that the firm uses its inputs well and with the methods it knows.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Production turns inputs into output. Some inputs are fixed and some are variable. In the short run at least one input is fixed; in the long run all can change. The production function links inputs to output.

> A check on fixed and variable inputs.

*Pause and check.* A tailor owns two sewing machines and can hire more tailors at short notice. Which input is fixed, and which is variable? Name one change that she could make only in the long run.

> The sentence to test yourself against.

*You should now be able to say:* production is making goods and services from inputs, and the production function shows the most output that each amount of inputs can make.

## Product in the Short Run

> Three ways to measure the output of a variable input.

@ Total product | All the output made with a given amount of the variable input.
@ Average product | Output per unit of the variable input.
@ Marginal product | The extra output from one more unit of the variable input.
To study the short run, we measure output in three ways. The **total product** is all the output made with a given number of workers. The **average product** is the output per worker. The **marginal product** is the extra output made when one more worker is hired. "Marginal" means "at the edge", as it did for utility in Chapter III: it looks at the last unit added, not at the whole amount.

> The three measures written as formulas.

In words: average product equals total product divided by the number of workers. Marginal product equals the new total product minus the old total product. In symbols, AP = TP ÷ L, and MP = change in TP ÷ change in L, where TP is total product. When we add one worker at a time, the change in L is 1, so MP equals the change in TP.

> The bakery's output with one oven, from one to seven bakers.

:case Case file, illustrative. Step 9. The bakery has one oven, which is fixed in the short run, and it hires bakers, which are variable. With one to seven bakers, the loaves made per day are 20, 50, 90, 120, 140, 150 and 150.

> A worked example: setting out the problem.

*Given.* One oven. Total product for one to seven bakers: 20, 50, 90, 120, 140, 150, 150. *Find.* The average product and the marginal product at each number of bakers. *Formula.* AP = TP ÷ L; MP = TP now minus TP before.

> A worked example: the arithmetic.

*Steps.* Take three bakers first. AP = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 loaves per baker. MP = 90 − 50 = 40 loaves. For the first baker, "TP before" is zero, because no baker means no loaves: MP = 20 − 0 = 20. For four bakers, AP = 120 ÷ 4 = 30 and MP = 120 − 90 = 30. We repeat the same two lines for every row. The table shows the result. We have written the average product to one decimal place.

:table Product of the bakery's kitchen, per day, one oven
| Bakers | Total product | Average product | Marginal product |
| 1 | 20 | 20.0 | 20 |
| 2 | 50 | 25.0 | 30 |
| 3 | 90 | 30.0 | 40 |
| 4 | 120 | 30.0 | 30 |
| 5 | 140 | 28.0 | 20 |
| 6 | 150 | 25.0 | 10 |
| 7 | 150 | 21.4 | 0 |

> The answer and a test of it.

*Answer.* The marginal product rises to 40 with the third baker, then falls to zero with the seventh. The average product rises to 30, then falls. *Check.* The marginal products must add up to the total product. For three bakers, 20 + 30 + 40 = 90, which matches the table. For seven bakers, 20 + 30 + 40 + 30 + 20 + 10 + 0 = 150.

> Marginal and average product follow a fixed pattern.

The table shows three rules. First, while marginal product is above zero, total product rises. At the seventh baker, marginal product is zero, so total product stops rising. Second, marginal product pulls the average with it. Think of your scores in a class. A new score above your average raises the average, and a new score below it lowers the average. The third baker adds 40 loaves, which is above the old average of 25, so the average rises to 30. The fifth baker adds 20, below the old average of 30, so the average falls to 28. Third, when the new unit adds exactly the old average, the average does not change. The fourth baker adds 30, the same as the old average of 30, so the average stays at its highest point. Marginal product therefore cuts the average product curve at the top of that curve.

:fig The bakery's product: total product above; marginal and average product below. Marginal product cuts average product at its highest point. | fig_product.svg

> Three slips to avoid.

*Common slips.* Do not find marginal product by dividing total product by the number of workers; that gives the average. Do not forget that the first marginal product is measured from zero. Do not read marginal product as the output of the whole group: it belongs to the last worker added.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Total product is all the output. Average product is output per worker. Marginal product is the extra output of the last worker. When marginal product is above average product, the average rises; when it is below, the average falls.

> A check on the arithmetic.

*Pause and check.* A garage has one workshop, and two mechanics repair 6 cars a week. With three mechanics the garage repairs 10. What is the marginal product of the third mechanic, and what is the average product of three?

## The Law of Diminishing Returns

@! Go slowly here.
> A farmer's plot shows the problem in a different setting.

Picture a farmer with one acre of land. One worker can plant, weed and water only part of it, so the land is poorly tended. A second worker lets every task be done on time, and the harvest rises sharply. A third helps again. By the fifth worker, every task is done, and the land can use no more care. The sixth worker mostly stands and watches. The land, which is the fixed input, has become the limit. The bakery's oven plays the same part in the table above. This pattern is so regular that economists state it as a law.

> The law in one plain statement.

@ Law of diminishing returns | Beyond some point, each extra unit of a variable input adds less output than the one before.
The **law of diminishing returns** says this: when more and more of a variable input is added to a fixed input, the extra output from each added unit becomes smaller beyond some point. It is a statement of tendency, as Chapter II explained for every economic law. Look at the bakery's marginal product: 20, 30, 40, then 30, 20, 10, 0. The marginal product rises for the first three bakers, because each one can specialise on a task. From the fourth baker onward, it falls, because the oven and the kitchen limit what more bakers can do.^1

> If the law did not hold, one small plot could feed the world.

Why must the law be true? Suppose it were false, and each extra worker always added as much as the one before. Then a farmer could keep adding workers to one flowerpot and grow all the world's wheat there. Nobody has ever seen this. So the extra output must shrink, because the fixed input has to be shared among more and more units of the variable input.

> The law needs four conditions.

The law is true only under conditions. First, at least one input is fixed, so we are in the short run. Second, the units of the variable input are alike: the seventh baker is as skilled as the first. Third, the methods of production stay the same. Fourth, we change one input at a time and keep the others unchanged. If any condition fails, the law is not tested by the table. A new oven, for instance, would end the short run and bring us to the long run.

> Production passes through three stages as workers are added.

We can divide production into three stages. The first stage runs while average product is still rising. The second stage runs from the top of average product until marginal product reaches zero. The third stage is where marginal product is below zero and total product falls. The table introduces the stages for the bakery. We have not tabulated a stage three, because the case file stops at seven bakers; it would begin if an eighth baker made the kitchen so crowded that output fell.

:table Stages of production in the bakery
| Stage | What happens | Bakery |
| One | Average product rises | One to four bakers |
| Two | Marginal product falls to zero; total product still rises | Four to seven bakers |
| Three | Marginal product is negative; total product falls | More than seven bakers |

> A sensible firm works in stage two.

A sensible firm avoids stage one and stage three. In stage one, more workers still raise output per worker, so the firm should not stop. In stage three, extra workers reduce output, so the firm should never hire them. The firm therefore works in stage two. Where in stage two depends on the wage and on the price of bread. We return to that choice in Chapter XV. For now, note that the seventh baker adds nothing but costs Tk 600, so no owner would hire the seventh.

> What the law does not say, and why it matters.

The law does not say that total output falls; it says that the *increase* in output falls. It does not forbid growth: a better method or a better seed lifts the whole production function, as the history at the end of this chapter shows. And it matters for later chapters. Because each extra worker adds less, each extra loaf costs more to make. That rising cost, studied in Chapter X, is why firms in the short run supply more only at higher prices, as Chapter VII found.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Beyond a point, each extra unit of a variable input adds less output. The law holds when one input is fixed, units are alike and methods do not change. A firm works in stage two.

> A check on the law.

*Pause and check.* In the bakery, which baker is the first to add less than the baker before? Why is the sixth baker still worth considering, even though the marginal product is low?

> The sentence to test yourself against.

*You should now be able to say:* when a fixed input is shared among more and more of a variable input, each extra unit adds less output, and the law needs the short run and unchanged methods.

## Returns to Scale

> The long run lets every input change together.

In the long run, no input is fixed, so the owner can add ovens and bakers together. A new question arises: if all inputs double, does output double? Adam Smith (1776) described a small pin workshop in which ten workers, each given one or two of the separate tasks, made far more pins in a day than the same ten could make working alone.^2 His example introduces a cause of what follows.

> Splitting a job into tasks makes workers faster.

@ Division of labour | Splitting a job into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
**Division of labour** means splitting a job into separate tasks, each done by a different worker. In a larger bakery one person mixes, one shapes, one bakes and one packs. Each becomes quick at one task, and little time is lost moving between tasks. This is possible only when the workforce is large enough to share the tasks.

> Scale means the size of all inputs together.

@ Scale of production | The size of the firm, with all its inputs taken together.
@ Returns to scale | The change in output when all inputs change by the same proportion.
The **scale of production** is the size of the firm, with all its inputs taken together. **Returns to scale** means the change in output when all inputs change by the same proportion. If the owner doubles ovens and bakers, inputs rise by 100 per cent. Returns to scale ask by how much per cent output rises.

> Three cases: output rises faster, equally, or more slowly.

@ Increasing returns to scale | Output rises by a larger proportion than inputs.
@ Constant returns to scale | Output rises by the same proportion as inputs.
@ Decreasing returns to scale | Output rises by a smaller proportion than inputs.
There are three cases. With **increasing returns to scale**, output rises by a larger proportion than inputs. With **constant returns to scale**, output rises by the same proportion as inputs. With **decreasing returns to scale**, output rises by a smaller proportion than inputs. A firm can pass through all three as it grows.

> The case file's plan for a larger bakery.

:case Case file, illustrative. Step 9, long run. A plan to enlarge the bakery adds ovens and bakers together, one oven for every two bakers. The daily output at one to five ovens is 50, 110, 180, 240 and 280 loaves. The first row agrees with the short-run table: one oven and two bakers make 50 loaves.

> A worked example: setting out the problem.

*Given.* The output at scale 1 to 5 above. *Find.* The kind of returns to scale at each step. *Formula.* Per cent change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100, for inputs and for output. Compare the two.

> A worked example: the arithmetic.

*Steps.* From scale 1 to 2, inputs rise by (2 − 1) ÷ 1 × 100 = 100 per cent. Output rises by (110 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = 120 per cent. Output rises faster: increasing returns. From 2 to 3, inputs rise 50 per cent and output (180 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100 = 63.6 per cent: increasing. From 3 to 4, inputs rise 33.3 per cent and output (240 − 180) ÷ 180 × 100 = 33.3 per cent: constant. From 4 to 5, inputs rise 25 per cent and output (280 − 240) ÷ 240 × 100 = 16.7 per cent: decreasing.

:table Returns to scale in the bakery's plan
| Scale (ovens) | Output per day | Rise in inputs | Rise in output | Returns to scale |
| 1 | 50 | — | — | — |
| 2 | 110 | 100.0% | 120.0% | Increasing |
| 3 | 180 | 50.0% | 63.6% | Increasing |
| 4 | 240 | 33.3% | 33.3% | Constant |
| 5 | 280 | 25.0% | 16.7% | Decreasing |

> The answer, a check, and the causes behind each stage.

*Answer.* Returns increase up to three ovens, are constant from three to four, and decrease after four. *Check.* Output per scale unit (output ÷ ovens) is 50, 55, 60, 60 and 56. It rises, stays level, then falls, which agrees with the verdicts. The causes differ. Increasing returns come from division of labour and from machines that work well only when large. Constant returns arise when the firm can repeat what it already does. Decreasing returns arise when a larger firm is harder to manage, and instructions travel slowly and are less clear.

> Two ideas that are often confused.

Returns to scale and diminishing returns answer different questions. The comparison below shows how. Read each row across: the left column gives the feature, and the other two columns say how each idea treats it.

:table Diminishing returns and returns to scale
| Feature | Diminishing returns | Returns to scale |
| Period | Short run | Long run |
| Inputs changed | One input; others fixed | All inputs together |
| What is measured | Marginal product of the added unit | Total output against total inputs |
| Main cause | A fixed input is shared more thinly | Division of labour; later, management strain |

> Three slips to avoid.

*Common slips.* Do not call a firm's falling marginal product "decreasing returns to scale"; that is diminishing returns. Do not change only the number of bakers and call it a change of scale. A firm can show increasing returns to scale and diminishing returns together, because they belong to different periods.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Returns to scale compare the rise in output with the rise in all inputs together. They may increase, stay constant or decrease. Diminishing returns concern one input in the short run.

## Least-Cost Combination

@! Advanced section; you may defer it.
> Many mixes of inputs can make the same output.

A farmer can grow the same crop with many workers and one power tiller, or with fewer workers and two tillers. Both ways give the same harvest, but they cost different amounts. Which mix is cheapest? This section answers by two lines and a rule. It extends the idea of the budget line from Chapter VI.

> The isoquant shows equal-output mixes; the isocost line shows equal-cost mixes.

@ Isoquant | A line joining all mixes of two inputs that make the same output.
@ Isocost line | A line joining all mixes of two inputs that cost the same money.
An **isoquant** is a line joining all mixes of two inputs that make the same output. The word means "equal quantity". An **isocost line** is a line joining all mixes of two inputs that cost the same money. A bigger budget gives an isocost line farther from the corner. The cheapest mix for a given output lies where the isoquant touches the lowest possible isocost line.

> The cheapest mix has the lowest cost for the chosen output.

@ Least-cost combination | The mix of inputs that makes a given output at the lowest cost.
The **least-cost combination** is the mix of inputs that makes a given output at the lowest cost. We find it by working out the cost of each mix on the isoquant and choosing the smallest.

> A worked example: setting out the problem.

*Given.* A small farm can make one season's rice with these mixes of labour-days and tiller-days: (24, 2), (16, 3), (12, 4), (8, 6), (6, 8). A labour-day costs Tk 500 and a tiller-day costs Tk 1,500. *Find.* The cheapest mix. *Formula.* Cost = labour-days × Tk 500 + tiller-days × Tk 1,500.

> A worked example: the arithmetic and the answer.

*Steps.* The mix (24, 2) costs 12,000 + 3,000 = Tk 15,000. The mix (16, 3) costs 8,000 + 4,500 = Tk 12,500. The mix (12, 4) costs 6,000 + 6,000 = Tk 12,000. The mix (8, 6) costs 4,000 + 9,000 = Tk 13,000. The mix (6, 8) costs 3,000 + 12,000 = Tk 15,000. *Answer.* The cheapest mix is 12 labour-days and 4 tiller-days, at Tk 12,000. The figure shows the isoquant as a smooth curve, and the isocost line for Tk 12,000, which touches it at that mix.

:fig The isoquant touches the lowest isocost line (Tk 12,000) at 12 labour-days and 4 tiller-days. | fig_isoquant.svg

> The rule behind the answer.

@ Equal-ratio rule | At least cost, each input gives the same extra output for each taka spent on it.
*Check.* Moving from (16, 3) to (12, 4) adds one tiller-day, costing Tk 1,500, and saves four labour-days, worth Tk 2,000, so it saves Tk 500. Moving on to (8, 6) adds two tiller-days, costing Tk 3,000, and saves four labour-days, worth Tk 2,000, so it costs Tk 1,000 more. We stop at (12, 4). This is the **equal-ratio rule**: at least cost, the extra output for each taka spent on labour equals the extra output for each taka spent on the tiller. In symbols, MP of labour ÷ price of labour = MP of tiller ÷ price of tiller. If the two ratios differ, the firm moves spending to the input that gives more output per taka. Chapter III used the same idea for the consumer.

> Slips and a recap.

*Common slips.* Do not compare the number of units; compare the cost of each mix. Do not choose the cheapest input alone: the cheapest mix may use a dearer input, if it saves enough of the other. *Before moving on.* The isoquant shows equal output, the isocost line shows equal cost, and the least-cost mix is where they touch.

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

Production turns inputs into output, and the production function links the two. In the short run one input is fixed. Marginal product first rises and then falls, and the law of diminishing returns states the fall. In the long run all inputs change; returns to scale may be increasing, constant or decreasing. A firm finds its cheapest mix of inputs where the isoquant touches the lowest isocost line.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common errors, each corrected.

*Error:* "Diminishing returns means total output falls." *Correction:* only the extra output falls; total output still rises until marginal product is zero. *Error:* "Marginal and average product are the same." *Correction:* marginal is the last unit; average is the total shared by all units. *Error:* "Diminishing returns and decreasing returns to scale are one idea." *Correction:* the first concerns one input in the short run, the second all inputs in the long run. *Error:* "Short run means a few months." *Correction:* it means that at least one input is fixed, however long that lasts.

## In History

> A new seed lifted the whole production function.

In 1966 the International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines released a short-stemmed rice variety called IR8. With enough water and fertiliser, it gave far higher yields from the same land than traditional varieties. This did not break the law of diminishing returns. It moved the production function upward: the same land and labour now made more rice. Later extra fertiliser still added less and less, as the law says. The lesson is that methods are one of the conditions of the law, and new methods can lift output for a long time.

## Sources

> Where the ideas first appeared.

Adam Smith, *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776), opens with the division of labour in pin-making. David Ricardo and Edward West each published essays in 1815 that set out diminishing returns on land, which later writers extended to all inputs.
