:chapter XII | Perfect Competition
:mood When no seller can move the price, what is left to choose?
:desc This chapter follows a firm that must accept the market price. It shows how the firm picks its output, when it should keep producing at a loss and when it should close, how the industry adds up its firms, and how entry and exit bring profit to its normal level in the long run.

> A puzzle: a firm that cannot choose its price still has a hard decision.

Early one morning the bakery learns that the market price of a loaf has fallen. The owner cannot argue with the price, and she cannot change it. She can decide only how many loaves to bake. Should she bake as many as before, bake fewer, or close the shop until the price rises? Each answer costs money. In perfect competition, the output choice is the whole of the firm's decision, so it must be made with care. This chapter gives the rule, tests it as the price falls, and then asks what happens when many firms are drawn into, or driven out of, the market.

> What you should know before reading on.

*You will need:* the profit rule and the price taker from Chapter XI; the cost table of Chapter X (average, average variable and marginal cost); and equilibrium from Chapter III.

## Features

> Five conditions make a market perfectly competitive.

Chapter XI named perfect competition. We now list its conditions, because every result in this chapter depends on them. First, there are very many sellers and buyers, each so small that none can change the price. Second, the product is homogeneous, so buyers do not care which seller supplies it. Third, entry and exit are free: a new firm can join, and an old firm can leave, at any time. Fourth, buyers and sellers know the price asked everywhere. Fifth, inputs can move freely from one use to another.

> Perfect competition asks for more than pure competition.

Pure competition needs only the first three conditions, as Chapter XI said. Perfect competition adds full knowledge and free movement of inputs. If buyers did not know prices, a seller might charge more than rivals and still sell. So full knowledge is what makes the single price possible. No market meets all five conditions, but some come close: markets for grain, jute, vegetables and foreign currency.

> Because of these conditions, each firm is a price taker.

@ Perfectly competitive firm | A firm in perfect competition, which must accept the market price.
@ Industry | All the firms that make the same product.
A **perfectly competitive firm** is a firm in perfect competition, and it must accept the market price. The **industry** means all the firms that make the same product; the bakeries of the neighbourhood are the bread industry. The industry as a whole, through its supply and the buyers' demand, decides the price. The firm then faces this price as a given.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Perfect competition needs many sellers, a homogeneous product, free entry and exit, full knowledge and free movement of inputs. Its firms are price takers. The industry is all the firms that make the product.

## Price Determination

> The price depends on how much time sellers have.

The market price is set by demand and supply, as Chapter VIII showed. But supply depends on how much time sellers have to change their output, as Chapter VII explained. We therefore look at three periods.

> In the market period, supply cannot change.

In the market period, output is already made and cannot be changed. The loaves baked this morning are all that exist. Supply is then a fixed amount, and price depends on demand alone. A strong demand raises the price; a weak demand lowers it. A seller of perishable goods, such as fish, must sell today whatever the price.

> In the short run, firms change variable inputs only.

In the short run, firms can change their variable inputs, but the plant is fixed (Chapter IX). Supply can rise as firms hire more bakers, but only up to the limit of their ovens. Price is set where demand meets this supply. In the long run, firms can change all inputs, and new firms can enter, so supply can change much more. We study that case in section 12.5.

> The firm's demand curve is a horizontal line.

Once the industry has set the price, the single firm faces that price. It can sell any amount at Tk 60, and nothing at Tk 61. So its demand curve is a horizontal line at Tk 60. The firm's marginal revenue equals its price, as Chapter XI showed. The firm's own output is too small to move the industry's price, in the way that one drop of water does not change the level of a lake.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Price is set by the industry. In the market period supply is fixed. In the short run it can rise within the limits of plants. The firm faces a horizontal demand curve at the market price.

## Short-Run Equilibrium of the Firm

@! Go slowly here.
> A firm is in equilibrium when it has no reason to change its output.

@ Short-run equilibrium | The output at which a firm has no reason to change, with its plant fixed.
Chapter III defined equilibrium as a state with no reason to change. The **short-run equilibrium** of a firm is the output at which it has no reason to change its output, with its plant fixed. For a profit-maximising firm, this is where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. For a price taker, marginal revenue equals the price, so the condition is price equals marginal cost. If price were above marginal cost, one more loaf would add profit. If price were below marginal cost, one fewer loaf would add profit.

> Case file: the bakery at a price of Tk 60.

:case Case file, illustrative. Step 12. The loaf market price is Tk 60. The bakery's best output is 140 loaves a day. Its revenue is Tk 8,400, its cost is Tk 6,800, and its profit is Tk 1,600 a day.

> A worked example: setting out the problem.

*Given.* Price Tk 60. Marginal cost of the steps to 20, 50, 90, 120, 140 and 150 loaves: Tk 50, 40, 35, 40, 50 and 80. Total cost: 2,000; 3,200; 4,600; 5,800; 6,800; 7,600. *Find.* The equilibrium output and the profit. *Formula.* Produce while MC is not above P; profit = P × Q − TC.

> A worked example: the arithmetic, the answer and a check.

*Steps.* The steps to 20, 50, 90, 120 and 140 loaves each have MC of Tk 50 or less, which is below the price. The step to 150 has MC of Tk 80, above the price, so the bakery stops at 140. Profit = 60 × 140 − 6,800 = 8,400 − 6,800 = Tk 1,600. *Answer.* 140 loaves, with a profit of Tk 1,600. *Check.* At 120 loaves the profit is 7,200 − 5,800 = Tk 1,400, and at 150 it is 9,000 − 7,600 = Tk 1,400. Both are lower.

> A profit above the normal level is called supernormal.

@ Supernormal profit | Profit above normal profit.
Chapter X said that normal profit is the owner's opportunity cost and counts as a cost. Profit above that level is **supernormal profit**. A firm earns supernormal profit when its revenue covers all its costs, including normal profit, with something left. We return to this in section 12.5, because the profit in the table counts explicit costs only.

> What happens as the price falls.

Now let the price fall. The bakery follows the same rule at each price. At Tk 48, the steps to 120 loaves have MC of Tk 40 or less, but the step to 140 has MC of Tk 50, above the price. So the output is 120 loaves. Revenue is 48 × 120 = Tk 5,760, and cost is Tk 5,800, so the result is a loss of Tk 40. At Tk 40, the output is 120 or 90; revenue is Tk 4,800 against a variable cost of Tk 4,800. At Tk 30, no step has MC below 30 except none: the first step costs Tk 50, so the firm should produce nothing.

> Two key prices: break-even and shut-down.

@ Break-even point | The price at which revenue just covers total cost.
@ Shut-down point | The price at which revenue covers only variable cost.
The **break-even point** is the price at which revenue just covers total cost. For the bakery it is the lowest average cost in the table, about Tk 48, reached at 120 loaves. Below it the firm makes a **loss**, which is revenue below total cost. The **shut-down point** is the price at which revenue covers only variable cost. For the bakery it is the lowest average variable cost, Tk 40. Between Tk 40 and Tk 48, the firm should keep producing, because its revenue covers all its variable cost and part of its fixed cost. Below Tk 40, it should close, because each loaf then adds more to cost than to revenue.

> A worked example: a firm decides between producing and closing.

*Given.* Fixed cost Tk 1,000 a day. *Find.* The loss at Tk 48, Tk 40 and Tk 30, if the bakery produces and if it closes. *Steps.* If it closes, it loses its fixed cost, Tk 1,000, at every price. At Tk 48 it produces 120 loaves and loses Tk 40, which is far less than Tk 1,000. At Tk 40 it loses 4,800 − 5,800 = −Tk 1,000 at 120 loaves, the same as closing. At Tk 30 it would lose more than Tk 1,000 by producing, so it closes. *Answer.* It produces at Tk 48, is indifferent at Tk 40, and closes at Tk 30. *Check.* The rule is to compare the loss when producing with the fixed cost, and choose the smaller.

> The table gives the best output and the result at each price.

:table The bakery's choice at four prices (taka a day)
| Price | Best output | Revenue | Total cost | Profit or loss |
| 60 | 140 | 8,400 | 6,800 | 1,600 |
| 48 | 120 | 5,760 | 5,800 | −40 |
| 40 | 120 | 4,800 | 5,800 | −1,000 |
| 30 | 0 (close) | 0 | 1,000 | −1,000 |

> Three slips to avoid.

*Common slips.* Do not close the firm as soon as it makes a loss: compare the loss with the fixed cost. Do not use average cost for the choice of output; use marginal cost. Do not forget that fixed cost must be paid even when the firm closes, in the short run.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* A price taker is in equilibrium where price equals marginal cost. At the break-even price, revenue covers all cost. At the shut-down price, it covers only variable cost. Between these two prices, the firm produces at a loss; below the shut-down price, it closes.

> The sentence to test yourself against.

*You should now be able to say:* a price taker produces where price equals marginal cost, and in the short run it closes only when the price is below average variable cost.

## Short-Run Equilibrium of the Industry

> The firm's marginal cost curve is its supply curve.

The rule above tells us the output of the firm at each price. It is therefore the firm's supply curve: the part of the marginal cost curve above the shut-down price. At Tk 40, the bakery supplies about 120 loaves; at Tk 50, 140; at Tk 80, 150. For a price between these points, the firm supplies the output of the last point whose marginal cost does not exceed the price. At Tk 60, it supplies 140.

> The industry's supply is the sum of its firms' supplies.

To find the industry supply, add the supplies of all firms at each price. Suppose the industry has 100 firms like the bakery. Then at Tk 40 the industry supplies 12,000 loaves; at Tk 50, 14,000; at Tk 80, 15,000. The table shows the sums. This is the same addition as for market demand in Chapter IV.

:table Supply of one bakery and of an industry of 100 bakeries
| Price (Tk) | One firm (loaves) | Industry (loaves) |
| 40 | 120 | 12,000 |
| 50 | 140 | 14,000 |
| 80 | 150 | 15,000 |

> Industry equilibrium sets the price that the firm takes.

The industry is in short-run equilibrium where industry supply equals industry demand. That gives the price. Each firm then takes that price and applies the rule of section 12.3. If demand is such that the price is Tk 60, each of the 100 bakeries produces 140 loaves, and the industry produces 14,000. If demand falls, the price falls, and the firms move down their marginal cost curves, so the industry output falls. In the short run, the number of firms is fixed, so firms may earn supernormal profit, or make a loss.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* The firm's supply curve is its marginal cost curve above the shut-down price. The industry supply is the sum of its firms' supplies. Demand and industry supply set the price.

## Long-Run Equilibrium

> In the long run, firms may enter or leave.

@ Long-run equilibrium | A state in which no firm wants to enter or leave the industry, and no firm wants to change its plant.
The short run allows no change in the number of firms. In the long run, **entry** is a new firm joining the industry, and **exit** is an old firm leaving it. Entry is free in perfect competition. The **long-run equilibrium** is a state in which no firm wants to enter or leave the industry, and no firm wants to change its plant.

> Supernormal profit draws new firms in.

If firms earn supernormal profit, new firms are attracted. The industry supply increases, because there are more firms. The supply curve moves to the right, so the price falls. Profit falls with it. Entry continues until supernormal profit is zero. If firms make losses, some leave. Industry supply moves to the left, and the price rises. Exit continues until the losses have gone.

> The bakery's profit counts explicit costs only.

Here we must use the cost idea of Chapter X with care. The Tk 1,600 a day of profit counts only explicit costs. The owner's implicit cost is Tk 43,000 a month, which is about Tk 1,433 a day. So the bakery's economic profit at Tk 60 is about 1,600 − 1,433 = Tk 167 a day, which is a small supernormal profit. It is enough to attract entry.

> The price falls until economic profit is zero.

*Given.* Explicit cost per day as in the cost table; implicit cost Tk 1,433 a day. *Find.* The long-run price at which economic profit is zero. *Steps.* Add the implicit cost to total cost at each output, and divide by output. At 120 loaves, (5,800 + 1,433) ÷ 120 = Tk 60.28. At 140 loaves, (6,800 + 1,433) ÷ 140 = Tk 58.81. At 150 loaves, (7,600 + 1,433) ÷ 150 = Tk 60.22. *Answer.* The lowest is Tk 58.81, at 140 loaves, so entry pushes the price down to about Tk 59. *Check.* At Tk 58.81, revenue is 58.81 × 140 = Tk 8,233, which equals the total of explicit and implicit cost.

> The long-run position: price equals the lowest average cost.

In long-run equilibrium, each firm earns only normal profit. The price equals the lowest average cost, counting normal profit as a cost, and it also equals marginal cost, because the firm produces where P = MC. Each firm therefore produces at the lowest cost possible with its plant, and buyers pay the lowest price that covers it. This is why economists value competition: it brings the price to the cost of production.

> Cautions about the result.

Real markets do not always reach this position. Costs and demand keep changing, so the firms are always adjusting. Entry takes time, and some firms are more efficient than others. The result is a tendency, as Chapter II described for every economic law.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Supernormal profit draws entry; losses drive exit. In the long run, price equals the lowest average cost and each firm earns normal profit. Price also equals marginal cost.

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

A perfectly competitive firm takes the price and chooses only its output. In the short run it produces where price equals marginal cost. It closes if the price falls below average variable cost. Its marginal cost curve is its supply curve, and the industry supply is the sum of these. In the long run, entry and exit bring the price down to the lowest average cost, so each firm earns only normal profit.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common errors, each corrected.

*Error:* "A firm in a loss should close at once." *Correction:* in the short run it should continue if revenue covers variable cost. *Error:* "A firm sets its price by adding a profit to its cost." *Correction:* a price taker accepts the price. *Error:* "In the long run, firms earn no profit at all." *Correction:* they earn normal profit, which counts as a cost. *Error:* "A firm should produce where average cost is lowest." *Correction:* it should produce where price equals marginal cost.

## In History

> Grading made a crop a single product.

The Chicago Board of Trade was founded in 1848 as a meeting place for grain merchants. Before long, merchants agreed on standard grades of grain, so that a buyer in one city could purchase grain from a seller in another without seeing it. Once the grain was graded, a bushel of one grade was the same from every seller. Standard grades made grain a homogeneous product, which is one of the conditions of perfect competition. The case shows how a market can be built toward the conditions.
