:chapter XI | Market Structures and the Goal of the Firm
:mood Who sets the price, and what does the seller want?
:desc This chapter sorts markets by the number of sellers, the kind of product and the ease of entry. It introduces revenue, shows how a seller who takes the price differs from one who sets it, and states the rule that a firm follows when it chooses its output.

> A puzzle: one seller cannot choose the price, another can.

The bakery sells a loaf at Tk 60. If the owner asks Tk 65, the neighbours buy from the many other bakeries, and her sales fall to almost nothing. Now think of the only pharmacy on a small island. Its owner can raise the price of a medicine, and the islanders must still buy. Both are sellers, and both want a good profit, yet one must accept the price and the other can choose it. What makes the difference? The answer lies in the shape of the market, and it decides how each firm picks its price and its output. This chapter describes the shapes, measures the money a firm receives, and states the rule for choosing output.

> What you should know before reading on.

*You will need:* the demand curve and the market (Chapters IV and VIII); total revenue and elasticity (Chapter V); and average and marginal cost (Chapter X).

## What Market Structure Means

> A market has features that shape how its sellers behave.

@ Market structure | The features of a market: the sellers, the product and the ease of entry.
Chapter VIII defined a market as buyers and sellers dealing with each other. Markets differ in how they are built. **Market structure** means the features of a market that decide how its sellers behave: how many sellers there are, what kind of product they sell, how easily new sellers can enter, and how much control sellers have over price. We study these features one by one.

> The first feature: the number of sellers.

The first feature is the number of sellers. A town may have thousands of vegetable sellers, or a few large firms may supply all its cement, or one firm may supply its gas. The fewer the sellers, the more each seller's choice affects the price and the rivals.

> The second feature: whether products are alike.

@ Homogeneous product | A product that buyers see as identical from every seller.
@ Differentiated product | A product that buyers see as different from rivals' products.
The second feature is the kind of product. A **homogeneous product** is a product that buyers see as identical from every seller; a kilogram of coarse rice of one grade is an example. A **differentiated product** is a product that buyers see as different from rivals' products, because of brand, quality or place; the milk bun of one bakery is an example. When products are alike, buyers care only about price. When they are different, a buyer may pay more for a favourite.

> The third feature: how easily new sellers can enter.

@ Barrier to entry | Anything that stops new sellers from entering a market.
The third feature is ease of entry. A **barrier to entry** is anything that stops new sellers from entering a market. A law that allows only one licensed seller is a barrier. So is a very large cost of starting, or control of the only source of a key input. Where entry is free, high profits attract new sellers and bring prices down. Where entry is blocked, high profits can last.

> The fourth feature: how far sellers can choose the price.

The fourth feature is control over price. It follows from the other three. Many sellers of a homogeneous product, with free entry, leave each seller no control. One seller behind a barrier has a great deal. Between these two lie the other cases, which the next section names.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Market structure is the number of sellers, the kind of product, the ease of entry and the control over price. A homogeneous product is the same from every seller; a differentiated product is not. A barrier to entry blocks new sellers.

> A check on the four features.

*Pause and check.* A town has one railway station and only one licensed taxi stand. Which of the four features does this describe, and what is the effect on control over price?

## The Main Types

> Four main types arise from the four features.

Combining the features gives four main types. We state each type briefly here. Chapters XII to XIV study them in turn.

> Perfect and pure competition: many sellers, no control over price.

@ Perfect competition | A market with many sellers, one homogeneous product, free entry and full knowledge.
@ Pure competition | Competition with no element of monopoly: many sellers, an identical product and free entry.
**Perfect competition** is a market with many sellers, one homogeneous product, free entry and exit, and full knowledge of prices by buyers and sellers. **Pure competition** is a market with the first three of these only: it has no element of monopoly, but knowledge and the movement of inputs may be imperfect. Pure competition is the wider idea; perfect competition adds the last two conditions.^1 A seller in either market has no control over price. A village rice market with many growers and buyers comes close.

> Monopoly: one seller.

@ Monopoly | A market with one seller of a product that has no close substitute.
**Monopoly** is a market with one seller of a product that has no close substitute. The seller has strong control over price, if entry is blocked. Later chapters name the kinds: a *pure monopoly* is a sole seller with no substitute, and a *bilateral monopoly* is one seller facing one buyer (Chapter XIII).

> Monopolistic competition: many sellers, differing products.

@ Monopolistic competition | A market with many sellers of products that differ from each other.
**Monopolistic competition** is a market with many sellers of products that differ from each other, and with free entry. Restaurants in one town are an example. Each restaurant is the only seller of its own dishes, so it has some control over price, but rivals are close, so the control is small.

> Oligopoly: a few sellers.

@ Oligopoly | A market with only a few sellers.
**Oligopoly** is a market with only a few sellers. Each firm is so large that its price and output change what its rivals do. A market served by three or four large firms, such as the market for mobile phone service, is an example.

> The four types compared in a table.

The table sets the types side by side. Read each row across: the first column names the type, and the others give its features.

:table The main market structures
| Type | Sellers | Product | Entry | Control over price |
| Perfect competition | Very many | Homogeneous | Free | None |
| Monopoly | One | No close substitute | Blocked | Strong |
| Monopolistic competition | Many | Differentiated | Free | Small |
| Oligopoly | Few | Either kind | Hard | Considerable, but rivals reply |

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Perfect competition has many sellers and no control over price. Monopoly has one seller. Monopolistic competition has many sellers with different products. Oligopoly has a few sellers.

## Revenue

> The money a firm receives is the starting point for its choice.

To choose its output, a firm must know how much money it receives. This section measures that money in three ways. You already know the first from Chapter V.

> Total, average and marginal revenue.

@ Average revenue | The revenue received for each unit sold.
@ Marginal revenue | The extra revenue from selling one more unit.
The total revenue is the price times the quantity sold. The **average revenue** is the revenue received for each unit sold; it equals total revenue divided by quantity, which is the price. The **marginal revenue** is the extra revenue from selling one more unit. In symbols, TR = P × Q, AR = TR ÷ Q = P, and MR = change in TR ÷ change in Q.

> A seller who must accept the market price.

@ Price taker | A seller who must accept the price set by the market.
A **price taker** is a seller who must accept the price set by the market. The seller can sell any amount at that price, but nothing at a higher one. For such a seller, each extra loaf brings the same price, so marginal revenue equals price. Average revenue also equals price. The seller's demand curve is a horizontal line.

> The bakery as a price taker.

:case Case file, illustrative. Step 11. The loaf price of Tk 60 is given by the market. For the bakery, average revenue and marginal revenue are both Tk 60 at every output.

> A worked example: setting out the problem.

*Given.* Price Tk 60 a loaf. Outputs of 20, 50, 90, 120, 140 and 150 loaves. *Find.* Total, average and marginal revenue. *Formula.* TR = P × Q; AR = TR ÷ Q; MR = change in TR ÷ change in Q.

> A worked example: the arithmetic, the answer and a check.

*Steps.* At 20 loaves, TR = 60 × 20 = Tk 1,200 and AR = 1,200 ÷ 20 = Tk 60. From 20 to 50 loaves, TR rises from 1,200 to 3,000, so MR = (3,000 − 1,200) ÷ 30 = Tk 60. Repeat for each row. *Answer.* The table shows that AR and MR are both Tk 60 throughout. *Check.* The marginal revenue of one loaf is the price of that loaf, so MR = P.

:table Revenue of the bakery as a price taker (taka)
| Loaves | Price (AR) | Total revenue | Marginal revenue |
| 20 | 60 | 1,200 | 60 |
| 50 | 60 | 3,000 | 60 |
| 90 | 60 | 5,400 | 60 |
| 120 | 60 | 7,200 | 60 |
| 140 | 60 | 8,400 | 60 |
| 150 | 60 | 9,000 | 60 |

> A seller who sets the price.

@ Price maker | A seller who can set the price, within what buyers will pay.
A **price maker** is a seller who can set the price, within what buyers will pay. Such a seller faces a falling demand curve, as Chapter IV found for the market. To sell more, the seller must lower the price. And the lower price applies to every unit, not only the extra one. This is why marginal revenue is below the price.

> The one-seller bun market.

:case Case file, illustrative. Step 11. Suppose one seller served the whole neighbourhood bun market of Chapter IV. Its demand is P = 18 − Q ÷ 30. Then TR = 18Q − Q × Q ÷ 30, and MR = 18 − Q ÷ 15.

> A worked example: setting out the problem.

*Given.* P = 18 − Q ÷ 30. *Find.* Price, total revenue and marginal revenue at 0, 60, 120, 180, 240 and 300 buns. *Formula.* TR = P × Q; MR = change in TR ÷ change in Q.

> A worked example: the arithmetic and the answer.

*Steps.* At 60 buns, P = 18 − 60 ÷ 30 = Tk 16, and TR = 16 × 60 = Tk 960. At 120 buns, P = 14 and TR = 1,680. The step from 60 to 120 adds 1,680 − 960 = Tk 720 over 60 buns, so MR = 720 ÷ 60 = Tk 12. Repeat for each row. *Answer.* The table shows that MR falls faster than price, and is zero from 240 to 300 buns. *Check.* The formula MR = 18 − Q ÷ 15 at the middle of the first step (30 buns) gives 18 − 2 = Tk 16, which equals the first step in the table.

:table Revenue of a one-seller bun market (taka)
| Buns | Price (AR) | Total revenue | Marginal revenue |
| 0 | 18 | 0 | — |
| 60 | 16 | 960 | 16 |
| 120 | 14 | 1,680 | 12 |
| 180 | 12 | 2,160 | 8 |
| 240 | 10 | 2,400 | 4 |
| 300 | 8 | 2,400 | 0 |

> The two sellers compared, and the link to elasticity.

The price taker's marginal revenue stays at the price, but the price maker's falls below the price and can reach zero. Zero marginal revenue is the peak of total revenue, which is at 270 buns and Tk 9, where Chapter V found unit elasticity. So a price maker who wants more revenue stops raising output at the point of unit elasticity.

> Three slips to avoid.

*Common slips.* Do not say that marginal revenue equals price for every seller; it does only for a price taker. Do not forget that a price cut applies to all units. Do not take total revenue as profit: it takes no account of cost.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Total revenue is price times quantity; average revenue is the price; marginal revenue is the extra revenue of one more unit. A price taker has MR equal to price. A price maker has MR below price.

## The Profit Rule and Other Goals

> What a firm wants decides what it does.

@ Profit | Revenue minus cost.
@ Profit maximisation | Choosing output to make profit as large as possible.
**Profit** is revenue minus cost. In symbols, profit = TR − TC. **Profit maximisation** means choosing output to make profit as large as possible. It is the usual goal in the study of the firm, because it gives a clear rule. Chapter X showed that cost can mean accounting cost or economic cost; the table of this chapter uses the cost table of Chapter X, which counts explicit costs.

> Profit can be found row by row.

*Given.* The bakery sells at Tk 60, and the cost table of Chapter X. *Find.* The output with most profit. *Steps.* At 140 loaves, TR = 8,400 and TC = 6,800, so profit = Tk 1,600. At 150 loaves, TR = 9,000 and TC = 7,600, so profit = Tk 1,400. The table shows every row. *Answer.* Profit is largest at 140 loaves. *Check.* Profit rises until 140 and then falls, so 140 is the peak among these outputs.

:table Profit of the bakery at a price of Tk 60 (taka)
| Loaves | Total revenue | Total cost | Profit |
| 20 | 1,200 | 2,000 | −800 |
| 50 | 3,000 | 3,200 | −200 |
| 90 | 5,400 | 4,600 | 800 |
| 120 | 7,200 | 5,800 | 1,400 |
| 140 | 8,400 | 6,800 | 1,600 |
| 150 | 9,000 | 7,600 | 1,400 |

> The rule that gives the same answer without a long table.

The profit rule gives a quicker way. Add one more unit if it brings in more than it costs, that is, if marginal revenue is above marginal cost. Stop when marginal revenue is no longer above marginal cost. The firm's best output is therefore where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, or the last unit that satisfies MR ≥ MC. In the bakery, MR is Tk 60 for every loaf. The marginal costs of the steps in Chapter X are Tk 50, 40, 35, 40, 50 and 80. The step to 140 loaves has MC of Tk 50, below Tk 60, so it adds profit. The step to 150 has MC of Tk 80, above Tk 60, so it reduces profit. The firm stops at 140, as the table shows.

> Why the rule works.

The rule works because each step is a small decision. If the last loaf adds more to revenue than to cost, producing it raises profit. If it adds less, producing it lowers profit. At the best output, no further step can raise profit. Chapter XII uses this rule for a firm in perfect competition, and Chapter XIII for a monopoly.

> Firms may seek other goals.

Some firms seek goals other than profit. A manager paid by the amount sold may prefer a high revenue to a high profit. The one-seller bun market shows the difference. Revenue is largest at 270 buns, where marginal revenue is zero, but profit is largest where marginal revenue equals the higher marginal cost. A firm may also seek growth, to become larger than its rivals, or safety, to survive in a risky market. Berle and Means (1932) noted that in large firms the managers who decide are often not the owners who want profit, which opens the way for such goals. In this book, unless we say otherwise, a firm maximises profit.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Profit is revenue minus cost. The best output is where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. Firms may seek sales, growth or safety, but profit maximisation is our standard goal.

> A check on the profit rule.

*Pause and check.* At 90 loaves the bakery's price is Tk 60 and the marginal cost of the last step is Tk 35. Should it make more? What does the answer say about MR and MC?

## How Structure Shapes Pricing

> The same costs can lead to different prices in different markets.

Suppose four bakeries have the same cost table, but each sells in a different market. Their choices differ, because of what each can do about the price. This section sets out what each type of seller can and cannot do.

> The perfect competitor can choose only output.

In perfect competition, the baker cannot choose the price. She chooses only the output, by the profit rule, and the market sets the price at Tk 60. If she raises her price, she loses all her buyers. If she lowers it, she gives up revenue and gains nothing. Chapter XII follows this firm.

> The monopolist chooses both, but is limited by demand.

A monopolist can choose the price or the output, but not both independently, because buyers decide how much they will take at each price. Choosing a higher price means selling less. The profit rule applies, with marginal revenue below price. The result is a higher price and a smaller output than under competition. Chapter XIII shows this.

> The monopolistic competitor has a little room.

A firm in monopolistic competition can set its own price within a narrow range, because buyers like its product. If its price rises too far, buyers move to the many rivals. The bakery's branded milk bun is an example. Chapter XIV follows it.

> The oligopolist must watch its rivals.

A firm in an oligopoly cannot decide alone. If it cuts its price, rivals may cut theirs, and nobody gains. If it raises its price, rivals may keep theirs, and it loses buyers. Each firm's choice depends on rivals' replies. Chapter XIV also explains this.

> Recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* A price taker chooses only output. A monopolist faces demand and sets price and output together. A monopolistic competitor has a narrow range of price. An oligopolist watches its rivals.

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

Market structure is the number of sellers, the kind of product, the ease of entry and the control over price. The main types are perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly. A price taker has marginal revenue equal to price; a price maker has marginal revenue below price. A profit-maximising firm chooses the output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. Some firms seek other goals.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common errors, each corrected.

*Error:* "All sellers can set the price." *Correction:* only sellers with market power can; a price taker cannot. *Error:* "Marginal revenue always equals price." *Correction:* only for a price taker. *Error:* "Highest revenue means highest profit." *Correction:* profit also depends on cost. *Error:* "A monopoly is a large firm." *Correction:* monopoly means one seller, however large or small.

## In History

> A law was written to protect competition.

By the late nineteenth century, a few large firms in the United States controlled whole industries such as oil and railways. Many people feared that these firms would set prices without rivals. On 2 July 1890, Congress passed the Sherman Act, the first major law against agreements that restrained trade and against monopolising a market. It showed that societies treat market structure as a matter of public concern. Bangladesh also has a competition law, the Competition Act 2012, which is enforced by a Competition Commission. Chapter XIII returns to it.

## Sources

> Where the ideas first appeared.

Edward Chamberlin, *The Theory of Monopolistic Competition* (1933), and Joan Robinson, *The Economics of Imperfect Competition* (1933), separated the cases between perfect competition and monopoly. Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means, *The Modern Corporation and Private Property* (1932), raised the question of who controls the large firm.
