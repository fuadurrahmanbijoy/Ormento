## A Question That Someone Has Already Asked

> A bakery owner meets two problems and wonders whether anyone has solved them before.

Return to the Morning Loaf Bakery in its third month. The owner has learned to see the four functions at work, and has measured productivity. Now two small troubles appear at the same time. Packing bread at the counter bench is slow, and the person packing walks back and forth for bags, labels and string. At the same moment, two people give orders to the counter assistant, and each order is different.

> The owner suspects that these troubles are common, not special to one shop.

The owner could solve each trouble by trial and error. A better way is to ask a question first. Has anyone studied these troubles carefully before? The answer is yes. For more than a century, careful people have studied how work is arranged and how orders flow. Some of them watched workers with a clock. Some of them wrote rules about command. Some of them asked why workers behave as they do. Their ideas did not agree, and the disagreements teach us something.

> This chapter tells that story and tests each idea against the bakery.

In this chapter we follow those ideas in the order in which they appeared. We ask what each school of thought believed, what it got right, and what it missed. We give most space to two landmark writers, Frederick Taylor and Henri Fayol. At the end we ask a harder question: is management a science, an art, or a profession?

> You need two earlier ideas: the four functions, and productivity.

You will need two ideas from Chapter II. The first is the four functions of management: planning, organising, leading and controlling. The second is productivity, which is output divided by input. If either idea feels uncertain, read the matching part of that chapter again before going on.

## Why Ideas About Management Changed

> People managed for thousands of years, but they began to study management only about a century ago.

Kings built pyramids. Merchants ran caravans across deserts. Armies marched in order, and cities collected taxes. All of this needed managers, and the managers learned their work by doing it. For most of history, a young manager learned from an older one, and nobody wrote down a general theory.

> Large factories created problems that old habits could not solve.

Around the year 1800, factories began to replace small workshops in Britain, and the change spread to other countries. A workshop might hold six people who knew each other. A factory held hundreds, working with machines, in rows, for long hours. Suddenly an owner had to ask new questions. How should hundreds of tasks be divided? Who gives orders to whom? How much should each worker produce, and how should the owner know?

@! Go slowly here. This is a new kind of idea: a way of grouping ideas.

@ School of thought | A group of ideas about management that share one main belief.

> A school of thought is a group of writers who share one main belief about how to manage.

Writers tried to answer these questions, and their answers fell into groups. We call each group a **school of thought**. A school of thought is a set of ideas about management that share one main belief. For example, one school believed that the best way to raise output is to study the work. Another believed that the best way is to understand the worker.

> A new school usually appears because the old one left a problem unsolved.

Think of a school timetable as a bridge to this idea. A school may first give every teacher the same number of lessons, because that is easy to arrange. Soon teachers complain that some subjects are harder to teach than others. The school then adjusts the timetable. Later, it finds that students need breaks, and it adjusts again. Each change answers a weakness in the earlier one. The history of management ideas moves in the same way.

> Two cautions: schools overlap, and old ideas do not disappear.

Keep two cautions in mind. First, the schools overlap in time, and their dates are approximate. Second, an old school is not wrong only because a new one follows it. Managers still use time study today, and they still use Fayol's rules. Each school left something of lasting value. The figure below shows the order in which the main schools appeared.^1

> Studying the history of ideas helps a manager choose between them.

Why should a student of management study history at all? There is a practical reason. Every manager meets the same old problems: slow work, unclear orders, unhappy staff, changing markets. Earlier writers have already tried answers to each of them. When you know the history, you do not need to invent a solution from the beginning. You can ask which school met your kind of problem, what it advised, and where it failed. The story that follows is therefore a store of tried methods, and not only a record of the past.

> You should now be able to say why a new school of thought appears.

You should now be able to say this: a school of thought is a group of ideas that share one main belief, and a new school usually appears to answer a weakness in an earlier one.

## Scientific Management

> Taylor believed that the way work is done can be studied and improved, like any other problem.

### The Man and the Problem

> Frederick Taylor was an American engineer who watched factory workers closely.

Frederick Winslow Taylor (1856–1915) was an American engineer. He began as a worker in a steel company and rose to be its chief engineer. Because he had worked on the shop floor, he knew what workers did when nobody watched. What he saw troubled him.

@ Soldiering | Working slower than one could, on purpose, so as not to do more than others.

> Taylor found that workers often worked slowly on purpose.

Taylor saw that many workers worked well below their ability. He called this **soldiering**. Soldiering means working at a slow pace on purpose, so that nobody expects more. The workers had reasons. They feared that if they produced more, the owner would cut the pay for each piece, or that fewer workers would be needed. Managers, for their part, did not know how long a task should take. Neither side had facts.

@ Rule of thumb | A method based on habit and guesswork, not on measurement.

> Rule of thumb means a method learned from habit, not from measurement.

Managers of that time relied on a **rule of thumb**. A rule of thumb is a method based on habit and guesswork, not on measurement. Each worker used a personal method. The manager could not say which method was best, because nobody had compared them. Taylor wanted to replace guesses with facts.

### The Four Principles

@ Scientific management | Studying work by measurement to find the best method, then teaching it.

> Taylor's book of 1911 named four principles for managers.

In 1911 Taylor published *The Principles of Scientific Management*. In it he named an approach that he called science, not guesswork. We call it **scientific management**. Scientific management means studying work with measurement in order to find the one best method, and then teaching that method to every worker.

> The first principle replaces rule of thumb with a method found by study.

Taylor set out four principles, which we state in plain words. The first principle is to develop a science of each task. A manager studies the task, tests different methods, and chooses the best one. The chosen method replaces the rule of thumb.

> The second principle is to select and train workers for the method.

The second principle is to choose workers with care and to train them. Different tasks need different strengths. A manager should match each worker to a task, and then teach the best method. Before Taylor, workers often trained themselves, and trained each other badly.

> The third principle joins the method to the worker through cooperation and pay.

The third principle is cooperation. The manager works with the workers so that the task is done in the way the study found. Taylor wanted workers to follow the method because they gained from it. He therefore linked pay to output. His best known plan paid a higher rate for each piece to the worker who reached the standard, and a lower rate to the worker who did not.

@ Piece rate | Pay that depends on the number of pieces a worker produces.

> A piece rate pays by the number of units a worker makes.

The word **piece rate** needs a short explanation. A piece rate is a payment for each unit that a worker produces. A baker who is paid Tk 2 for each loaf packed is on a piece rate. The more loaves the baker packs, the more the baker earns.

> The fourth principle divides the work fairly between managers and workers.

The fourth principle is a division of duty. Managers take over the planning of work, which workers had done by habit. Workers carry out the work according to the plan. Taylor believed that each side would do what it did best, and that the two sides would share the gain.

### Time and Motion Study

@ Time-and-motion study | Timing a task and examining each movement to remove waste.

> Taylor's main tool was the clock, used with careful method.

To find the best method, Taylor needed facts. His tool was the **time-and-motion study**. A time-and-motion study means timing a task and examining each movement in it, in order to remove movements that waste time.

> A time study measures how long each movement takes.

The study has two parts. In the first part, the observer breaks a task into small movements and times each one with a stopwatch. In the second part, the observer asks whether each movement is needed. Could the worker reach less far? Could a tool be kept closer? Could two movements become one? Frank and Lillian Gilbreth, an American husband and wife, developed this second part and used it to study how workers lay bricks and perform other tasks.^2

^2: Frank Gilbreth (1868–1924) and Lillian Gilbreth (1878–1972) worked on motion study and on fatigue. Their work added the study of movement to Taylor's study of time.

> The packing bench shows how a time-and-motion study works.

Apply this to the bakery's packing bench. The owner watches the person who packs for an afternoon. Each loaf takes 40 seconds to pack. Most of the time is not spent on packing. The packer walks to a shelf for a bag, walks back for a label, and walks again for string. The owner moves bags, labels and string to one place within the packer's reach. The task now takes 30 seconds. We shall return to these figures in the worked case at the end of this chapter.

@! Go slowly here. Notice that the method changed, not the effort.

> The gain came from a better arrangement, not from harder work.

Notice what happened. The person packing did not work harder. That person worked in a better arrangement. This is the central idea of scientific management: much waste hides in the way work is arranged, and a careful study can find it.

> A hospital ward shows that the same method can serve a very different task.

Scientific management is not only for factories. Consider a hospital ward where nurses give medicine to patients each morning. A manager times the round and finds that the nurse walks to the store room many times, because each trip brings the medicine for one patient only. The manager then arranges the medicine for all the patients in a tray before the round begins. The nurse makes one trip and spends the saved time with the patients. The method is the same as at the bakery's bench: time the task, remove the movements that waste time, and teach the better arrangement. The setting differs, but the idea carries over.

> In a ward, the method must protect safety as well as save time.

A caution belongs here. In a hospital, a faster round that causes a mistake is worse than a slow one. The goal of the study is therefore not speed alone. The study must keep the task safe, and any new arrangement must be checked for that before it is adopted. This shows that a method found by study still needs judgement.

### What Scientific Management Got Right

> Scientific management brought measurement, method and training into management.

Scientific management gave management three lasting gifts. The first gift is measurement: a manager can ask how long a task takes, and can answer with a number. The second gift is method: tasks can be improved by study, and not only by effort. The third gift is training: a worker should learn the best method and not be left alone to find one.

> Taylor's ideas raised productivity in many factories.

These gifts raised productivity, the ratio of output to input that we met in Chapter II. When a task takes less time for the same output, the same workers produce more. Later in the twentieth century, factories around the world used time study, standard methods and piece rates. Some still do.

> Taylor's claims about particular results need careful handling.

One caution is needed. Taylor described spectacular results at a steel works in Pennsylvania, where he said the output of workers who handled heavy iron bars rose several times. Later writers have questioned the figures and the way he reached them.^3 We therefore treat these stories as claims, not as proven facts. The idea of studying work does not depend on them.

^3: Historians disagree about how accurate Taylor's reports were. Treat any single figure from his own accounts with caution.

### What Scientific Management Missed

> The first weakness is that it treated workers as parts of a machine.

Critics point to three weaknesses. The first is that Taylor treated workers mainly as parts of a machine. He assumed that money was the chief reason people work. He paid little attention to pride, friendship, boredom or fear. As later chapters show, people work for many reasons, and money is only one of them.

> The second weakness is that it suited repeated tasks better than varied ones.

The second weakness is that the method works best for tasks that are repeated many times. A packing bench is a good example. The work of a manager who deals with new problems each day is harder to time and divide. The third weakness is that workers and unions often saw the method as a way of forcing a faster pace. Where trust was low, workers resisted the stopwatch, and the gains were small.

> Taylor looked at the task; another writer looked at the whole firm.

Taylor looked closely at the task, at the level of the workshop. A French engineer asked a different question. What does a manager do, and by what rules should the whole firm be run? We turn to him next.

## Fayol and the Fourteen Principles

> Fayol studied the work of the manager, and Taylor had studied the work of the worker.

### The Man and His Question

> Henri Fayol was a French mining engineer who became the head of a large company.

Henri Fayol (1841–1925) was a French mining engineer. He worked for a company that mined coal and made iron and steel, and in 1888 he became its managing director. The company was then in serious difficulty. Under his direction it recovered, and he later gave the credit to the way it was managed.^4

^4: The company is usually named as Commentry-Fourchambault. Accounts of its recovery come mainly from Fayol's own writing and should be read with care.

> Fayol asked what managers do and what rules guide them.

Fayol wrote down what he had learned. He published *Administration industrielle et générale* in 1916. It reached English readers much later, in a translation of 1949. Taylor started with the worker and worked upward. Fayol started with the whole firm and worked downward. He wanted every manager, in any firm, to have a clear set of rules.

> Fayol divided the work of a firm into six groups, and one of them was management.

Fayol saw six groups of activity in any firm. They are technical work (making), commercial work (buying and selling), financial work (finding and using money), security work (protecting people and property), accounting work (recording), and managerial work (planning, organising and the rest). He believed that managerial work was the least understood of the six, and he gave it most of his attention.

> Fayol listed five elements of management, close to our four functions.

Fayol named five elements of management: to plan, to organise, to command, to coordinate and to control. You will recognise them. They are close to the four functions of Chapter II. To command, which means to give orders and direct people, belongs to what we call leading. To coordinate, which means to fit the parts of the firm together, we place within organising. The idea that management is a process of connected activities comes largely from Fayol.

> The table lines up Fayol's five elements with the four functions of Chapter II.

The match between Fayol's five elements and our four functions is easy to state in a table. Read each row as one pair.

:table Fayol's five elements and the four functions
| Fayol's element | Meaning in plain words | Our function |
| To plan | Look ahead and prepare a course of action | Planning |
| To organise | Build the structure of people and materials | Organising |
| To command | Give orders and direct people | Leading |
| To coordinate | Fit the parts of the firm together | Organising |
| To control | Check that results match the plan | Controlling |

### What a Principle Is

@ Principle | A general rule that guides action in many different situations.

> A principle is a general rule, not a law of nature.

Fayol offered fourteen **principles** of management. A principle is a general rule that guides action in many situations. A principle is not a law of nature. Water always boils at the same temperature at sea level, but a principle of management holds with exceptions. Fayol himself said that management is a matter of proportion, and that no principle should be applied as an unbreakable rule.

@ Fayol's principles | Fourteen general rules of management set out by Henri Fayol.

> The fourteen principles fall into three groups for easier learning.

We call these rules **Fayol's principles**. Fourteen separate rules are hard to remember, so we number them in our own order and sort them into three groups: principles about work and order, principles about authority and command, and principles about people and spirit. The figure below shows the groups, and the sections that follow explain each rule in turn.

### Principles About Work and Order

@ Division of work | Splitting a job into parts so that each person does one kind of task.

> The first principle is that dividing work raises output.

The first principle is **division of work**. Division of work means splitting a job into parts, so that each person does one kind of task. Return to the school timetable. If one teacher taught every subject to every class, that teacher would know each subject only slightly. When each teacher teaches one subject, each becomes more expert, and the school teaches better. In a bakery, one person may bake while another packs and sells. Each person becomes quick at one task.

> Division of work has a limit, because a very narrow job becomes dull.

The principle has a limit. If a job becomes very narrow, the worker may become bored, and the worker may not see how the task joins the others. Fayol saw the gain from division of work as real, but he did not ask for more division without end.

@ Unity of direction | One head and one plan for each group of activities with the same aim.

> Unity of direction means one plan and one head for each group of related activities.

The second principle is **unity of direction**. It means that a group of activities with the same aim should have one head and one plan. Suppose a firm's production team has two plans at once: one to bake more bread and one to bake new kinds of cake. The team would pull in two directions. One head and one plan keep the effort together.

@ Order | A proper, known place for each thing and each person.

> Order means that each person and each thing has a proper place.

The third principle is **order**. Order means a proper place for each thing and each person. Fayol spoke of material order, which concerns tools and goods, and of social order, which concerns people and jobs. A bench where bags, labels and string lie in fixed places has material order. A chart that shows who holds each job has social order.

@ Equity | Fair and kind treatment of staff.

> Equity means fair and kind treatment of staff.

The fourth principle is **equity**. Equity means treating staff with fairness and kindness. Fayol argued that staff who feel fairly treated give loyalty and effort. Equity is not the same as equal treatment. A fair manager may treat two workers differently if their tasks and their needs are different, and can explain why.

@ Stability of tenure | Keeping staff in their posts long enough to learn and give good service.

> Stability of tenure means keeping good staff and avoiding needless turnover.

The fifth principle is **stability of tenure**. Tenure means the length of time a person holds a job. Fayol advised that staff should not be moved or dismissed without good reason, because new staff need time to learn. A baker who leaves after four months takes the skill that the bakery has recently paid to teach.

### Principles About Authority and Command

@ Authority | The right to give orders and to use the firm's resources to get results.

> Authority is the right to give orders, and it must come with responsibility.

The sixth principle in our list is **authority**, the right to give orders and to expect obedience. Fayol joined authority to responsibility, which is the duty to answer for results. Whoever receives authority also receives responsibility. A manager who has the right to order but no duty to answer for results may use power carelessly. A manager who answers for results but has no authority cannot act. Chapter X studies this pair in detail.

@ Discipline | Obedience to agreed rules and respect for the agreements between firm and staff.

> Discipline means obedience and respect for agreed rules, kept by good leaders.

The seventh principle is **discipline**. Discipline means obedience to agreed rules and respect for the agreements between the firm and its staff. Fayol said that discipline depends on good leaders at every level, on clear agreements, and on sensible penalties used with fairness. Discipline that rests only on fear does not last.

@ Unity of command | One boss for each person: orders come from one superior only.

> Unity of command means that each person receives orders from one superior only.

The eighth principle is **unity of command**, and it is the one that the bakery's second trouble breaks. Unity of command means that each person receives orders from one superior only. When two people give different orders, the worker does not know whom to obey. One order is ignored, or the worker tries both and does neither well. Quarrels follow, because each superior believes the worker belongs to him or her.

@! Go slowly here. Unity of command and unity of direction sound alike.

> The two unities differ: one concerns a person's boss, the other a team's plan.

Keep the two unities apart. Unity of command is about one person: who is that person's boss? Unity of direction is about a group: do all its members follow the same plan? A team can have unity of command, with every member holding a single boss, and still lack unity of direction if the boss gives the team two conflicting plans.

> A hospital ward shows how hard unity of command can be to keep.

Consider a hospital ward, a setting where this principle is tested. A nurse may receive instructions from the ward's senior nurse about bed care and from a doctor about treatment. Both people have a right to speak, and the nurse serves two bosses in part. Hospitals handle this by dividing the subjects: the doctor decides treatment, and the senior nurse decides ward routine. The principle is not abandoned. It is applied to each kind of order.

@ Scalar chain | The line of authority from the top of the firm to the lowest level.

> The scalar chain is the line of authority from top to bottom.

The ninth principle is the **scalar chain**. The scalar chain is the line of authority that runs from the top of the firm to the lowest level. Orders travel down it, and reports travel up. In a firm with an owner, a supervisor and two workers, the chain has three levels. A message should normally pass through each level.

> Fayol allowed a direct link between equals, with their superiors' knowledge.

Fayol saw that a long chain can be slow. Suppose a baker needs help from a rider, and both sit on the same level but in different parts of the firm. To pass the message up and down the chain would waste days. Fayol therefore allowed a bridge, which he called a gang plank. The two may speak directly, provided their superiors know of it.

@ Centralisation | The degree to which authority is held at the top of the firm.

> Centralisation concerns how much authority is held at the top.

The tenth principle is **centralisation**. It concerns how far authority is held at the top and how far it is shared with lower levels. Fayol did not say that more is always better, or that less is. He said that the right amount depends on the firm, its size and its people. In a very small firm, the owner holds nearly all authority, and that suits the firm. Chapter X returns to this principle.

### Principles About People and Spirit

> The eleventh principle asks workers to put the firm's interest first.

The eleventh principle is **subordination of individual interest to general interest**. Here to subordinate means to place second. When a worker's wish and the firm's good conflict, the firm's good should usually come first. Fayol knew that people forget this, from ignorance or ambition or weakness. He asked managers to set an example and to keep agreements fair, so that sacrifice is not demanded from one side only.

> Remuneration means pay that is fair to workers and to the firm.

The twelfth principle is **remuneration**. Remuneration means payment for work. It should be fair to the worker and to the firm, and it should reward effort that deserves reward. Pay that is too low drives good workers away. Pay that is too high may put the firm in danger.

@ Initiative | The freedom to think out a plan and to carry it through.

> Initiative means freedom to think out a plan and carry it through.

The thirteenth principle is **initiative**. Initiative means the freedom to think out a plan and to carry it through. Fayol counted it among the keenest satisfactions of a person at work. A manager must sometimes give up personal vanity in order to let others act. In a bakery, a helper who suggests a new bread, and is allowed to try it, is using initiative.

@ esprit de corps | Team spirit: harmony and unity among the members of a firm.

> Esprit de corps means unity and good feeling among the members of a firm.

The fourteenth principle is **esprit de corps**, a French phrase for team spirit.^5 It means harmony and unity among the members of a firm. Fayol warned managers against dividing a team, and against the habit of sending written notes when a short talk would do. Where staff feel that they belong, they work better together.

^5: The phrase is French. It is pronounced roughly "es-PREE duh KOR". Use it with that meaning only: the spirit of a united group.

### How the Principles Are Used

> The principles form a checklist for diagnosing a troubled firm.

A manager can use the principles as a checklist. When a firm is in trouble, the manager runs through the fourteen and asks which one is being broken. Many problems of small firms have simple causes. Two bosses break unity of command. Unclear jobs break order. A worker with no freedom to try new ideas breaks initiative.

> The principles have limits, and critics have named three.

Critics have named three limits. The first is that the principles are general, and in a real case two of them may pull in opposite directions. Unity of command may conflict with the need to use expert advice. The second is that the principles were written from the experience of one man in large industrial firms, and he did not test them in other settings. The third is that they say little about how people feel. Even so, they remain a useful starting point, and many later writers built on them.

> The principles are best used as guides to be weighed, not rules to be applied blindly.

Fayol would have agreed with these cautions. He wrote that the principles are flexible and must be used with judgement. The skill of the manager lies in knowing which principle matters most in a given case, and in what proportion.


### Taylor and Fayol Compared

> Taylor and Fayol looked at management from opposite ends.

Taylor and Fayol worked in the same years, but in different countries, and they did not copy each other. Taylor began at the level of the worker and the task. Fayol began at the level of the whole firm and the manager. Taylor's question was how a task should be done. Fayol's question was how a firm should be run. Their answers fit together like two halves of one picture, and a student gains most by seeing both.

> The two men agreed that management can be taught, and disagreed on where to begin.

On one point they agreed strongly. Both believed that management is not a gift that a few people are born with. Both believed that careful study can produce rules that anyone can learn. This belief, more than any single rule, is their shared gift to the subject. On other points they differed, and one difference deserves a closer look.

@ Functional foremanship | Taylor's plan to replace one foreman with several specialist foremen, each directing one part of a worker's task.

> One of Taylor's own plans seems to break Fayol's rule of unity of command.

Taylor proposed a plan that he called **functional foremanship**. Functional foremanship means replacing a single foreman with several specialist foremen, each of whom directs one part of a worker's task. One foreman might direct speed, another the care of the machine, and another the order of the work.^6 The idea has a logic: each foreman is an expert. But the worker now has several bosses. Fayol's rule of unity of command says that this causes confusion. Here two great writers disagree, and a manager must decide which risk is greater.

^6: Taylor's plan is usually described as using several specialist foremen, some in a planning room and some on the shop floor. Check the number and the roles before citing them.

> The table sets out the main differences between the two writers.

We now list the differences in a table. The first column names the feature, and the next two columns describe each writer.

:table Taylor and Fayol compared
| Feature | Taylor | Fayol |
| Starting point | The worker and the task | The whole firm and the manager |
| Main tool | Time-and-motion study | Fourteen principles |
| Main concern | Output per worker | Sound running of the firm |
| View of orders | Several specialist foremen | One boss for each person |
| Main weakness | Treats workers as machines | Principles are general and can conflict |

> The comparison shows two views that a manager needs together.

A manager of a bakery can use both writers on the same day. In the morning the manager studies the packing bench, as Taylor would. In the afternoon the manager checks that each person has one boss, as Fayol would. The two views do not compete. They answer different questions.

## Later Approaches to Management

> Four later approaches added people, mathematics, whole systems and situations.

Taylor and Fayol gave management its first body of ideas. Their ideas were useful, but each had a gap. Taylor said little about what workers feel. Fayol said little about how a firm reacts to the world outside it. Later writers filled the gaps, and four approaches stand out. We take them in the order of their rise: the human relations approach, management science, the systems approach and the contingency approach.

### The Human Relations Approach

> The human relations approach began when a study of lighting found that people, not lights, changed output.

In the 1920s, researchers began a series of studies at the Hawthorne Works, a factory near Chicago owned by the Western Electric Company.^7 They wanted to learn how conditions such as lighting and rest breaks affect output. The results surprised them. Output rose when the lighting improved. It also rose when the lighting was made worse. Something other than lighting was at work.

^7: The studies ran from the middle 1920s into the early 1930s. Elton Mayo and colleagues from Harvard joined the work. Dates and details vary between accounts, and later critics have questioned the methods.

@ Human relations approach | An approach that stresses people's feelings, attention and group membership.

> The researchers concluded that attention and group belonging affect how people work.

The researchers reached a new idea. The workers knew that they were being watched, and they felt that managers cared about them. They also formed a close group among themselves, with its own unwritten rules about how much to produce. From these findings grew the **human relations approach**. The human relations approach stresses that workers are people with feelings, and that attention, respect and group membership strongly affect how well they work.

> The researchers used a small test room to watch a group of workers closely.

In one study, a small group of women who assembled telephone relays were moved to a separate room and watched, while the researchers changed their hours, breaks and pay in turn. Output rose again and again, whatever the researchers changed. The researchers decided that the workers' pride in being chosen, and their friendly relations with each other and with the observers, had raised their effort.

> In a second study, the workers' own group set a limit on output.

In another study, a group of men who wired telephone equipment was observed in a room of their own. Here the researchers found that the group had an unwritten rule about how much each member should produce. A member who produced too much, or too little, was teased or pushed back into line. The group, and not the manager, set the limit. This finding showed that an informal group can hold great power over how people work, a point we take up in Chapter XI.

> The approach corrects Taylor's view that pay is the main reason people work.

You can see how this approach answers Taylor. Taylor believed that pay and a good method drive effort. The Hawthorne researchers found that feelings and groups matter as well. A worker who feels respected may work harder than one who is only well paid. We study this idea more fully in Chapter XI, where we ask what motivates people.

> The approach has limits: later critics doubted the studies and the idea that contented workers are always productive.

The approach also has limits. Critics say that the Hawthorne studies were not carefully controlled, and that their findings were stated too strongly. Critics also say that a contented worker is not always a productive one. Still, the approach changed management for good. After it, managers could no longer ignore how people feel.

### Management Science

@ Management science | Using mathematical models and calculation to help managers solve problems.

> Management science uses mathematics and computers to solve problems that can be measured.

A different line of thought grew from the Second World War. Teams of scientists were asked to solve practical problems, such as how to place ships and aircraft for the best protection. They built mathematical models of the problem, tested different choices on paper, and recommended the best. After the war, firms borrowed the method. The result is called **management science**. Management science means using mathematical models and calculation to help managers solve problems.

@ Model | A simplified picture of a real problem, made with numbers and rules.

> A model is a simplified picture of a problem that uses numbers.

The word **model** needs a short explanation. A model is a simplified picture of a real problem, drawn with numbers and rules. A bakery might build a model that shows how many loaves to bake each day. The model uses the average number of customers, the cost of ingredients, and the loss from unsold bread. Changing a number in the model shows what would happen, without any risk to the real bakery.

> Management science suits problems with clear numbers, such as stock, scheduling and transport.

Management science works best on problems that have clear numbers: how much stock to hold, how to schedule work, how to plan deliveries, how to choose between projects. Computers made the calculations quick, and the approach spread. We use some of its tools in Chapter VIII, where we choose between alternatives using probability.

> Management science helps with measurable problems but says little about people.

Its weakness is the other side of its strength. Many managerial problems cannot be reduced to numbers. How should a manager handle a worker who feels unfairly treated? What mathematics decides that? Management science is a strong tool, but it is not a whole theory of management.

### Two Different Kinds of Science

> Scientific management and management science have similar names but different meanings.

Beginners often confuse the two terms. Scientific management is Taylor's method of studying work. Management science is the later use of mathematical models. Both claim to use science, and the names are close, so we put them side by side in a table. The first column names the feature, and the next two columns describe each approach.

:table The two approaches that use the word science
| Feature | Scientific management | Management science |
| When it appeared | Early twentieth century | Middle twentieth century |
| Main figure or source | Frederick Taylor | Wartime scientific teams and later researchers |
| What it studies | How a worker does a task | How a manager solves a problem |
| Main tool | Stopwatch and movement study | Mathematical model and computer |
| Typical question | What is the best way to pack a loaf? | How many loaves should we bake tomorrow? |
| Main weakness | Treats workers as machines | Hard to use where numbers are missing |

> The table shows that one studies tasks and the other studies decisions.

Read the table across a row to compare the two on one feature. The pattern is plain. Scientific management studies the task at the level of the worker. Management science studies the problem at the level of the manager. Both rely on measurement, and that is why both carry the word science.

### The Systems Approach

@ Systems approach | Seeing an organisation as a set of linked parts that work together.

> The systems approach sees an organisation as a set of linked parts working toward a goal.

The third later approach asks us to look at the firm as a whole. A **system** is a set of parts that are linked and that work together toward a purpose. A bicycle is a system. The wheels, chain, pedals and frame each do a different task, and the bicycle moves only when all of them work together. The **systems approach** treats an organisation in the same way: as a set of linked parts, such as departments, that cannot be understood one by one.^8

^8: A Austrian-born biologist, Ludwig von Bertalanffy, developed general systems theory in the middle of the twentieth century. Management writers later applied it to organisations.

@ Feedback | Information about results that returns to the firm to guide later work.

> A system takes inputs, changes them, and delivers outputs.

The figure below shows how a business works as a system. First, the firm takes in inputs, the resources we met in Chapter I: people, money, materials, equipment and information. Next, the firm transforms them by baking, selling, or some other work. Finally, it delivers outputs, such as bread and profit. The results return to the firm as information, and this return is called **feedback**. The firm uses feedback to correct its next round of work.

:fig A firm seen as a system, with its environment around it | fig_system.svg

> The lesson of the systems approach is that a change in one part affects the others.

The lesson of this approach is that the parts depend on each other. If the baking team makes more bread, the packing bench must pack more. If the packing bench is slow, bread waits and goes stale. A manager who improves one part and ignores the others may make the whole system worse. The approach also reminds us that the firm is surrounded by an environment, which we study in Chapter IV.

### The Contingency Approach

@ Contingency approach | The view that the best way to manage depends on the situation.

> The contingency approach says that the best method depends on the situation.

The last approach answers a question that all the earlier ones left open. Is there one best way to manage? The **contingency approach** says no. It holds that the best method depends on the situation. The word contingent means depending on something else.

> Researchers found that firms in changing markets and in steady markets need different structures.

The idea grew from research in the middle of the twentieth century. Researchers studied firms in different industries and found that successful firms were not organised in the same way. A firm in a fast-changing market did better with flexible roles and quick talk between people. A firm in a steady market did better with fixed roles and clear rules.^9 Neither form was best everywhere. Each fitted its situation.

^9: Studies by Tom Burns and G. M. Stalker, by Joan Woodward, and by Paul Lawrence and Jay Lorsch are often named here. Check each source before citing it.

> Contingency thinking asks a manager to match the method to the situation.

Contingency thinking turns every managerial question into a pair of questions. What is the situation? Which method fits it? Size, technology, the environment and the people involved all change the answer. A rule that works in a large factory may fail in a small bakery. The approach is more demanding than the earlier ones, because it offers no single rule. It offers a way of thinking.

> A table shows how different situations call for different methods.

The next table gives three situations and the method that would probably fit each. It is an illustration of the way of thinking, not a rule. Read each row as one pair: the situation first, and then the method that fits it.

:table Situation and fitting method
| Situation | A method that probably fits | Why |
| A bakery makes the same bread every day, in a steady market | Fixed roles and written routines | The work repeats, so routines save effort |
| A bakery must create new products quickly for a festival | Flexible roles and frequent talk | The work changes, so rigid roles slow it |
| A bakery of four people compared with one of forty | Direct talk with the owner, then more levels and rules | A larger group needs more structure to keep order |

> The table shows that the right method changes when the situation changes.

Notice that the same bakery appears in all three rows, and the fitting method changes. The manager's task is to read the situation first. A manager who applies one favourite method to every case will sometimes be right and sometimes be wrong, and will not know why.

> Each approach is useful, and a good manager draws on all of them.

Do not suppose that the contingency approach has defeated all the others. It has joined them. A manager facing a slow packing bench uses Taylor's study. A manager facing two bosses uses Fayol's rule. A manager facing unhappy staff uses the human relations idea. A manager facing a stock problem uses management science. A manager considering all of these together uses the systems approach, and then the contingency approach decides which to prefer. The ideas in this chapter that are old are not dead. They form a toolbox.

### A Note on Bureaucracy

> One more idea belongs in this history, though it is not one of the four approaches.

At about the same time as Taylor and Fayol, a German scholar named Max Weber (1864–1920) studied how large organisations, such as governments and armies, were run. He described a form of organisation built on written rules, fixed ranks and written records, in which people are chosen for their skills and not for their family or friends. Weber saw that such a form could be fair and steady. Later writers pointed out that it could also become slow and rigid. We study this form in Chapter IX, where we look at the ways in which organisations are designed.

### The Schools Side by Side

> A table helps a student keep the schools in order.

Five schools have now been described, and they are easy to confuse. The next table sets them in order and gives for each the main belief, a strength and a weakness. Read it across, one row at a time. Use it as a map when you revise the chapter.

:table The main schools of thought compared
| School | Main belief | A strength | A weakness |
| Scientific management | Study work to find the best method | Measurement and method | Treats workers as machines |
| Fayol's principles | A firm needs general rules | A checklist for any firm | Rules can conflict |
| Human relations | Feelings and groups matter | Attention to people | Doubts about the studies |
| Management science | Models can solve problems | Precision with numbers | Weak where numbers are missing |

> The table shows a pattern: each school corrected a weakness in an earlier one.

Look at the last column and the first. Each weakness was the opening for a later school. Treating workers as machines opened the way for human relations. A lack of attention to numbers opened the way for management science. A search for one best rule opened the way for contingency thinking. This is the pattern that the timeline at the start of the chapter promised.

> We now ask what kind of knowledge all these ideas amount to.

We have now told the story. One question remains. When we gather these ideas together, what kind of subject is management? We take the question next.

## Is Management a Science, an Art or a Profession?

> People disagree about what management is, so we need tests.

### Three Words, Three Tests

> We need three tests, one for each word.

People call management by three names. Some say it is a science, some an art, and some a profession. A dispute of this kind is settled by tests, not by opinion. We shall state one test for each word, and then apply it.

> A subject is a science when it has organised knowledge that has been tested.

The first word is **science**. A subject is a science when its knowledge is organised, built on facts that have been tested, and able to explain cause and effect. It should also give results that others can repeat. Physics is a science. When you heat water in the same conditions, you get the same result each time.

> A subject is an art when success depends on personal skill and judgement.

The second word is **art**. A subject is an art when success depends on personal skill, practice and judgement, and not only on knowledge. A good cook and a good musician use rules, but their skill comes from practice and from a feeling for the situation. Two cooks with the same recipe may produce different food.

@ Profession | An occupation with trained skill, a code of conduct and set standards.

> A subject is a profession when it has trained members, a code and a standard.

The third word is **profession**. A profession is an occupation that rests on a body of knowledge learned through long training, a code of conduct that members accept, and a duty to serve others. Doctors and lawyers are professions. Their members must pass tests, and a licensing body may remove those who break the code.

### Testing Management Against Each Word

> Management meets the test of science in part.

First, is management a science? In part, it is. Managers use organised knowledge, such as the principles and models in this chapter, and many ideas have been tested by research. But management deals with people, who differ and change. The same method may succeed in one firm and fail in another. Management therefore does not give the repeatable results of physics.

> Management meets the test of art fully, because success depends on judgement.

Second, is management an art? Here the answer is stronger. Two managers with the same knowledge may do very different work. One knows when to give an order, and when to wait. One notices an unhappy worker before the worker complains. This sense of the right moment comes from practice. The contingency approach says as much: the manager must judge the situation.

> Management meets the test of profession only in part.

Third, is management a profession? In part again. Management has a growing body of knowledge, and universities teach it. Many managers belong to associations, and some have codes of conduct. But in most countries a person may become a manager with no licence and no set course of study. A manager who behaves badly is not removed by a professional body. Management is moving toward a profession but has not reached it.

:table Management measured against the three tests
| Test | What it asks | How far management meets it |
| Science | Is knowledge organised, tested and repeatable? | In part: knowledge is organised, but people vary |
| Art | Does success depend on personal skill and judgement? | Strongly |
| Profession | Is there trained entry, a code and a standard? | In part: growing, but no licence in most places |

> The table summarises the answer: management uses science, practises as art and moves toward a profession.

The table gives a fair verdict. Management uses science, because it draws on tested knowledge. It is practised as an art, because skill and judgement decide the result. It is moving toward a profession, because training and codes are growing. A person who asks which one word is right has asked the wrong question. The better question is how much of each is present.

:box IN SHORT
Management is not only a science, only an art, or only a profession. It applies organised knowledge, with personal judgement, in an occupation that is moving toward professional standards.

> Why the answer matters: it tells a student what to learn.

The answer also tells the student what to learn. Knowledge of the principles is necessary, because it is the science. Practice and reflection are necessary, because they build the art. A sense of duty to staff, customers and society is necessary, because it belongs to a profession. Chapter V takes up that duty.

## A Worked Case: Two Troubles at the Bakery

> The case applies two ideas from this chapter to the bakery's third month.

This case applies the chapter to one situation. It has five parts: the situation, the question, the reasoning, the conclusion and a check. It is not a model answer to copy. It shows one way of thinking that you can use on other cases.

> The case states the facts of month 3 before any reasoning begins.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Month 3. The Morning Loaf Bakery has four staff: two bakers, one counter assistant and one rider. Output is 250 loaves a day, produced in 32 staff hours. Packing a loaf at the counter bench takes 40 seconds. The owner moves the bags, labels and string close to the packer, and packing then takes 30 seconds. Until now, two people have given orders to the counter assistant. The owner decides that from now on only one will.

> The first step is to name each trouble and match it to an idea.

*Question.* What do the ideas of Taylor and Fayol say about each trouble? And how much does the change at the bench gain?

> The packing trouble belongs to scientific management.

*Reasoning, first trouble.* The packing bench is a task, and Taylor studied tasks. The owner timed the work, found movements that were not needed, and moved the supplies. That is a time-and-motion study in small form. Now we measure the gain. Before the change, one loaf took 40 seconds, so one person could pack 3,600 ÷ 40 = 90 loaves in an hour. After the change, one loaf takes 30 seconds, so the same person can pack 3,600 ÷ 30 = 120 loaves in an hour. Output per hour has risen by 30 loaves, which is a rise of one third.

> At 250 loaves a day, the time saved at the bench is about 42 minutes.

We can also count the time saved in a day. At 250 loaves a day, packing took 250 × 40 = 10,000 seconds before the change. It takes 250 × 30 = 7,500 seconds after the change. The saving is 2,500 seconds, which is about 42 minutes. The counter assistant can use that time to serve customers.

> The second trouble belongs to Fayol's unity of command.

*Reasoning, second trouble.* The counter assistant received orders from two people. This breaks unity of command, which says that each person should have one superior. The cost is confusion. When one person says to pack first and another says to serve first, the assistant must choose, and whichever choice the assistant makes will displease someone. By naming one person to give orders, the owner restores the principle.

> The conclusion is that two different ideas solved two different troubles.

*Conclusion.* The bench trouble was a trouble of method, and the cure came from studying the task. The command trouble was a trouble of authority, and the cure came from a clear line of command. The two troubles needed two different ideas, which shows why a manager needs more than one approach.

> The check confirms that the arithmetic and the logic agree.

*Check.* The time per loaf fell from 40 to 30 seconds, a cut of 10 ÷ 40 = one quarter. If the time falls by one quarter, the output per hour rises by one third, because 1 ÷ 0.75 = 1.33. This agrees with the rise from 90 to 120 loaves, which is also one third. The figures are consistent. In words, the check on the second trouble is that each person now has exactly one boss.

## Summary

> The summary restates the main ideas of the chapter in a few lines.

A school of thought is a group of ideas that share one main belief. New schools appear because earlier ones left problems unsolved. Scientific management, from Frederick Taylor, studies work by measurement to find the best method, and its main tool is the time-and-motion study. Fayol's principles are fourteen general rules for running a firm, and they fall into groups about work and order, authority and command, and people and spirit. Unity of command means one boss for each person.

> The later approaches added people, models, wholes and situations.

The human relations approach added feelings and groups. Management science added mathematical models. The systems approach added the view of the firm as linked parts with feedback. The contingency approach added the idea that the best method depends on the situation. Management is partly a science, strongly an art, and moves toward a profession.

## Where People Go Wrong

> A common error is to think that scientific management and management science are the same.

*Error:* The two terms mean the same thing. *Correction:* Scientific management is Taylor's study of how workers perform tasks. Management science is the later use of mathematical models to solve managerial problems. Their names are alike, but their subjects and tools differ.

> A second error is to treat Fayol's principles as laws that never bend.

*Error:* Fayol's principles are laws that every firm must obey. *Correction:* Fayol said that the principles are flexible and must be weighed with judgement. A hospital ward cannot apply unity of command in the way a small shop does.

> A third error is to believe that a later approach cancels the earlier ones.

*Error:* The newest approach is the best, and the old ones are out of date. *Correction:* Each approach solves a different kind of problem. Managers still use time study, still follow many of Fayol's rules, still pay attention to feelings, still build models, and still ask what fits the situation.

> A fourth error is to mix up unity of command and unity of direction.

*Error:* Unity of command and unity of direction are two names for one rule. *Correction:* Unity of command concerns one person and one boss. Unity of direction concerns one group and one plan.

## In History

> Fayol's firm recovered under his direction, and he gave the credit to management.

In 1888 Henri Fayol became managing director of a French mining and iron company that was in serious financial difficulty. He later wrote that the firm recovered, and that the cause was not luck but method: a clear plan, a careful choice of managers, and sound rules of administration.^10 Whatever the exact figures, the story shows why Fayol's work mattered. He tested his ideas in a real firm, in a real crisis, and then he wrote them down so that others could use them.

^10: This account rests mainly on Fayol's own writing. Historians have not always been able to check the details independently.

## Sources

> Taylor and Fayol are the two landmark sources of this chapter.

Taylor's book of 1911, *The Principles of Scientific Management*, is the landmark source for scientific management. It matters because it set out the idea that work can be studied and improved by measurement. Fayol's book, *Administration industrielle et générale* (1916), is the landmark source for the principles of management. It matters because it treated management as a body of teachable rules.
