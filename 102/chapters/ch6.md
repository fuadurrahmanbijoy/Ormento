## A Journey Without a Map

> A bakery owner has six months to grow the shop, and no plan yet.

Return to the Morning Loaf Bakery at the start of its sixth month. Sales are steady and the staff work well together. Yet the owner feels uneasy. Customers keep asking for more bread. Flour has become dearer. A festival will soon bring many buyers to the street. If the owner does nothing special, each of these events will arrive as a surprise.

> The owner can wait for events, or prepare for them.

The owner has two choices. One choice is to wait and then react to each event as it comes. The other choice is to look ahead, to decide what the shop should become, and to prepare for what is likely to happen. The second choice takes time now. It saves time and money later.

> This chapter studies the second choice, which is called planning.

Chapter II named planning as the first of the four functions. We now study it closely. We ask what a plan is, what kinds of plan exist, how a plan is made, why plans fail, and how a manager can make planning work.

> You need three ideas from earlier chapters.

You will need three earlier ideas. From Chapter II, the four functions and the meaning of a decision. From Chapter IV, the idea of the environment, because a plan must be made with the outside world in view. From Chapter I, the idea of resources, because a plan decides how resources will be used.

## Why Plan: Meaning, Nature and Purposes

### Planning in Everyday Life

> Planning is common in daily life, though people seldom use the word.

> A journey shows the main parts of planning.

Think of a student who must travel to a university in another city for an examination. The student does not leave the house without a thought. First the student decides to arrive the evening before. Then the student chooses the bus, buys a ticket, and packs a bag. The student also thinks about what might go wrong, such as a late bus, and leaves early to allow for it. Every one of these acts is planning, although none is called by that name.

> The student's planning has three parts: a place to reach, a way to get there, and thought about the road.

Look closely at what the student did. There was a place to reach, which was the city. There was a way to get there, which was the bus. And there was thought about the road, which was the allowance for delay. These three parts appear in every plan, from a student's journey to a firm's strategy for ten years.

### What a Plan Is

@ Plan | A chosen route from where we are to where we want to be.

> A plan is a chosen route to a goal.

The word **plan** has a clear meaning in management. A plan is a chosen route from where we are to a result that we want to reach. The result is called a **goal**, and we met that word in Chapter I. In the journey, the goal is to reach the city and the plan is the bus and the early start.

@ Planning | Deciding in advance what to do, how, when and by whom.

> Planning is the act of making plans.

**Planning** is the act of deciding in advance what to do, how to do it, when to do it, and who will do it. A plan is the result. Planning is the activity that produces it. Keep the two words apart: a manager does planning, and a firm holds a plan.

> Planning looks to the future, but it uses information from the past and the present.

Planning looks forward. Yet it does not guess in the dark. The manager studies what happened last month, what is happening today, and what is likely to happen next. The bakery owner, for example, looks at last year's sales, this week's orders and the date of the festival. Out of all this comes a picture of the months ahead.

### The Nature of Planning

> Planning has five features that distinguish it from other work.

Five features describe the nature of planning. We take them one by one.

> First, planning comes first among the functions.

First, planning comes before the other functions. A manager cannot arrange people (organising), guide them (leading) or check results (controlling) until it is known what they are supposed to do. Plans give the other three functions something to work on. This is why planning is called the first function, although in practice all four overlap.

> Second, planning is done at every level.

Second, planning happens at every level. The owner of a large firm plans for years ahead. A first-line supervisor plans the next shift. The scale differs, but the activity is the same. A bakery with only one manager still needs plans, and the owner is the planner.

> Third, planning is a continuing process.

Third, planning never ends. Conditions change, and a plan made in January may be out of date by April. The manager therefore returns to the plan, tests it, and revises it. A good plan is not a document in a drawer. It is a living guide.

> Fourth, planning is aimed at goals.

Fourth, planning aims at goals. A plan with no goal is like a bus with no destination. Chapter VII studies goals and objectives in detail. For now, remember that every plan asks first: what are we trying to reach?

> Fifth, planning involves choice.

Fifth, planning means choosing among alternatives. There are almost always several routes to a goal. The student may go by bus, by train or by a friend's car. Planning asks the manager to weigh the choices and to pick one. A problem with only one possible answer needs no planning.

### The Purposes of Planning

> Planning serves five purposes in a firm.

Why should a manager spend time on plans? Five purposes explain it.

> The first purpose is to give direction.

The first purpose is direction. When everyone knows the goal and the route, people pull in the same direction. In the bakery, bakers, counter staff and the rider all know that the shop will bake more bread by a certain date. Each can then decide what to do without asking the owner every hour.

> The second purpose is to reduce the effect of uncertainty.

The second purpose is to prepare for change. Nobody knows the future, but managers can think about what is likely. A plan that says flour may become dearer prepares the shop to buy flour early or to change its recipes. Planning does not remove uncertainty. It reduces the damage that surprise can do.

> The third purpose is to cut waste and overlap.

The third purpose is to avoid waste. When the work is planned, resources are used where they are needed. A baker does not wait for flour that was never ordered. A rider does not make two trips where one would do. Planned work wastes less time, money and material.

> The fourth purpose is to set up control.

The fourth purpose is to make control possible. Chapter XIII shows that managers check results against a standard. A plan supplies the standard. Without a plan, a manager can see what happened but cannot say whether it was good or bad.

> The fifth purpose is to encourage forward thinking.

The fifth purpose is to make managers think ahead. A manager who must write a plan has to look at the future, and that habit is useful in itself. Even when a plan changes, the thinking behind it leaves the manager better prepared.

:box IN SHORT
A plan is a chosen route to a goal. Planning is the activity of deciding in advance what to do, how, when and by whom. It gives direction, prepares for change, cuts waste, makes control possible, and trains managers to think ahead.

### Planning and the Other Functions

> Planning joins each of the other three functions.

Planning touches every other function, and it helps to see how. When a manager organises, the structure is built to carry out a plan. A plan to bake 300 loaves needs more bakers and a clearer division of tasks, and organising supplies them. When a manager leads, the plan tells the manager what to ask of people and why. When a manager controls, the plan supplies the standard of comparison. The four functions are not four separate boxes. They form one cycle, and planning is the place where the cycle starts again each time.

> A manager spends more time planning at higher levels.

The amount of planning in a manager's day depends on the level. A top manager spends much time on long-range plans and little on daily tasks. A first-line supervisor does the reverse. Yet every manager plans, and every manager must plan with the plans of the level above in view. A supervisor who ignores the firm's strategy may plan a perfect shift that serves no useful purpose.

### How Far Ahead Should a Manager Look?

> The time covered by a plan is called its horizon.

Every plan covers a stretch of time. We call this stretch the **planning horizon**. A short horizon covers days. A long horizon covers years. The right horizon depends on how quickly the firm's world changes and on how long its commitments last. A firm that buys an oven expected to last fifteen years must think many years ahead. A firm that sells fresh bread each day must plan hours ahead.

> The manager should look as far ahead as the commitments that are being made.

A useful rule is to look ahead as far as the present decision will have effects. If the bakery signs a five-year lease on a new shop, it is making a five-year commitment, and it should test the plan against five years of possible change. If the bakery buys flour for tomorrow, the horizon is one day. A manager who chooses too short a horizon is surprised by the consequences. A manager who chooses too long a horizon wastes effort on a future that cannot be known.

> You should now be able to say what planning is and why it matters.

You should now be able to say this: a plan is a chosen route to a goal, planning is the continuing work of choosing it, and planning gives direction, prepares for change, cuts waste, makes control possible and encourages thought about the future.

## Kinds of Plans

> Plans differ by how far ahead they look and by how often they are used.

Not all plans are alike. A plan to open a second shop is very different from a plan for cleaning the ovens each night. To keep the many kinds in order, we sort plans in two ways. First we sort them by level, which means by how far ahead they look and how much of the firm they cover. Then we sort them by use, which means whether the plan serves again and again or only once.

### Plans by Level

@ Strategic plan | A long-range plan for the whole firm, setting its main direction.

> A strategic plan sets the main direction of the whole firm for a long period.

The first level is the **strategic plan**. A strategic plan is a long-range plan for the whole firm. It answers the largest questions. What business are we in? Where do we wish to be in several years? Which markets will we serve? Top managers make strategic plans, because only they see the whole firm. A bakery that decides to become a chain of five shops within ten years has made a strategic plan.

@ Tactical plan | A middle-range plan that turns part of a strategic plan into work for one department.

> A tactical plan turns part of the strategy into work for one department.

The second level is the **tactical plan**. A tactical plan is a middle-range plan that shows how one department will carry out its part of the strategy. If the strategy is to open more shops, a tactical plan for the baking department might say how many bakers to train and which new ovens to buy in the next year. Middle managers usually make these plans.

@ Operational plan | A short-range plan for daily or weekly work in a single unit.

> An operational plan covers daily or weekly work.

The third level is the **operational plan**. An operational plan is a short-range plan for the daily or weekly work of one unit. It says who bakes what, at which hour, with which ingredients. First-line managers make these plans, because they are closest to the work.

> The three levels fit together like a ladder.

The three levels fit together. The strategic plan sets the direction. Each tactical plan carries part of it forward. Each operational plan turns a tactical plan into daily tasks. The diagram below shows the shape. At the top there are few plans, and each is broad. At the bottom there are many plans, and each is narrow.

:fig The three levels of plans | fig_levels.svg

> The levels differ in time, in width and in who makes them.

We can compare the three levels in a table. Each row names one feature, and each column describes one level.

:table Three levels of plans
| Feature | Strategic | Tactical | Operational |
| Time covered | Several years | One year or less | Days or weeks |
| Part of the firm covered | The whole firm | One department | One unit or task |
| Usually made by | Top managers | Middle managers | First-line managers |
| Typical question | Where should the firm be going? | How will this department help? | Who does what today? |
| Bakery example | Open a second shop | Train bakers for the new shop | Set tomorrow's baking list |

> The time spans are guides and not fixed limits.

The time spans in the table are guides. A very fast-moving firm may call one year long-range. A slow-moving firm may call five years middle-range. What matters is the order: strategic first, tactical next, operational last, each serving the one above.

> You should now be able to name the three levels of plan.

You should now be able to say this: a strategic plan is long-range and covers the whole firm, a tactical plan is mid-range and covers a department, and an operational plan is short-range and covers daily work.

### How the Levels Fit Together: A Bakery Chain of Plans

> One example can show how a strategic plan becomes daily work.

An example will show how the three levels join. Suppose the owner of the bakery decides on a strategy: within five years the firm will serve three districts of the city. That is the strategic plan. The owner then asks each part of the firm to prepare a tactical plan. The baking department decides to train two new bakers each year and to buy a second oven in the second year. The sales department decides to open a counter in a second district in the third year. Each of these is a tactical plan.

> The tactical plans in turn become operational plans.

The head baker now makes an operational plan. It says that this week, the new trainee will work beside an experienced baker on the morning shift, and that on Thursday the oven will be cleaned. These small plans look unimportant by themselves. Yet each is a small step toward the strategy. If the operational plans did not serve the tactical plans, and the tactical plans did not serve the strategy, the firm would be busy without arriving anywhere.

> A mismatch between levels is a common weakness.

A common weakness is a mismatch between levels. A strategy may promise growth, while the daily plans cut every cost that growth would need. The manager who finds such a mismatch should not blame the people at the lower level. The fault lies in the plans, and the cure is to fit them together.

### Plans by Use: Standing and Single-Use

@! Go slowly here. This sorting is a new idea.

> Some plans are used again and again, and others are made for one occasion only.

Now we sort plans in a second way. Think of a school. The school has a rule that every student must wear a uniform. This rule applies every day, to every student, year after year. The school also has a plan for its annual sports day. That plan is made once for one event and put away. The two plans serve different purposes, and management gives them different names.

@ Standing plan | A plan made once and used again and again for situations that repeat.

> A standing plan is used again and again.

A **standing plan** is a plan made once and used again and again for situations that repeat. It saves the manager from deciding the same matter each time. The school's uniform rule is a standing plan.

@ Single-use plan | A plan made for one occasion or one project, and not used again in the same form.

> A single-use plan serves one occasion.

A **single-use plan** is a plan made for one occasion or one project. When the occasion ends, the plan ends. The school's plan for sports day is a single-use plan.

> A table helps to keep the two apart.

We put the two kinds side by side in a table. Read it across to compare them.

:table Standing plans and single-use plans
| Feature | Standing plan | Single-use plan |
| Purpose | Handle matters that repeat | Handle one occasion or project |
| Life | Long, until changed | Ends with the occasion |
| Effort to make | Made once, used many times | Made fresh each time |
| Examples | Policy, procedure, rule | Programme, project, budget |
| Bakery example | The daily hygiene procedure | The festival-week order schedule |

### Kinds of Standing Plan

@ Policy | A general guide to thinking and decision, which leaves room for judgement.

> A policy is a general guide that leaves room for judgement.

Three standing plans need careful definition, because beginners confuse them. The first is the **policy**. A policy is a general guide to thinking and decision. It tells people the limits within which they may decide, and it leaves room for judgement. A bakery policy might say: we use only fresh ingredients and we treat customers fairly. The policy does not say what to do in each case. It guides the decision of the person who must act.

@ Procedure | A fixed series of steps for doing a repeated task.

> A procedure is a fixed series of steps in order.

The second is the **procedure**. A procedure is a fixed series of steps for a task that repeats. It tells people what to do and in what order. The bakery's hygiene procedure may say: wash hands, put on a clean apron, clean the table, and only then begin. Where a policy leaves room for judgement, a procedure leaves little.

@ Rule | A fixed instruction that allows no choice.

> A rule is a fixed instruction with no room for choice.

The third is the **rule**. A rule is a fixed instruction that says that something must or must not be done, with no choice. A rule might say: no one enters the baking room without a head covering. A rule has no steps and no order. It is a single instruction. Note the difference. A procedure is a list of steps, and a rule is one instruction.

> We can sum up the three in a table.

The next table shows the three side by side, with one bakery example for each.

:table Policy, procedure and rule
| Plan | What it gives | Room for judgement | Bakery example |
| Policy | A general guide | Much | Use only fresh ingredients |
| Procedure | Steps in order | Little | Hygiene steps before baking |
| Rule | One fixed instruction | None | Head covering in the baking room |

> The three differ in how much choice they leave to the person.

Read the last column from top to bottom. The same shop uses all three, and each plays a different part. The policy points the way. The procedure shows the steps. The rule draws a firm line.

### Kinds of Single-Use Plan

@ Programme | A single-use plan that groups the goals, steps, resources and timing for a large set of tasks.

> A programme is a large single-use plan made of many tasks.

The first single-use plan is the **programme**. A programme is a large single-use plan that groups the goals, steps, resources and timing for a whole set of tasks. A bakery's programme to open a second shop would include finding a site, buying ovens, hiring staff and advertising.

@ Project | A smaller single-use plan, often one part of a programme, with its own tasks and deadline.

> A project is a smaller plan, often one part of a programme.

The second is the **project**. A project is a smaller single-use plan, often one part of a programme, with its own tasks and deadline. Buying and installing the new ovens would be a project within the programme.

@ Budget | A plan expressed in numbers: money, materials or time to be used over a period.

> A budget is a plan in numbers.

The third is the **budget**. A budget is a plan written in numbers. It states how much money, material or time will be used or earned over a period. A budget may support any plan. For a manager, it is the plan's common language, because numbers can be added, compared and checked.

> We take care to say that budgets may be standing or single-use depending on how they are used.

A word of care is needed. In some books the budget is listed as a single-use plan, because it is made for one period and then replaced. In others it is listed separately. We list it with the single-use plans, since each budget covers one period only, and a new one is made for the next.

> You should now be able to tell the six plans apart.

You should now be able to say this: policy, procedure and rule are standing plans, and programme, project and budget are single-use plans, and each of the six can be explained with its own example.

## The Planning Process

> Making a plan follows an order of steps, and each step depends on the one before.

A good plan is not written in one burst of thought. It is built step by step, and each step gives the next one something to work on. We shall describe six steps. They are shown in the figure below, followed by the act of carrying the plan out and checking it.

:fig The six steps of planning | fig_steps.svg

> The steps are a guide, and real planners often move back and forth between them.

Real planning is not always so tidy. A manager may find at step five that the goal of step two was too bold, and go back to change it. The six steps show the logic of planning. They do not forbid a return to an earlier step.

### Step One: Find the Need or the Opportunity

> Planning begins when a manager notices that something needs to be done.

Planning begins with awareness. A manager sees a problem, or an opportunity, before it is fully clear. A fall in sales is a problem. A new housing estate near the shop is an opportunity. The manager who notices early has more time to plan. At the bakery, the owner sees three things: rising demand, a rising price of flour, and the coming festival.

> This step needs a look at the present position, the strengths and the weaknesses.

To judge the need, the manager must know the present position. Where does the firm stand today? What is it good at, and where is it weak? Without an honest answer, the plan may aim at something the firm cannot reach.

### Step Two: Set the Goal

> The goal says what the plan is trying to reach, and by when.

The second step is to set the goal. A goal states what the plan is trying to reach, and the time by which it should be reached. A weak goal says: we should bake more bread. A better goal says: we shall bake 300 loaves a day by the end of the twelfth month. The second can be checked, and the first cannot. We study goals more closely in Chapter VII.

> All later steps depend on the goal.

Everything that follows depends on this step. If the goal is vague, the manager cannot tell which alternative is better. If the goal is wrong, the best plan in the world will carry the firm to the wrong place.

### Step Three: State the Premises

@ Planning premise | An assumption about the future conditions under which a plan will be carried out.

> A planning premise is an assumption about the future that a plan rests on.

The third step is one that beginners often skip. The manager states the **planning premises**. A planning premise is an assumption about the future conditions under which the plan will be carried out. It answers the question: what do we expect the world to look like while the plan runs?

@! Go slowly here. A premise is not a fact. It is a belief about the future.

> The word premise needs everyday words to make it clear.

Think of a family that plans a picnic. The plan rests on a belief that the weather will be dry. That belief is a premise. If the day is wet, the plan fails, and the family fails with it. The family did not know the weather. It assumed. Every plan has premises of this kind, and the careful planner writes them down.

> Premises come from the environment inside and outside the firm.

Premises come from two sources. Some come from the outside environment, which Chapter IV describes: prices, laws, competitors, the economy, the habits of customers. Some come from inside the firm: how many staff there will be, how much money is available, how well the equipment will work. The bakery owner writes two premises: flour will rise in price by about 5 per cent, and demand for bread will rise by about 8 per cent.

> A premise should be stated openly so that everyone plans on the same assumptions.

Premises should be stated openly. If the head baker plans on the belief that flour will cost the same, and the owner plans on a rise, the two plans will not fit. Written premises keep everyone planning on one picture of the future. They also let the manager find the cause of a failure, since a plan fails when a premise proves false.

> A manager should choose the premises that matter most.

The list of possible premises is endless, so the manager must choose. A good test is to ask: if this assumption were false, would the plan fail? If the answer is yes, the assumption is a key premise and must be written down. If the answer is no, it can be left out.

### Step Four: List the Alternatives

> There are nearly always several ways to reach a goal.

The fourth step is to list the alternatives. An **alternative** is one possible way to reach the goal. The student's journey had three: bus, train, or a friend's car. The bakery has several ways to reach 300 loaves a day: buy a second oven, extend the opening hours, add a second shift, or buy dough from another supplier.

> The manager should search widely before choosing.

A manager should look widely. The first idea is not always the best, and a plan chosen from one alternative is not really a choice. It is better to list many and then to reduce the list.

### Step Five: Weigh the Alternatives and Choose

> Each alternative is measured against the goal and the premises.

The fifth step is to weigh the alternatives and choose one. The manager measures each alternative against two standards: how well it will reach the goal, and how well it will work under the premises. Cost, risk, time and the skills of the staff all enter the comparison.

> Some factors can be counted, and others must be judged.

Some factors can be counted. The cost of a second oven is a number. Others must be judged. How will bakers feel about a second shift? How will customers respond to a new bread? A good decision uses both kinds of information. Chapter VIII studies this step, the making of a decision, in detail.

### Step Six: Make Supporting Plans

> The chosen plan needs smaller plans and a budget to make it work.

The last step of planning is to turn the chosen plan into supporting plans. A plan to produce 300 loaves a day needs a hiring plan, a purchasing plan, a training plan and a schedule. It also needs a budget, which puts the whole plan into numbers. The budget shows whether the firm has the money, and it provides the figures against which results will later be compared.

> A plan is not finished until supporting plans and a budget exist.

Without supporting plans, a plan stays an idea. With them, each person knows the part to play.

### After the Plan: Act, Then Check

> The plan is carried out and then checked against its results.

Planning does not end when the plan is written. The manager organises the work, leads the people and then checks results against the plan. This is the function of controlling, studied in Chapter XIII. If the results differ from the plan, the manager corrects the work or changes the plan. The check completes the loop, and its findings feed the next round of planning.

> Planning and controlling depend on each other.

Planning and controlling are therefore like two sides of one coin. Planning sets the standard. Controlling compares the results with the standard. A manager who plans without controlling does not learn whether the plan worked. A manager who controls without a plan has nothing to compare against.

### The Ladder: One Process, Four Settings

> The steps can be practised in four settings, from familiar to complex.

To make the process familiar, we apply it in four settings. We begin with an everyday case and end with one that joins ideas from earlier chapters.

> First setting: a journey.

*A journey.* A student must reach an examination in another city. The need is to arrive rested. The goal is to arrive by six in the evening before the examination. The premises are that buses run on time and the road stays open. The alternatives are bus, train and a friend's car. The student weighs cost and risk, chooses the train, and then makes supporting plans: buy the ticket, pack the bag, set an alarm.

> Second setting: a week at the bakery.

*A week at the bakery.* The need is a larger order from a school on Thursday. The goal is to deliver 120 extra rolls on Thursday morning. The premises are that the ovens work all week and that flour arrives on Tuesday. The alternatives are to bake on Wednesday night or on Thursday before dawn. The owner chooses Wednesday night, since it leaves time to fix a problem. The supporting plans are a staff roster and a flour order.

> Third setting: a school fair.

*A school fair.* A school wishes to raise money at its yearly fair. The need is a new library cupboard. The goal is to raise a set sum by the day of the fair. The premises are good weather and a certain number of visitors. The alternatives are a food stall, a games stall or a book sale. The committee weighs cost and profit, chooses the food stall, and makes supporting plans: a list of helpers, a shopping list and a budget.

> Fourth setting: premises from the outside world.

*Premises from the environment.* A clinic expects a new law to raise the cost of its medicines. This is an outside force of the kind we studied in Chapter IV, and it becomes a premise: medicine costs will rise by a certain share. The clinic's plan to keep services free for poor patients must now be tested against that premise. If the rise is larger than expected, the plan must change. This case shows how the outside world feeds the inside of a plan through its premises.

> You should now be able to say the six steps in order.

You should now be able to say this: planning has six steps, which are to find the need, set the goal, state the premises, list the alternatives, weigh and choose, and make supporting plans, and after them the plan is carried out and checked.

## Limits to Planning and Why Plans Fail

> Planning is valuable, but it has limits, and many plans fail.

Planning is not magic. A manager can plan with care and still be disappointed. To use planning well, we must know where it stops being useful, and the common reasons why plans fail. We take the limits first, and then the failures.

### The Limits of Planning

> The first limit is that the future cannot be known.

The first limit is the unknown future. Every plan rests on premises, and premises are beliefs about a future that nobody can see. A flood, a sudden law or a rival's new shop may destroy a premise overnight. The further ahead a plan looks, the greater this risk. A plan for tomorrow is far safer than a plan for ten years.

> The second limit is that planning takes time and money.

The second limit is cost. Gathering information, holding meetings and writing plans use time and money. A small decision does not deserve a long plan. A manager who spends a week planning a task that takes an hour has wasted effort. The value of planning must be larger than its cost.

> The third limit is that a plan can make a firm rigid.

The third limit is rigidity. A written plan can become a habit that people follow even when the world changes. A firm that clings to its plan may miss a chance that nobody expected. Plans are guides, and they should bend when the facts bend.^1

^1: Some writers describe this danger as people becoming loyal to the plan and forgetting the goal that the plan was made to serve.

> The fourth limit is that planning can delay action.

The fourth limit is delay. A manager may keep studying and never decide, hoping for more certainty. By the time the plan is ready, the chance may be gone. There is a point beyond which more study adds little and waiting costs much.

> The fifth limit is that plans cannot cover everything.

The fifth limit is incompleteness. No plan covers every event. Staff will meet cases that the plan never imagined. The firm therefore needs people who can use judgement when the plan is silent.

### Why Plans Fail

> Plans fail for reasons that a careful manager can often prevent.

The limits above are built into planning. Plans also fail for reasons that lie within the manager's power. We name six of them.

> Reason one: the goals are unclear.

The first reason is unclear goals. If the people who must carry out the plan cannot say what the goal is, they cannot work toward it. The plan then wanders. A manager should be able to state each goal in one plain sentence.

> Reason two: the premises are wrong or never examined.

The second reason is false premises. A plan is only as sound as the beliefs under it. If the owner assumed a steady price of flour and the price rose sharply, the budget fails. The cure is to state premises openly, to check them from time to time, and to keep watching the outside world.

> Reason three: the people who carry out the plan did not help to make it.

The third reason is lack of involvement. A plan made in the office and handed down to the workers is often misunderstood or resisted. The people closest to the work know things that planners may not know. When they help to make the plan, they understand it and they feel it is their own.

> Reason four: the plan lacks resources or support.

The fourth reason is missing resources. A plan to bake 300 loaves needs ovens, flour, staff and money. If any one is missing, the plan stops. Managers must check, before they commit, that the necessary resources can be found.

> Reason five: the plan is not followed up.

The fifth reason is the lack of follow-up. A plan that nobody checks drifts. Chapter XIII shows that controlling is the other half of planning, and a plan without control is a wish.

> Reason six: the plan is too rigid or too weak.

The sixth reason is the wrong balance. A plan that is too rigid cannot adapt. A plan that is too loose gives no direction. The art of the manager lies in a plan that is firm about the goal and flexible about the route.

> We can collect the limits and failures in a table.

The next table lists the common causes of failure, with a short cure for each. Read each row as a problem and its remedy.

:table Common causes of failure and their remedies
| Cause of failure | What goes wrong | A remedy |
| Unclear goals | People do not know where to go | State each goal in one plain sentence |
| False premises | The plan rests on a wrong belief | Write premises down and check them |
| No involvement | People resist or misunderstand | Ask those who do the work to help plan |
| Missing resources | The plan stops half way | Check resources before committing |
| No follow-up | The plan drifts | Compare results with the plan regularly |
| Wrong balance | Too rigid or too loose | Fix the goal, keep the route flexible |

> You should now be able to give limits and causes of failure.

You should now be able to say this: planning is limited by an unknown future, by cost, by rigidity, by delay and by incompleteness, and plans commonly fail through unclear goals, false premises, lack of involvement, missing resources, no follow-up and the wrong balance between firmness and flexibility.

## Making Planning Effective

> Effective planning follows a few simple conditions.

If we turn the causes of failure upside down, we find the conditions for success. They are few and they are practical.

> First, make sure that planning has the support of top managers.

First, planning needs the support of top managers. When the owner or the senior team takes planning seriously, others do the same. When top managers ignore their own plans, nobody else will respect them.

> Second, state goals and premises in plain words and share them.

Second, goals and premises should be stated in plain words and shared with all who need them. A plan that stays in one head cannot guide ten people.

> Third, involve the people who will carry out the plan.

Third, involve the people who will do the work. Consult them while the plan is being made. They will improve it, and they will support it.

> Fourth, keep plans simple and linked.

Fourth, keep plans simple. A plan that nobody can understand will not be used. The plans of different levels and departments should also fit together, so that the operational plans serve the tactical plans and the tactical plans serve the strategy.

> Fifth, build in flexibility.

Fifth, allow for change. Good planners ask what they will do if a key premise proves false. A simple second plan, ready in advance, can save a firm that would otherwise be caught unprepared.

> Sixth, check results and revise the plan.

Sixth, check results and revise. Planning is never finished. The manager compares what happens with what was planned, learns from the gap, and plans again with better knowledge.

### Planning in Small and Large Firms

> Small firms plan too, though often in the owner's head.

Beginners sometimes think that planning belongs only to large firms with planning departments. This is not so. A small firm plans, but the planning is often informal. The owner keeps the plan in mind and talks it over with a few people. This works while the firm is small and the owner knows every detail. As the firm grows, the plan must be written, because one head can no longer hold it, and because new staff cannot read the owner's thoughts.

> A written plan helps a small firm to borrow, to hire and to grow.

A written plan has other uses for a small firm. A bank that is asked for a loan will wish to see one. A new manager will use it to understand the firm's direction. And the owner, in writing it, will find weak places that were hidden when the plan existed only in thought. For these reasons, even the smallest firm gains from putting its main plans on paper.

> The plans of a large firm are more formal and more numerous.

In a large firm, planning is shared among many people and often has its own staff, who collect information and help the departments to plan. The main danger there is distance: the planners may not know the work, and the workers may not know the plans. The conditions we listed apply with even greater force. Goals must be shared, and the people who do the work must be heard.

> A final test helps a manager to judge whether to plan at all.

A last question remains: when is a plan not worth making? Where the matter is small, where the cost of planning exceeds the possible gain, or where a quick action is needed and a delay would cause harm, the manager may reasonably act at once. But where the matter is large, repeated or costly if wrong, planning pays for itself many times.

## A Worked Case: A Six-Month Plan at the Bakery

> The case applies the whole planning process to the bakery's sixth month.

This case applies the six steps to one situation. It has five parts: the situation, the question, the reasoning, the conclusion and a check.

> The case states the facts first.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Month 6. The Morning Loaf Bakery sells a loaf at Tk 70. Ingredients cost about Tk 24 a loaf when flour is Tk 60 a kilogram, and each loaf uses 0.24 kilograms of flour. Flour now costs Tk 69 a kilogram. The shop trades 26 days a month and pays Tk 40,000 monthly rent. The owner expects flour to rise by about 5 per cent and demand to rise by about 8 per cent over months 7 to 12. A festival will bring a week of heavy orders. The owner wishes to plan for 300 loaves a day by month 12.

> The question asks for the steps of the plan.

*Question.* How should the owner plan months 7 to 12? What premises, what standing plan, what single-use plan and what budget does the plan need?

> The reasoning takes the steps in order.

*Reasoning.* Step one: the owner has noticed three things, which are rising demand, dearer flour and a festival. Step two: the goal is 300 loaves a day by the end of month 12. Step three: the premises are that flour will rise by about 5 per cent, so that it costs about Tk 72.45 a kilogram, and that demand will rise by about 8 per cent. Step four: the alternatives are a second oven, longer hours or buying dough elsewhere. Step five: the owner weighs the three, a task that Chapter VIII treats in detail, and carries the choice forward. Step six: the owner makes supporting plans, and we describe three of them.

> The first supporting plan is a standing plan for hygiene.

A standing plan is needed for hygiene. As output rises, more people handle more bread, and mistakes become more likely. The owner writes a hygiene procedure of four steps: wash hands, put on a clean apron, clean the table, and begin work. A rule is added: nobody enters the baking room without a head covering. Both will be used every day.

> The second supporting plan is a single-use plan for the festival.

A single-use plan is needed for the festival week. The owner writes a festival-week order schedule that says how much extra dough to prepare on each day, when extra flour must arrive, and who works late. It will be used once and then put away.

> The third supporting plan is a budget.

A budget is needed in numbers. At 300 loaves a day for 26 days, the shop will bake 7,800 loaves a month. At Tk 70 a loaf, sales will be 7,800 × 70 = Tk 546,000. Flour at the new price costs 0.24 × 72.45 = about Tk 17.39 a loaf. The other ingredients cost 24 − 14.4 = Tk 9.60. Ingredients are therefore about Tk 27 a loaf, which gives 7,800 × 27 = about Tk 210,600 a month. Rent stays at Tk 40,000.

> The conclusion states the plan in a few words.

*Conclusion.* The plan has a goal of 300 loaves a day by month 12, two stated premises, one standing plan with a procedure and a rule, one single-use plan for the festival, and a budget that shows sales of about Tk 546,000, ingredients of about Tk 210,600 and rent of Tk 40,000 a month at full output.

> The check tests the premise against the budget.

*Check.* We test the premise. If flour were to rise by 10 per cent instead of 5, it would cost Tk 75.90 a kilogram and Tk 18.22 a loaf. Ingredients would then be 18.22 + 9.60 = about Tk 27.8 a loaf, or about Tk 217,000 a month. The difference from the plan is about Tk 6,500 a month, which the budget can bear. The plan survives a wrong premise of this size, and the owner now knows how far the premise may be wrong before the plan must change.

## Summary

> The summary restates the main ideas in a few lines.

A plan is a chosen route to a goal, and planning is the continuing work of choosing it. Planning gives direction, prepares for change, cuts waste, makes control possible and encourages forward thinking. Plans are sorted by level into strategic, tactical and operational, and by use into standing plans (policy, procedure, rule) and single-use plans (programme, project, budget).

> The process has six steps and rests on premises.

The planning process has six steps: find the need, set the goal, state the premises, list the alternatives, weigh and choose, and make supporting plans. After the plan comes action, and then a check of results. A planning premise is an assumption about the future that the plan rests on. Plans fail through unclear goals, false premises, lack of involvement, missing resources, no follow-up and the wrong balance of firmness and flexibility. They succeed when these causes are removed.

## Where People Go Wrong

> A first error is to confuse a plan with a goal.

*Error:* A goal and a plan are the same thing. *Correction:* The goal is the result to reach. The plan is the chosen route to it.

> A second error is to treat a premise as a fact.

*Error:* A premise is something we know to be true. *Correction:* A premise is an assumption about the future. It may prove wrong, and the plan should say what will be done if it does.

> A third error is to mix up a policy, a procedure and a rule.

*Error:* A policy, a procedure and a rule are three names for one thing. *Correction:* A policy guides judgement, a procedure gives steps in order, and a rule is a single fixed instruction.

> A fourth error is to think that a plan, once made, must not change.

*Error:* A good plan is never changed. *Correction:* A good plan is firm about the goal and flexible about the route. When premises change, the plan should change.

## In History

> A national goal set in 1961 shows planning at a very large scale.

In May 1961 the American president, John F. Kennedy, told his country's lawmakers that the nation should commit itself, before the decade was out, to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to Earth. The goal was clear and carried a date. The space agency then built the plan in stages. It flew crews in orbit, practised meeting and joining spacecraft in space, and tested each part before the next. In July 1969 the Apollo 11 mission landed two astronauts on the Moon, within the time set.^2 The case shows a firm goal, a plan made of many linked smaller plans, and the careful testing of each step.

## Sources

> The idea that managers plan first comes from Fayol's work.

Fayol, whose work Chapter III described, placed planning first among the elements of management and stressed the value of a written plan of action in his book of 1916. The ideas of strategic, tactical and operational plans, and of standing and single-use plans, were developed by later management writers in the middle of the twentieth century.
