## Why Did They Leave?

> Two people left a busy bakery, and the owner wants to know why.
:case Case file, illustrative. Morning Loaf Bakery, Month 11. In the last six months, 2 of the 8 staff have left, a loss of 25%. During the month the owner starts a team bonus of Tk 1,000 for waste of 5% or less, stops checking every tray, and accepts a helper's idea for a milk bread: 40 loaves a day at Tk 80.

> A manager who guesses the cause of behaviour will often act on the wrong cause.
Suppose you were the owner. Two people you trained have gone. The shop is busy, the oven runs all day, and customers return. Your first thought may be the wage. You could raise it for everyone. But you would be guessing, and a wrong guess costs money and changes nothing. Perhaps the two wanted something the wage cannot buy: to be trusted, to learn a new skill, to belong to a team that valued them. Perhaps they did not want anything different at all, and left for a shorter journey to work.

> Tools for the work help only if people use them willingly and well.
Every manager meets this puzzle sooner or later. Chapters 6 to 10 gave you tools for the work itself: plans, objectives, choices, structure and authority. Those tools are useful only if people use them willingly and well. The word *people* then raises a harder question than any chart can answer. What makes a person put effort into a task, keep it up, and aim it at the goals of the organisation?

> This chapter studies what drives effort, in individuals and in groups.
The chapter answers in six steps. Section 11.1 looks at what managers assume about the people they lead. Section 11.2 explains what starts effort. Section 11.3 describes the best-known theories of human need. Section 11.4 adds three theories about how people choose where to put their effort. Section 11.5 turns from the individual to the group, and section 11.6 shows where new ideas come from and how they reach use.

## 11.1 Human Factors and Assumptions about People

> A worker is a whole person, not only a pair of hands.
@ Human factors | The personal qualities, needs and beliefs that shape how a person behaves at work.
When a bakery hires a baker, it hires more than the baker's hands. It also takes in the person's skill, health, hopes, worries, family duties and opinions of the owner. These are the **human factors** of work: the personal qualities, needs and beliefs that shape how a person behaves at work. Two bakers with equal skill and equal pay can behave very differently, because their human factors differ. One may value a steady wage most. The other may value praise and the chance to learn.

> People differ in ability, in what they want, and in how they see the same event.
Three facts about human factors matter to a manager. First, people differ. The same instruction, wage or word of thanks does not reach two people in the same way. Second, a person is also a social being, who cares about friends, status and respect at work. Third, behaviour usually has more than one cause. When a worker arrives late, the cause may be distance, health, boredom or a quarrel, and the manager must find which before acting.

> Two people can see the same event and judge it very differently.
Consider one event. The owner moves a baker from the early shift to the late shift. One baker sees a sign of trust, because the late shift pays a little more and the closer holds the keys. Another baker sees a punishment, because the family dinner is now lost. The event is the same, and the meaning is different. To predict how a person will respond, a manager must ask how the person sees the event, and not only how the manager sees it.

> Earlier writers saw workers mainly as labour, and later writers saw them as people.
Ideas about workers have changed over time. In the early years of scientific management, described in Chapter 3, the worker was seen mainly as a source of labour who answered to pay and clear methods. Later research, including the studies described at the end of this chapter, showed that workers also respond to attention, friendship and a sense of being valued. The human relations approach grew from these findings. Its lesson does not cancel the earlier one. Pay and method still matter, and people matter as well. A modern manager uses both lessons at once.

> Before you manage people, you hold some beliefs about them, whether or not you notice.
Behind every managerial action lies a belief about what people are like. A manager who locks the flour store and counts every sack believes something about the staff. A manager who leaves the key with the head baker believes something different. In 1960 the American writer Douglas McGregor, in his book *The Human Side of Enterprise*, gave names to two such sets of belief.

> Theory X sees workers as unwilling and so leads to close control.
@ Theory X | The belief that people dislike work, avoid duty and must be directed and controlled.
**Theory X** is a set of assumptions that holds that the average person dislikes work, avoids responsibility when possible, and works hard only when watched, directed or threatened. A manager who holds Theory X sets tight rules, checks work often, and uses penalties and fixed rewards. The manager is not cruel. The manager believes that without such pressure little would be done.

> Theory Y sees workers as willing and so leads to trust and shared goals.
@ Theory Y | The belief that people can enjoy work, accept duty and direct themselves toward goals they share.
**Theory Y** is the opposite set of assumptions. It holds that effort at work is as natural as rest or play, that people direct themselves toward goals they accept, and that most people can learn to seek responsibility. It also holds that ideas and imagination are found widely, not only among managers. A manager who holds Theory Y agrees goals with the staff, passes down authority, and judges results rather than watching every movement.

> Theory X links to close control and Theory Y to agreed goals and delegation.
The two theories connect to earlier chapters. A Theory X manager is drawn to the one best method and the close supervision of scientific management in Chapter 3, and to authority held at the top, as in Chapter 10, section 10.5. A Theory Y manager is drawn to the agreed objectives of Chapter 7 and to the delegation of Chapter 10, section 10.3. The chapters on structure and authority describe tools, and this chapter describes the belief that decides how the tools are used.

> The table sets the two sets of assumptions side by side.
The two sets of assumptions lead to different habits. The table that follows sets them side by side. Read each row from left to right: the first column names a feature of management, and the other two show what a manager holding each theory tends to believe or do.

:table Two sets of assumptions about people at work
| Feature | Theory X | Theory Y |
| View of work | A burden to be avoided | As natural as rest or play |
| Source of effort | Pressure, reward and punishment | Goals the person accepts |
| Attitude to duty | Avoided if possible | Accepted and often sought |
| Use of ideas | Few people have useful ideas | Many people have useful ideas |
| Typical style | Close checking, tight rules | Agreed goals, delegation |

> The theories describe beliefs held by managers, not two kinds of worker, and a belief can bring about the behaviour it expects.
Here lies the first exception, and many students miss it. McGregor did not say that workers are of two kinds. He said that managers hold one of two sets of belief, and that the belief shapes what the manager does. A further point follows. Beliefs can make themselves true. A manager who treats staff as unwilling, with close checking and no trust, may find that staff do the minimum, which seems to confirm the belief. A manager who trusts may find that staff respond with care.

> The same manager looks different to the worker, the customer and the owner.
The two sets of belief also look different from other seats. A worker under Theory X sees a manager who does not trust, and may answer with careful but minimal effort. A worker under Theory Y sees a manager who trusts, and may feel a duty to deserve that trust. A customer sees the effect at the counter: a team told to follow a rule exactly may refuse to correct a small error on the spot, while a team trusted to use judgement may correct it. The owner sees the result in the monthly figures for waste and sales.

> Theory Y does not mean freedom for every person at every time.
Theory Y is not a gift of freedom to everyone at all times. A new helper who has never baked may need clear and close instruction at first, because freedom without skill produces waste. The choice of how much to direct depends on the person and the task, a point Chapter 12 develops. McGregor's lasting lesson is smaller and more useful: examine your assumptions, because your methods follow from them.

> The bakery owner moves from checking trays to agreed targets.
:case Case file, illustrative. Until Month 11 the owner checks every tray before it goes to the counter. In Month 11 the owner replaces this check with agreed targets: waste of 5% or less and on-time orders of 95% or more, set with each member of staff as in Chapter 7. The change moves from a Theory X habit toward a Theory Y habit. The targets remain in place, so control has not been given up; it has changed its form.

> A short recap of section 11.1.
*Before moving on.* Human factors differ from person to person. Theory X assumes people avoid work, and Theory Y assumes they can direct themselves. A manager's assumptions shape the methods the manager chooses.

## 11.2 Motivation: Meaning and the Needs-Goal Model

@! Go slowly here. One idea at a time.
> The hunger story has five parts that will return in a model.
It is eleven in the morning and you are studying. You have eaten nothing since the evening before. At first you notice little. Then your stomach feels empty and your thoughts drift. The feeling grows until reading is hard. You stand up, walk to the canteen, and eat. The feeling fades, your thoughts return to the page, and you read on. Nobody gave you an order. Something inside you started a chain of events and ended it. Keep this small story in mind, because the whole of this section is built on it.

> Motivation is an inner push, visible only through what people do.
@ Motivation | The push that starts and keeps a person's effort going toward a goal.
In management this chain has a name. **Motivation** means the push that starts a person's effort and keeps it going toward a goal. In the story, hunger was the push, and the goal was a meal. At work, the push might be the wish to earn a wage, to be praised, or to finish a hard task well. A manager cannot see motivation. A manager sees only its results: a person who works with care, or one who does not.

> A need is felt as a gap, and the gap is uncomfortable.
@ Need | Word in use. In daily life, something you must have to live. In management, a gap a person feels between what they have and what they want.
The push begins with a need. In daily life, a *need* is something you must have in order to live. In management, a **need** is a gap that a person feels between what the person has and what the person wants. Hunger is a need for food. The wish to be noticed is a need for recognition. In both cases the person feels the gap, and the gap is uncomfortable.

> The model has five steps: need, drive, action, goal and relief.
@ Needs-goal model | A chain of five steps: need, drive, action, goal, relief.
@ Drive | The inner tension that a need creates and that pushes a person to act.
The **needs-goal model** shows how a need leads to effort and then to rest. It has five steps. First, a person feels a *need*. Second, the need creates a **drive**, which means an inner tension that pushes the person to act. Third, the person takes *action*. Fourth, the action reaches a *goal*, which means the result that the person wanted. Fifth, the goal brings *relief*, which means the tension fades. The figure shows the five steps in order.

:fig The needs-goal model: a need creates a drive, the drive leads to action, and action reaches a goal that brings relief | fig_11_1_needs_goal.svg

> Relief is brief, and so the chain begins again with a new need.
The figure also shows an arrow from relief back to the start. It is there because relief does not last. Hunger returns after some hours, and the wish for praise returns after a few weeks. A satisfied need stops pushing, but a new need soon takes its place. This is why motivation is never finished. A manager who solved one need today will meet the next tomorrow.

> The same five steps explain a baker's wish for recognition.
Now place the model in a bakery. A young baker wishes to be seen as skilled. That is the need. The wish makes the baker restless, which is the drive. The baker takes action: shapes each loaf with extra care for the festival order. The goal is reached when the owner notices and says so. Relief follows, and for some weeks the baker is content. Then a new need arises, perhaps to learn a harder recipe, and the chain starts again.

> A blocked goal causes frustration, which can lead to anger or withdrawal.
@ Frustration | The discomfort a person feels when a goal is blocked.
What if the goal cannot be reached? Suppose the owner never notices the baker's care. The drive remains, with nowhere to go. The person feels **frustration**, which means the discomfort of a blocked goal. Some people respond well: they look for a different route, such as showing the work to a customer. Others respond badly. They may become angry, withdraw from the team, or give up. A manager who sees these signs should ask what goal has been blocked.^1

^1: Psychologists use other terms for these responses. The simple division into constructive and defensive responses is enough for this book.

> Test your use of the model on a new case before moving on.
*Pause and check 1.* A counter worker wants to be trusted with the cash till. The owner never allows it. Which step of the model is blocked, and what may the worker do next?

> Rewards from the work itself and rewards from outside meet different needs.
@ Intrinsic reward | A reward that comes from the work itself, such as pride.
@ Extrinsic reward | A reward that comes from outside the work, such as pay.
Two more ideas complete the picture. A reward that comes from the work itself, such as pride in a well-shaped loaf, is an **intrinsic reward**. A reward that comes from outside the work, such as pay or a bonus, is an **extrinsic reward**. Both can relieve a need, but they relieve different needs. Pay meets the need for food and shelter. Pride meets the need to feel skilled. This difference will matter when you ask, at the end of the chapter, whether money alone can motivate.

> A manager cannot see a need, but can learn it by asking, watching and listening.
Since a need cannot be seen, how does a manager find it? Three habits help. The manager can ask, in a private and unhurried talk, what the person values in the work. The manager can watch, and notice what makes a person brighter or more silent. And the manager can listen to what the person says about colleagues and tasks, because complaints often point to a blocked goal. None of these gives certainty, but together they give a better guess than one made at a desk. A person may also hide a need, such as the wish for status, so the manager should be patient.

> A met need no longer pushes, so the same reward can fail the second time.
Consider what happens to a reward over time. The owner gives a baker a rise, and for some weeks the baker is pleased. Then the baker takes the new wage for granted, and its power to push fades. This is the last step of the model at work, since relief ends the drive. A manager who relies on one reward must keep raising it, and the cost grows. A manager who learns the person's open needs can offer different rewards over time, such as a new task, a new skill or a new title, and gain more for less.

> Results depend on motivation, ability and means together.
Finally, keep motivation apart from two things that resemble it. Motivation is not the same as ability: a highly motivated person who has never baked still cannot make bread of high quality. Nor is it the same as means: an eager baker cannot work if the oven is broken. The result of work depends on all three, and a manager who blames low motivation for every poor result will often miss the true cause.

> The same chain looks different from the seat of the employee, owner and customer.
The same chain can be seen from three seats. For the employee, it is a felt gap and a wish to close it. For the owner, it is a question of whether the closing of that gap also serves the bakery. For the customer, the result appears as the care in the loaf and the manner at the counter. A manager succeeds when the employee's way of closing the gap, and the bakery's aims, point the same way. The rest of the chapter returns to this idea again and again.

> A one-line test of the motivation passage.
*You should now be able to say:* "Motivation is the push that starts and keeps effort. It follows five steps: need, drive, action, goal and relief, and then a new need begins."

> A short recap of section 11.2.
*Before moving on.* A need is a felt gap. A drive is the tension it creates. Action aims at a goal, relief follows, and a new need soon arises. A blocked goal causes frustration.

## 11.3 Need Theories: Maslow and Others

> Maslow's 1943 paper is the starting point for most later need theories.
The needs-goal model shows how any need works. It does not say which needs people have. In 1943 the American psychologist Abraham Maslow published a paper called "A Theory of Human Motivation" that gave an answer. He was studying human nature in general, not factories, but managers soon found his ideas useful. The theory remains one of the best-known accounts of human need, and many later theories are compared with it.

@! Go slowly here. Five levels, one at a time.
> The hierarchy has five levels arranged from basic to advanced.
@ Hierarchy of needs | Maslow's five levels of need, from basic survival at the base to personal growth at the top.
Maslow's idea is called the **hierarchy of needs**: five levels of need arranged one above another, from the most basic to the most advanced. The levels are physiological, safety, social, esteem and self-actualisation. The figure shows them as a stepped pyramid, with the most basic level at the base. Beside each level the figure gives an example from a bakery.

:fig Maslow's hierarchy of needs, with a bakery example beside each level | fig_11_2_maslow.svg

> The two lowest levels are survival and safety.
The first level holds the *physiological* needs: food, water, rest and shelter. At work these are met by a wage large enough to live on, and by breaks. The second holds the *safety* needs: freedom from danger and from sudden loss. At work they are met by a steady job, safe equipment and clear rules. A baker who fears a burn from a faulty oven, or fears dismissal without notice, is thinking about this level.

> The upper levels are belonging, respect and personal growth.
@ Self-actualisation | The wish to use and grow one's abilities as far as one can.
The third level holds the *social* needs: to belong, to have friends, and to be accepted. At work these are met by a friendly team and a place at the table. The fourth holds the *esteem* needs: to be respected by others and to respect oneself. They are met by praise, a fair title and real responsibility. The fifth holds the need for **self-actualisation**, which means the wish to use and grow one's abilities as far as one can. A baker who invents a new recipe may be at this level.

> Unmet needs push, lower needs come first, and the levels overlap.
Maslow's theory rests on three rules. The first rule is that an unmet need motivates, and a met need does not. A baker with a good wage is not moved by the offer of more wage. The second rule is that lower needs come first: a person who is hungry or afraid has little interest in praise. The third rule is that the levels are not fully separate. A person can be partly satisfied at one level and already be pushed by the next.

> The order of the levels is a general tendency, not a law for each person.
The theory has limits, and a good manager knows them. Research has not found that people always climb the levels in the stated order. Some people accept hunger for a cause they value, and an artist may create while poor. Cultures also differ in what they rank first; in some, belonging to family and community can outrank personal respect. Maslow himself described the order as a general tendency, not a fixed law. Treat the hierarchy as a useful map, not as a rule for every person.^2

^2: Studies of Maslow's theory in workplaces have given mixed results. Verify the current state of the research before relying on a stronger claim.

> Each level calls for a different kind of action from the manager.
The hierarchy can be turned into a short list of managerial actions. For the physiological level, pay a fair wage and allow rest breaks. For safety, give clear terms of work, keep equipment in good repair and avoid sudden changes. For belonging, build teams and allow time for people to know one another. For esteem, give honest praise, a fair title and real responsibility. For self-actualisation, offer new tasks that stretch a person, and training that opens a path. The list is a guide to what to try, not a promise that each action will work for each person.

> The lower levels may stay open longer where one wage supports many people.
Context changes which levels are open. In a country where many families depend on a single wage, a manager should expect the first two levels to matter strongly, and for longer, than in a place where each household has several earners. This does not mean that higher needs are absent. A baker who worries about next month's rent may still care deeply about respect. It means that a manager should not offer praise as a substitute for a fair and steady wage.

> The practical lesson is to match the reward to the level now open.
The use of the theory for a manager is practical: find the level at which a person is now pushed, and offer a reward that fits it. Consider a call centre where agents take forty calls a day from a fixed script. Pay is fair and the building is safe. Yet several agents leave each month. The theory suggests that the lower levels are met, and that the missing items are belonging and esteem. Rotating agents into small teams, and letting them handle hard calls in their own words, would aim at the levels that are open. A pay rise would aim at a level already met.

> Apply the levels to a baker before moving on.
*Pause and check 2.* A baker has a steady job, a good wage and many friends in the team. At which level is the baker most likely to be pushed now, and what could the owner offer?

> A one-line test of the Maslow passage.
*You should now be able to say:* "There are five levels of need, from survival to self-actualisation. An unmet need pushes a person, a met need does not, and a manager should offer the reward that fits the level now open."

> Herzberg separates items that prevent discontent from items that build effort.
@ Hygiene factor | An item whose absence causes discontent but whose presence does not drive effort.
@ Motivator | An item that raises effort and satisfaction, such as recognition or interesting work.
Other writers changed or added to Maslow's picture. Three are worth knowing. Frederick Herzberg and his colleagues, in a book of 1959, reported that the things which prevent unhappiness at work are not the same as the things which create real effort. He called pay, working conditions, supervision and company rules **hygiene factors**: items whose absence causes discontent, but whose presence does not by itself drive effort. He called achievement, recognition, interesting work, responsibility and growth **motivators**: items that raise effort and satisfaction. Herzberg's lesson is that a manager must supply both kinds. A good wage removes a complaint, while meaningful work builds the push.

> A bakery shows the two kinds of factor in action.
A bakery shows the difference. A broken oven, a late wage or a rude supervisor causes complaints. When these are repaired, the complaints stop, but the staff are not yet eager. Eagerness grows from other sources: a baker who is trusted with a new recipe, a counter worker who is thanked by a regular customer, a helper whose suggestion is used. Herzberg's critics note that his method relied on what people said about past events, and that some people place pay among the motivators. Treat the two lists as a useful guide, not a final law.

> Alderfer and McClelland changed the number and kind of needs.
Clayton Alderfer, writing in the early 1970s, reduced the five levels to three: needs of existence, of relatedness and of growth. Existence covers the first two of Maslow's levels, relatedness covers belonging and respect from others, and growth covers self-respect and self-actualisation. Alderfer differed from Maslow in one important way. He said that a person blocked at a higher level may return to a lower one. A baker refused promotion may start to care more about pay. David McClelland, in work from the 1950s and 1960s, argued that people learn three strong needs: for *achievement*, for *affiliation* and for *power*. Chapter 12 returns to power. The table compares the four theories.

:table Four need theories compared
| Writer | Main idea | Kinds of need | Lesson for a manager |
| Maslow | Needs rise through five levels | Five levels | Offer the reward that fits the open level |
| Herzberg | Two sets of factors | Hygiene and motivators | Remove complaints, then build real interest |
| Alderfer | Three groups; people may move down | Existence, relatedness, growth | Expect a return to lower needs when blocked |
| McClelland | Three learned needs | Achievement, affiliation, power | Match the job to the person's strongest need |

> Alderfer and McClelland suggest how to fit jobs to people.
Both writers give a practical guide. If a baker's need for growth is blocked, Alderfer would expect the baker to turn back to pay and to friends until growth is possible again, so a manager who sees a sudden interest in pay should ask whether growth has been blocked. McClelland would expect a person with a strong need for achievement to like tasks with a clear target and quick feedback, such as a daily count of waste. A person with a strong need for affiliation may prefer work in a pair or a small team, and may dislike tasks that set the person apart from colleagues.

> The four theories agree that people differ, and that a manager must find the open need.
The four theories do not agree on the number of needs or on whether they follow an order. They do agree on a central point: a manager who offers the same reward to everyone will motivate few people. Chapter 7 gave one practical use of this point. When targets are agreed one by one with each person, as in management by objectives, the manager has a chance to learn which need each person is trying to meet.^3

^3: The cross-reference is to the MBO cycle in Chapter 7, section 7.4.

> A plan of pay and recognition should reach several levels at once.
Now combine the theories in one plan. Suppose the owner wishes to design a plan of pay and recognition for the bakery's 8 staff. The plan should reach every level of Maslow's hierarchy, and should supply both hygiene factors and motivators. The wage and written terms of work serve the first two levels, and they are hygiene factors. A monthly team lunch serves the social level. A monthly title, such as baker of the month, serves esteem. One hour each Friday, in which a baker may try a new recipe, serves self-actualisation. The last three are motivators.

> A single reward reaches few levels, and each person should be asked what is valued.
An owner who offered only a higher wage would reach two levels and ignore three. The plan also has costs, in the price of the lunch and in the hour of lost baking time, and the owner must weigh them against the gain. Before adopting it, the owner should ask each person which of the rewards the person values, because the theories describe tendencies, and people differ.

> A short recap of section 11.3.
*Before moving on.* Maslow named five levels of need, and unmet needs push people. Herzberg separated hygiene factors from motivators. Alderfer allowed a return to lower needs, and McClelland named three learned needs.

## 11.4 Process Theories in Brief

> Process theories study how people choose, not only what they want.
@ Process theory | A theory that explains how a person decides where to put effort.
Section 11.3 asked what people want. A second group of theories asks a different question: how does a person decide where to put effort? These are the **process theories** of motivation, which explain the thinking by which a person chooses to work hard, or not. Three are brief enough to take together. Each gives the manager a question to ask.

> Effort needs three beliefs: I can do it, it will be rewarded, and I want the reward.
The first theory is *expectancy*, set out by the American writer Victor Vroom in 1964. It says that a person works hard only when three beliefs hold together. The person believes that effort will lead to good performance. The person believes that good performance will lead to a reward. And the person values the reward. If any one belief is weak, effort falls. A baker may know that a bonus is offered for low waste, yet doubt that the oven is reliable enough to reach the target. The first belief fails, and the bonus changes nothing.

> A small example shows how one weak belief can lower effort.
Take a small example, with each belief rated from 0 (none) to 1 (full). A baker rates the belief that effort will reach the waste target at 0.9, the belief that reaching it will bring the bonus at 0.9, and the value of the bonus at 0.2, because Tk 125 is a small sum to this baker. Multiplying the three gives 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.2 = 0.162, or about 0.16. If the bonus mattered much more, and the baker rated its value at 0.9, the product would be 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.729, or about 0.73. The numbers are illustrations, because nobody can measure belief so exactly. They show why one weak link lowers the whole.

> People judge fairness by comparing their own give-and-get with another person's.
The second theory is *equity*, described by J. Stacy Adams in 1963. People compare what they give to the job, such as effort, skill and time, with what they receive, such as pay and praise. Then they compare this ratio with the ratio of a colleague. If the two seem equal, the person is content. If the person seems to give more for less, the person feels unfairly treated and may reduce effort, ask for more, or leave. Note that the comparison is made by the person, and a pay scale that the manager thinks fair may look unfair from another seat.

> Clear, hard and accepted goals raise effort, but too many goals scatter it.
The third theory is *goal-setting*, developed by Edwin Locke from 1968. It says that a clear and difficult goal, which the person accepts, produces more effort than a loose request such as "do your best". Feedback on progress adds to the effect. This links directly to Chapter 7: the agreed objectives of management by objectives are an application of the theory. The exceptions are plain. A goal that is too hard is dropped, a goal imposed without consent is resisted, and too many goals at once scatter effort.

> Behaviour that brings a pleasant result tends to be repeated.
@ Reinforcement | Following a behaviour with a result that makes the behaviour more likely to be repeated.
A fourth view is simpler still, and is linked with the American psychologist B. F. Skinner. **Reinforcement** means following a behaviour with a result that makes the person likely to repeat it. A baker who is thanked after cleaning the bench early is more likely to do so again. A result that the person finds unpleasant tends to reduce the behaviour. This view does not ask what a person feels. It asks what follows the behaviour. Its use for a manager is plain: reward the behaviour that you want, and do so soon after it happens. Its limit is also plain, because it pays little attention to how the person judges the reward or its fairness.

> Need theories say what to offer, and process theories say how to offer it.
The need theories and the process theories answer different questions, and a manager need not choose between them. Need theories help to decide what to offer. Process theories help to decide how to offer it. An owner who offers the right reward, but in a way that the staff find unfair or out of reach, will still fail. An owner who offers a fair and reachable reward that nobody wants will fail as well.

> Ask three questions: does effort pay, is it fair, and is the goal clear?
The three theories add to the need theories, and do not replace them. A manager can use them as three questions. Does the person believe that effort will pay? Does the person think the reward is fair? Is the goal clear and accepted? In the bakery, the team bonus of Tk 1,000 a month, if shared equally among 8 staff, gives each person Tk 125. Whether this changes behaviour depends on whether each person thinks the target is reachable, the share fair, and the sum worth having.

> A short recap of section 11.4.
*Before moving on.* Expectancy asks whether effort will pay. Equity asks whether the reward is fair compared with others. Goal-setting asks whether the goal is clear, hard and accepted.

## 11.5 Groups and Teams; Informal Groups

> Nobody told four bakery staff to eat lunch together, yet they do.
:case Case file, illustrative. Month 9: when the staff grew from 4 to 8, a lunch group formed among 4 of the staff. No order created it, and the chart of the bakery does not show it.

> People form groups to gain company, safety, standing and help with tasks.
Chapter 9 introduced the informal organisation: the friendships and habits that exist outside the chart. This section looks at the same facts from the side of the person. Why do people join groups? The reasons are old and strong. People need company, which is the social level of Maslow's hierarchy. They gain safety in numbers, because a group can protect its members. They gain standing from belonging to a respected group. And some tasks cannot be done alone.

> A team is a group with a joint goal and a shared duty for results.
@ Group | Two or more people who meet, influence one another and see themselves as belonging together.
@ Team | A group with joint goals, fitting skills and shared duty for the result.
A **group** means two or more people who meet, influence one another and see themselves as belonging together. Four friends who eat lunch together form a group. A **team** is a special kind of group: people who work together toward a joint goal, with skills that fit one another and a shared duty for the result. The bakery's production staff are a team, because the loaf cannot be made unless each does a part, and all answer for the quality of the bread.

> The bakery's production staff show the features of a team.

The bakery's production staff show these features. Their joint goal is a batch of good loaves on time. Their skills fit one another: the head baker mixes and judges, the two bakers shape and bake, and the helper cleans and carries. They share a duty, since a spoiled batch costs the whole shop. The 7 am briefing of Month 9 gives them a daily chance to confirm the goal. The lunch group has none of these features by design, yet it supports the team, because friends who trust one another at lunch tend to speak plainly at the bench.

> A group may have no shared purpose, but a team always has one.
The difference matters to a manager. A group may have no purpose beyond itself, and may pull in any direction. A team has one purpose, and the manager's task is to keep that purpose clear. Not every group is a team, and not every team works well. A team needs a clear goal, a mix of skills that fit the task, and members who trust one another enough to say when something is going wrong.

> Formal groups are created by order, and informal groups by choice.
@ Formal group | A group created by the organisation to do a task.
@ Informal group | A group that forms by the members' own choice, not by order.
Groups are also divided by how they begin. A **formal group** is created by the organisation to do a task, such as the production staff or a committee. An **informal group** forms by choice, not by order, from friendship, common interest or closeness at work. The lunch group is informal. Members of an informal group often belong also to a formal one, and the two overlap in every organisation.

> Norms are unwritten rules, and cohesion is the strength of the bond.
@ Norm | An unwritten rule about how members of a group should behave.
@ Cohesion | How strongly the members of a group feel bound to one another.
Informal groups develop two features that a manager should understand. The first is a set of **norms**, which means unwritten rules about how members should behave. A norm in the lunch group might be that nobody speaks of problems at the counter during the meal. The second is **cohesion**, which means how strongly members feel bound to the group and to one another. A group with strong cohesion pressures its members to follow its norms, and a member who breaks them may be left out.

> Members often follow group norms even against their own judgement.
Members of a cohesive group often follow its norms even when their own judgement differs. A new baker may slow down to match the pace of the others, because working faster would set the baker apart. A person who wishes to belong accepts the group's rules, and the price may be lower output. This is why targets agreed only with individuals may meet a quiet limit set by the group. A manager who talks with the group as well as with each person makes it more likely that the target is accepted by both.

> Informal groups help with belonging and news, but can resist change and limit output.
Informal groups bring benefits to the organisation. They meet social needs, they carry news faster than the chart can, and members help one another with work in ways no rule requires. They bring risks as well. A group may resist a change that the owner wants. Rumours can spread quickly. And if the norms of a cohesive group are against high output, members may hold one another back. The first Hawthorne researchers noticed this last effect among workers in a factory in the United States.^4

^4: The Hawthorne studies, at the Western Electric company in the United States, began in the 1920s. Verify dates, names and the later criticisms of the findings before this note is finalised.

> News spreads through informal groups faster than through notices.
@ Grapevine | The informal flow of news among people, outside the official channels.
Informal groups carry news by the **grapevine**, which means the informal flow of news among people outside the official channels. If the owner plans a change in shifts, the grapevine may carry a distorted version to every counter worker by lunch. What it carries is often accurate, though it is also often incomplete. A manager cannot close it, but can use it: tell the group the facts early and plainly, and the grapevine carries the facts.

> Teams fail through unclear goals, poor fit of skills, loose contact and no leader.
Teams fail for reasons that a manager can learn to see. The goal may be unclear, so each member works toward a different aim. The skills may not fit the task, so some members idle while others are overloaded. Size can matter as well: in a small team members know one another well, and as the team grows, contact thins and some members do less. And a team without any leader tends to drift. Chapter 12 studies leadership, and shows how a leader can keep a team pointed at its goal.

> The same group looks different to a member, the owner and the customer.
The informal group looks different from each seat. To a member, it is a place of belonging and a shield against pressure. To the owner, it is a source of help and a possible source of resistance. To the customer, it appears in the mood of the counter: a friendly group greets a regular customer by name, and a divided one does not. A manager who wishes to understand the group should try all three views before acting.

> A manager should work with an informal group, not order it to end.
How should a manager respond? Ordering such groups to end does not work, because the needs that created them remain. A better course has four parts. First, learn that the group exists and who its natural leader is. Second, speak openly to the group about changes before they happen. Third, align the group's goal with the goals of the organisation, for example by sharing a target. Fourth, treat the informal leader as a partner. A manager who works with the informal group gains a channel of information and, often, a source of support.

> Teams change over time, and early disagreement is normal.
A work team also changes as members come to know one another. At first they are polite and unsure of their roles. Disagreement often follows, as members test one another. Later the team settles its norms and begins to work smoothly. A manager who expects the early disagreement, and does not mistake it for failure, can guide the team through it. New members added later may restart the process.

> Test the group ideas on a new case before moving on.
*Pause and check 3.* The four staff in the lunch group disagree with the owner about a new shift plan. Name one risk and one benefit of this group for the owner.

> A short recap of section 11.5.
*Before moving on.* A group is people who influence one another. A team has a joint goal. Informal groups form by choice, create norms and cohesion, and can help or hinder the organisation.

## 11.6 Creativity and Innovation

> A helper had an idea, and the owner had to decide what to do with it.
:case Case file, illustrative. Month 11: a helper suggests a milk bread, 40 loaves a day at Tk 80. A plain loaf sells at Tk 70.

> Creativity makes an idea, and innovation puts it to use.
@ Creativity | Making new ideas.
@ Innovation | Putting a new idea to use.
Ideas do not arrive by order, yet every organisation lives on them. The bakery of Month 1 would not survive in Month 24 if it never changed its bread, its methods or its service. Two words are often used for the same thing, though they are different. **Creativity** means making new ideas, and **innovation** means putting a new idea to use. The helper showed creativity by thinking of a milk bread. The owner will show innovation only if the milk bread is baked, priced and sold.

> Ideas without use change nothing, and use without ideas only copies.
The difference is worth keeping. A drawer full of good ideas does not help a business. Many organisations have creative people yet little innovation, because the ideas die before they reach a customer. Others try to innovate without any fresh ideas, and copy what others have done. The best organisations manage both parts: they gather ideas, and they have a way of testing and using them.

> Creativity is a skill of many people, not only of artists.
Many people think that creativity belongs to artists or to rare geniuses. The helper who thought of a milk bread was not a genius. The helper knew the work well, saw a gap in what the customers could buy, and spoke up. Creativity grows from knowledge of the work and freedom to say what one sees. Most people have it to some degree, and organisations can raise it or lower it by how they treat people who speak.

> New ideas often follow four stages, and innovation adds a fifth.
Writers on creativity have long noticed a pattern in how new ideas come. The English writer Graham Wallas described four stages in 1926. In *preparation*, the person studies the problem and gathers facts. In *incubation*, the person leaves the problem aside, and the mind works on it unseen. In *illumination*, the new idea suddenly appears. In *verification*, the person tests the idea and finds out whether it works. Innovation then adds a fifth step: the verified idea is put to use. The figure shows the whole line.

:fig From creative idea to innovation: four stages of making an idea, then putting it to use | fig_11_3_creativity_innovation.svg

> Each stage of the idea needs a different kind of help from the manager.
The stages are not a strict recipe. Some ideas arrive in minutes and some take years. They are useful because they show the manager where to help. In preparation, give people time and information. In incubation, do not demand an answer at once. In verification, offer a safe way to test: a small trial, not a full launch. At the last step, innovation needs money, space in the plan, and someone to answer for the result.

> Ideas come from inside the bakery and from outside it.
Where do useful ideas come from? Inside the bakery they come from staff who handle the work every day, like the helper who suggested the milk bread. Outside, they come from customers who ask for something not on the shelf, from suppliers who offer a new flour, and from rivals whose changes can be studied. A manager who listens in all these places gains more ideas than one who waits for a flash of genius. A small habit, such as noting each customer request in a book and reading it each week, can be a steady source of ideas.

> Blame and rigid rules block ideas, while freedom and praise encourage them.
Certain features of an organisation block creativity. Fear of blame for a failed idea makes people keep quiet. Rules that cannot be questioned and workloads with no free time do the same. Other features help. These include freedom to try within agreed limits, praise for a good attempt even if it fails, and a habit of listening to ideas from every level. Notice the link with section 11.1. A manager who holds Theory Y believes that ideas are found widely, and the belief shows in these habits.

> Innovation may change the product or the process, and is often small.
Innovation also takes different forms. A *product* innovation changes what is sold, like the milk bread. A *process* innovation changes how it is made, like a new mixer or a better layout. Both can be small. In a small bakery, innovation seldom means a great invention. More often it means a small change that is tried, measured and kept or dropped. This is the form of innovation most managers will meet, and the form this chapter's case follows.

> Not every trial succeeds, and a manager must accept that.
Innovation always carries risk, because a new product may not sell. Chapter 8 showed how risk can be weighed with probabilities before a choice is made. But the manager must also decide how to treat a trial that fails. If the owner blames the helper for a milk bread that sells poorly, the staff learn to stop proposing ideas. If the owner studies why it failed and thanks the helper for trying, the staff learn that effort in new directions is safe. The first habit saves a small loss and costs many future ideas.

> A trial turns a hopeful idea into evidence.
An innovation must also be managed like any other change. A new product needs a plan, as Chapter 6 teaches, with objectives from Chapter 7 and a choice among alternatives from Chapter 8. Results of the trial are then compared with a standard, which is the work of Chapter 13. The owner might trial the milk bread for four weeks, count the loaves sold and wasted each day, and compare the waste with the 5% target. A trial of this kind turns a hopeful idea into evidence.

> A short recap of section 11.6.
*Before moving on.* Creativity makes new ideas, and innovation puts them to use. New ideas often pass through four stages, and each needs a different kind of help. A manager's beliefs about people shape how many ideas are heard.

## Worked Case: Why Two People Left

> Situation: two leavers, three new actions, and no stated reason for leaving.
:case Case file, illustrative. Situation. In six months, 2 of the 8 staff left, a loss of 25%. The owner introduced a monthly team bonus of Tk 1,000 for waste of 5% or less, stopped checking every tray, and accepted a helper's milk bread: 40 loaves a day at Tk 80. The case file does not record why the two left.

> Question: match each action to a need and decide the first step.
:case Case file, illustrative. Question. Which actions suit which needs, and what should the owner do first?

> First gather facts, because the cause of leaving is not recorded.
Reasoning, step 1. We begin with what the file does not say. It gives no reason for leaving, so the owner cannot yet know the open need. The theories of this chapter suggest what to ask. Was it a lower level, such as pay or a safe oven? Or a higher level, such as belonging or respect? The first step is therefore to ask the staff who remain, and, if possible, the two who left. The owner gathers information before acting.

> The bonus signals what matters, and the other two actions aim at esteem and growth.
Reasoning, step 2. We now place each action at a level of Maslow's hierarchy. The bonus is extrinsic. Shared equally, it gives each of the 8 staff Tk 1,000 ÷ 8 = Tk 125 a month. At this size it is more likely to signal that low waste matters than to meet a physiological need. Stopping the tray check and agreeing targets is a Theory Y step; it speaks to esteem, because it shows trust. Adopting the helper's milk bread speaks to esteem and to self-actualisation, because the helper's own idea reaches the customer.

> The three process theories test whether the plan can work.
Reasoning, step 3. Next we use the process theories. For expectancy, the staff must believe that waste of 5% or less is reachable. The file does not yet show whether the target can be reached, so the owner should watch the first results. For equity, the bonus is shared equally, which most members may judge fair. For goal-setting, the target is clear and measurable, and was agreed one by one, as Chapter 7 teaches.

> The milk bread brings Tk 83,200 a month in sales, not profit.
Reasoning, step 4. Last, the milk bread. At 40 loaves a day and Tk 80 each, daily sales are 40 × Tk 80 = Tk 3,200. Over 26 trading days the sales are Tk 3,200 × 26 = Tk 83,200 a month. This is a sales figure, not profit, because the costs of the new bread are not in the file. As an innovation, it should be tried first as a small batch, in line with the verification stage.

> Conclusion: ask first, keep the targets, and test the new bread on a small scale.
:case Case file, illustrative. Conclusion. The owner should ask first, then keep the agreed targets and the bonus as signals of trust and fairness. The milk bread should be tested as a small trial. If the answers show a lower-level need, such as pay or safety, the owner should respond at that level.

> Check: each action is matched to a need, and the figures are right.
Check. Does each action match a named need? Yes: the bonus (signal), the targets (esteem), the milk bread (esteem and growth). Is the arithmetic right? Tk 1,000 ÷ 8 = Tk 125. Tk 80 × 40 = Tk 3,200. Tk 3,200 × 26 = Tk 83,200. Does the answer claim more than the file allows? No: it states that the reason for leaving is unknown and that the sales figure is not profit.

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas, in short.
Managers hold beliefs about people. Theory X assumes people avoid work and need control; Theory Y assumes they can direct themselves. Motivation is the push that starts and keeps effort. It follows the needs-goal model: need, drive, action, goal and relief, and then a new need. Maslow's hierarchy gives five levels of need: physiological, safety, social, esteem and self-actualisation. Herzberg, Alderfer and McClelland added other views. Process theories ask how people choose: expectancy, equity and goal-setting. Groups form because people need company, safety, standing and help. Informal groups create norms and cohesion and may help or hinder. Creativity makes ideas, and innovation puts them to use.

:box IN SHORT
Effort begins in a need. A manager who finds the open need, offers a fitting reward, treats people as willing, and works with their groups gets better results than one who guesses.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake: money is all that motivates.
*"Motivation means money."* Money meets some needs, mainly at the lower levels, and signals worth. It cannot give belonging, trust or pride in the work.

> Mistake: the levels are a fixed ladder for everyone.
*"Maslow's levels are a strict ladder."* Maslow described a general tendency. Levels overlap, and people differ.

> Mistake: one theory is always right.
*"Theory X is bad and Theory Y is good."* Both are sets of belief. A new worker may need close direction, and an experienced one may need freedom. The right choice depends on the person and the task.

> Mistake: informal groups only cause trouble.
*"Informal groups are a threat."* They meet real needs and carry useful news. The risk lies in their norms, which a manager should learn.

> Mistake: the two words mean the same.
*"Creativity and innovation are the same."* Creativity makes an idea. Innovation puts it to use.

## In History

> A true account of how one study changed ideas about workers.
From the middle of the 1920s, researchers studied workers at the Hawthorne Works of the Western Electric company, near Chicago, in the United States. The first tests looked at the effect of lighting on output. Output rose when light was increased, but it also rose in other conditions, which puzzled the researchers. Later work, joined by Elton Mayo and colleagues from Harvard, pointed to the attention paid to the workers and to the pull of the group. Output depended on feelings, friendships and group norms as well as on conditions of work. This helped give rise to the human relations approach of Chapter 3. Later critics argued that the conclusions went beyond the data, and the studies are still debated. This chapter's lesson is smaller and still safe: people are social beings, and their groups matter.

## Sources

> Where the main ideas of this chapter come from.
