:chapter XIII | Controlling
:mood A plan is only a wish until someone checks what happened.
:desc Every plan can drift from its goal, a little each day. This chapter shows how a manager sets a standard, measures the result, compares the two, and corrects the gap. It describes three moments for control, the barriers that weaken it, and the way control joins planning, efficiency and power.

## A Plan Is Not Enough

> A bus driver watches the road, the clock and the fuel all the way.

Imagine a bus that leaves Dhaka for Rajshahi at six in the morning. The driver knows the plan: arrive at noon, by the main road, with one stop for rest. Does the driver hold the wheel straight and stop watching? No. The driver looks at the road, the clock, the fuel gauge and the speed. If a road is closed, the driver changes the route. Without these checks the plan would exist, but the bus might not arrive.

> A manager is in the driver's position: plans meet a changing world.

A manager is in the same position as the driver. In earlier chapters we learned to make plans and to set objectives. But a plan is only a statement about the future, and the future then arrives. Prices rise, workers fall ill, machines stop, and customers choose another shop. How does a manager learn that the plan is slipping? How soon must the manager learn it? What should be done next?

> At Month 12 the bakery has made 280 loaves a day, not 300.

:case Case file, illustrative. At the end of Month 12, the owner of Morning Loaf Bakery looks at the plan made in Months 6 and 7. The plan asked for 300 loaves a day. The shop now makes 280. Is that a small miss or a large one? Why did it happen? Should the owner change the work, or change the plan? The owner cannot answer these questions by feeling alone. The owner needs a method.

> This chapter gives the method, called control, and returns to the bakery at the end.

This chapter teaches that method. It is called control, and it is the last of the four functions that we met in Chapter 2. We shall learn what control is, how the process works step by step, when to apply it, and what weakens it. We shall then see how it fits with planning, efficiency and power. At the end we return to Morning Loaf and answer the owner's questions.

## What Control Is

### The Meaning of Control

> The everyday word control means holding power; in management it means checking and correcting.

@! Word in use. Control. In daily life, to hold power over a person or a thing. In management, to check results against plans and correct them.
@ Control | Checking results against plans and correcting any gap.

In daily English, to control a thing means to hold power over it. A driver controls a bus, and a parent controls a child's spending. In management the word has a narrower meaning. **Control** is the work of checking results against plans and correcting any gap between them. The manager does not try to hold power for its own sake. The manager tries to keep the work on the road that the plan has chosen.^1

^1: Fayol wrote in French. The French word for control, *contrôle*, carries the sense of checking and inspecting, which is close to the meaning used in this book.

> A family budget shows the whole idea in four small actions.

@! Go slowly here.

Let us see the idea in a family. A family plans to spend Tk 12,000 on food in one month. On the fifteenth day the mother counts the money spent, and the total is Tk 9,000. That is more than half of the plan after only half of the month. The mother does not ignore this fact. She buys less fish for the second half of the month and cooks more rice at home. In this small story four things happened. A plan existed. A result was counted. The result was compared with the plan. An action followed. These four things are the heart of control.

> Early writers on management already placed control beside planning and organising.

Fayol, whom we met in Chapter 3, listed control among the main elements of management in 1916. For him, control meant seeing that everything happens in agreement with the plan and the orders given. Later writers made control one of four functions, with planning, organising and leading. In this book we use the four functions of Chapter 2, so control is the fourth. It is the function that closes the circle, because it sends information back to planning.

### Why Managers Control

> Control has five purposes, each protecting a different part of the plan.

Managers control for five reasons. The first is to keep the plan on course, so that small differences do not grow into large ones. The second is to find mistakes early, when they cost little to correct. The third is to protect resources from waste, whether the resource is money, material, time or effort. The fourth is to give information to the next plan, since good planning needs facts about what happened before. The fifth is to show who is accountable, because a result can only be questioned when someone can be named as answerable for it.

### The Nature of Control

> Control has six features that distinguish it from a single act of checking.

Control has six features. First, it is a function of every manager, and not only of senior managers. A first-line manager controls a shift, and a general manager controls a whole unit. Second, it is continuous. It is a cycle that repeats, and not an event that happens once at the end. Third, it looks forward and backward. It uses past results to correct present work, and it also tries to prevent future error. Fourth, it depends on planning, because a result can only be judged against something planned. Fifth, it depends on information, since a manager cannot correct what the manager cannot see. Sixth, it involves people, because people do the work, and people accept or reject the checking.

> The sixth feature, people, decides whether control is accepted or resisted.

The sixth feature deserves special care. A thermostat in a room has no feelings about the temperature that it reads. A worker does. If a worker believes that control exists only to find fault, the worker may hide errors or change the numbers. If the worker believes that control helps the work, the worker will often report problems early. Later in this chapter we shall see that many failures of control are failures of trust, and not failures of arithmetic.

> Control is needed in a small firm as much as in a large one.

Control is needed in a small firm as much as in a large one. The owner of a one-shop bakery may check everything personally, and may keep no written plans or reports. Even so, the owner compares results with a plan, in the head if not on paper. The difference between a small and a large firm is not whether control exists, but how it is organised. In a small firm one person does all four steps. In a large firm the steps are shared among many people, and so the system must be written down.

### The Control System and the Controller

> A control system is the whole set of parts that keep a plan on course.

@ Control system | The standards, measures, reports and people that keep a plan on course.

A single check is not enough for a large organisation. A bakery with one shop can rely on the owner's eyes. A business with many shops needs a **control system**. This is the whole set of standards, measuring methods, reports and responsible people that together keep the plans on course. The system should work whether or not any one manager is present.

> The controller runs the system; every manager still controls his or her own area.

@ Controller | The person who runs the control system.

Someone must design the system and keep it running. We call this person the **controller**. In a small firm, the owner is the controller. In a large firm, the controller is often a specialist who collects the figures, prepares the reports and tells managers where results differ from plans. The controller does not replace the other managers. Each manager still controls his or her own area, in the way that the head baker controls the baking. The controller supplies the system and the facts.

> In the bakery the part-time bookkeeper acts as a controller of records.

:case Case file, illustrative. In Month 10 the owner of Morning Loaf Bakery engaged a part-time bookkeeper at Tk 8,000 a month. The bookkeeper may require receipts from all staff. This is a form of functional authority, which we studied in Chapter 10. It is the right to give instructions about one process, even to people outside one's own line. In this way the bookkeeper helps to run the control of money records, while the owner remains answerable for the shop as a whole.

> The controller should be independent of the work that is checked.

An important rule follows from the meaning of the word. A person who checks should not be the same person who did the work. If a baker counts his own waste, he may be honest, but the owner cannot be sure. When the checker is independent, the owner can trust the report more. We shall see in the history section of this chapter what happens when this rule is broken in a large organisation.

### Principles of Control

> Six principles guide the design of any control, large or small.

Principles are general rules that hold in many cases. Six principles guide the design of control. They do not give exact figures. They tell a manager what to keep in mind when building a way of checking.

> First principle: control follows the plan.

The first principle is that control follows the plan. A manager cannot check what was never planned. If the plan says 300 loaves a day, control can compare the daily count with 300. If the plan says only "make more bread", control has nothing to measure. A clearer plan makes a clearer control. For this reason Chapters 6 and 7 come before this chapter.

> Second principle: attention goes to the critical points and to large differences.

@ Critical point | A place in the work where a failure would harm the goal most.

The second principle is that attention goes to the critical points. A **critical point** is a place in the work where a failure would harm the goal most. A manager who watches every detail will be tired and will miss the important facts. In a bakery the critical points include the quality of flour at delivery, the heat of the oven and the count of loaves packed. A manager should also give most attention to large differences from the plan, and less to small ones.

> Third principle: a check must lead to action.

The third principle is that a check must lead to action. A report that nobody reads, or a difference that nobody corrects, wastes the effort of the people who prepared it. Before adding a new check, a manager should ask: who will receive this information, and what will they do if the result is poor?

> Fourth principle: prevent where possible.

The fourth principle is that control should prevent error where possible. It is better to stop poor flour entering the bakery than to throw away poor bread afterward. Control that looks forward saves more than control that only looks backward. In the third section of this chapter we shall see how managers do this.

> Fifth principle: every difference must be traceable to a person who can act.

The fifth principle is that every difference from the plan should be traceable to a person who can act. If a result is poor and nobody is responsible, nobody will correct it. This links control to the authority and delegation of Chapter 10. A manager who is made answerable for a result must also have the authority to change the work that produces it.

> Sixth principle: the checker is independent of the work checked.

The sixth principle is the one that we met above: the checker should be independent of the work that is checked. Where this is not possible, as in a very small firm, the manager should at least make the records open to another person.

### Control and Monitoring

> Monitoring watches; control watches and then acts.

@ Monitoring | Watching results without yet acting on them.

Two words are often mixed. **Monitoring** means watching results without yet acting on them. A baker who reads the oven thermometer is monitoring. Control includes monitoring, and it adds two further steps: comparing the reading with a standard (the planned level, defined in the next section), and correcting if the reading is wrong. Monitoring is therefore a part of control, and not a rival to it. A report that is only read, and never compared or acted upon, stays at the level of monitoring.

> The table sets the two side by side on five points.

The next table compares the two ideas on five points. Read each row from left to right.

:table Control and monitoring compared
| Point | Monitoring | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main question | What is happening? | What is happening, and what must change? |
| Action | Watches and records | Compares with a standard and corrects |
| Standard needed | Not always | Always |
| Result | A report | A report and a change in the work |
| Bakery example | The baker reads the oven thermometer | The baker reads it, finds it above the standard, and lowers the heat |

> Monitoring becomes control only when comparison and correction are added.

:box IN SHORT
Control is the work of checking results against plans and correcting any gap. It has four parts: a plan, a measured result, a comparison, and an action. Monitoring is the watching part only.

## The Control Process

### The Four Steps

> The control process has four steps, and the fourth sends the manager back to the first.

Every act of control, in a bakery or a bank, follows the same four steps. The manager sets a standard. The manager measures the actual result. The manager compares the result with the standard. The manager then acts: this means correcting the work, or accepting the result, or changing the standard. After the action the cycle starts again. The next figure shows these steps as a circle.

:fig The control cycle: four steps, each leading to the next | ch13_fig1_control_cycle.svg

> Some books name three steps; this book keeps four so that each task has its own name.

Some books name only three steps and join measuring and comparing into one. We keep four, for a practical reason. Measuring and comparing are different tasks, often done by different people, and they fail in different ways. A shop can measure well and compare badly, or the reverse. When each task has its own name, a manager can find the weak point more easily.

### Step One: Set Standards

> A standard is the exact level of result that is to be reached.

@! Word in use. Standard. In daily life, how good a thing is, as in a high standard of work. In management, the exact level of result to be reached.
@ Standard | The level of result that is to be reached.

The first step is to decide what result is wanted. The **standard** is the level of result that is to be reached. In daily English, people say a school has a high standard, which means good quality in a general way. In control, the word is exact. A standard says what level, of what thing, by what time. "Good service" is a hope. "Ninety-five orders in every hundred delivered on time" is a standard.

> Standards come from the objectives and plans that the manager has already made.

Standards do not appear from nowhere. They come from the objectives of Chapter 7 and the plans of Chapter 6. When an owner decides that waste must be at most 5% by Month 12, the objective becomes the standard. This is why a manager who has written clear objectives has already done much of the first step. It also explains why unclear objectives lead to weak control.

> Standards are of five kinds, which answer five different questions.

Standards are of five kinds. Quantity standards say how much. Quality standards say how good. Time standards say how fast or by when. Cost standards say how much money may be used. Behaviour standards say how people must act. The next table gives an example of each from Morning Loaf Bakery.

:table Five kinds of standard, with examples from the bakery
| Kind | It asks | Bakery example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantity | How much? | 300 loaves a day |
| Quality | How good? | Waste of 5% or less |
| Time | How fast? | 95% of orders or more on time |
| Cost | How costly? | Ingredients of about Tk 24 a loaf |
| Behaviour | How must people act? | No claim on a label that is not checked |

> Standards are found from past results, from other firms, from careful study, or from judgment.

@ Benchmark | A result reached by a good firm, used as a guide by others.

Managers find standards in four ways. The first is past results: what the shop achieved last year. The second is a **benchmark**, which is a result reached by a good firm and used as a guide by others. The third is careful study of the work, such as the time study of Taylor that we met in Chapter 3. The fourth is the judgment of experienced managers, which is useful when no other source exists. Each source has a weakness. Past results may repeat past errors. A benchmark may come from a firm unlike our own. Study takes time and money. Judgment may be wrong. A manager should use at least two sources when it is possible.

> A good standard is exact, measurable, reachable, accepted and tied to a time.

A good standard has five qualities. It is exact, so that two people read it in the same way. It is measurable, which means it can be counted or checked, as Chapter 7 explained. It is reachable, because a standard that nobody can meet teaches people to ignore standards. It is accepted by the people who must meet it, and this is easier when they helped to set it. And it is tied to a time, so that the manager knows when to check.

> A tolerance allows a small difference without action.

@ Tolerance | The range around a standard inside which a result is accepted.

No result matches a standard exactly. A baker cannot make exactly 300 loaves every day, and the oven cannot hold exactly the same heat all hours. For this reason managers often set a **tolerance**. A tolerance is the range around a standard inside which a result is accepted.^2 If the standard for output is 300 loaves and the tolerance is 5%, then any day from 285 to 315 loaves needs no action. A tolerance saves a manager from reacting to small and natural changes. But it must be chosen with thought. If it is too wide, real problems pass unseen. If it is too narrow, the manager is busy with differences that mean nothing.

^2: In this chapter, percentages are rounded to one decimal place.

### Step Two: Measure Actual Results

> To measure is to find the actual result, using a method the manager can trust.

@ Measure | To find the actual result.

The second step is to **measure**, which means to find the actual result. The word actual is important. A standard states what should happen, and the measure states what did happen. A measure may be a count, such as loaves baked. It may be a ratio, such as loaves per working hour, which we met in Chapter 2. It may be a rate, such as complaints per week, or a time, such as minutes of waiting.

> Managers measure through observation, reports, records and samples.

Managers have four common ways to find actual results. They may observe the work themselves, as an owner does when walking through the kitchen. They may ask for oral or written reports from the people who do the work. They may read records, such as daily count sheets and sales slips. Or they may take a sample, which means checking a small part to judge the whole. A sample of ten trays from one hundred is quicker and cheaper than counting every loaf. But a sample must be chosen fairly, or it will mislead.

> The measure must be accurate, timely and cheap enough to repeat.

A measure should meet three tests. It must be accurate, since a wrong number leads to a wrong action. It must be timely, since a number that arrives after the problem has grown is of little use. And it must be cheap enough to repeat, because control is a cycle and not a single act. A measure that costs more than the problem it detects is not worth keeping.

> Measure what matters, not what is easy to count.

The most serious mistake in measuring is to count what is easy and ignore what matters. A clinic finds it easy to count the patients seen in an hour. It finds it harder to count how many patients were helped. A manager who measures only the easy figure may push the staff in the wrong direction. We return to this danger in the section on barriers. For now, we keep one rule: ask first what the plan is trying to achieve, and then find a measure that truly reflects it.

### Step Three: Compare Results with Standards

> Comparing finds the deviation, which is the gap between actual result and standard.

@ Deviation | The gap between the actual result and the standard.

The third step is to compare. The manager finds the **deviation**, which is the gap between the actual result and the standard. We write it in words and then in symbols:

> The deviation is found by subtracting the standard from the actual result.

Deviation equals actual result minus standard. In symbols, D = A − S. If the result is above the standard, D is positive. If the result is below, D is negative. A positive deviation is not always good. For output, a positive deviation is good. For waste, it is bad. The sign shows direction only. The manager must always ask whether the direction helps or harms the plan.

> Percentage deviation shows the size of the gap relative to the standard.

The size of a deviation is easier to judge when it is shown against the standard. For this we use the percentage deviation. In words, it is the deviation divided by the standard, multiplied by 100. In symbols, percentage deviation = D ÷ S × 100. A deviation of 20 loaves means more when the standard is 100 than when it is 1,000.

> A percentage point is a step on a percentage scale, which must not be confused with a percentage of a percentage.

@ Percentage point | One unit on a percentage scale, such as the step from 5% to 6%.

@! Go slowly here.

One more idea needs care. Many standards are themselves percentages, such as waste of 5%. If the actual waste is 8%, the gap is not 3%. It is 3 **percentage points**. A percentage point is one unit on a percentage scale, so the step from 5% to 8% is three of them. But the gap is also 60% of the standard, because 3 is 60% of 5. Both statements are true, and they say different things. To avoid confusion, a manager should state which one is meant.

> The manager then asks whether the deviation lies inside or outside the tolerance.

Next the manager asks whether the deviation is inside or outside the tolerance. A deviation inside the tolerance usually needs no action. A deviation outside it needs attention. A deviation at the edge needs a closer watch, because the next result may fall outside. The next figure shows weekly results against a standard and a tolerance. It is a simple form of a control chart, which we describe in the next section.

:fig Weekly results against a standard, with a tolerance band (illustrative figures) | ch13_fig2_tolerance_band.svg

> A deviation is a symptom; the cause must be found before acting.

A deviation tells the manager that something is wrong. It does not say what. A doctor treats a fever by finding its cause, and a manager must do the same. Five kinds of cause appear again and again. The work may have been done badly. The standard may have been set too high or too low. The plan may have rested on a wrong premise, such as a price that later changed. An outside force of the kind we studied in Chapter 4 may have changed the situation. Or the measure itself may be wrong. If the manager does not find which cause is at work, the action may fix the wrong thing.

### Step Four: Take Corrective Action

> Corrective action is the step that closes the gap, or closes the cycle if no gap exists.

@ Corrective action | A step taken to close the gap between result and standard.

The fourth step is **corrective action**, which means a step taken to close the gap between result and standard. It is the step that makes control different from mere watching. A manager has three choices. The first is to accept the result, when the deviation is inside the tolerance. The second is to correct the performance, by changing the way the work is done, the people who do it, or the resources they use. The third is to change the standard or the plan, when the cause is a standard that was never reachable or a world that has changed.

> A good corrective action treats the cause, not the symptom, and is checked afterward.

Two rules improve corrective action. The first is to act on the cause and not on the symptom. If waste is high because trays wait too long, a warning to the bakers does not help; a change in the loading of the ovens might. The second is to check afterward. The manager returns to step two and measures again. If the gap has closed, the action worked. If not, the cause was misjudged, and the cycle continues. In this way control is never finished. It is a loop.

### Two Worked Examples

> The first example uses clean numbers so that the method is the only thing to learn.

The first example is not from the bakery but from a small snack stall, so that the numbers stay clean. *Given.* The stall prepares 100 portions a day. The standard for waste is 5% of the portions. The actual waste in one week is 8%. *Find.* The deviation in percentage points, in portions, and as a percentage of the standard. *Formula.* Deviation equals actual minus standard; percentage deviation equals deviation divided by standard, multiplied by 100.

> The steps show each line of arithmetic with its unit.

*Steps.* In percentage points: 8% − 5% = +3 percentage points. In portions: 8 − 5 = +3 portions a day. As a percentage of the standard: 3 ÷ 5 × 100 = +60%. *Answer.* Waste is 3 percentage points above the standard. That equals 3 more portions wasted each day, or 60% more waste than the standard allows. *Check.* The standard plus the deviation must give the actual result: 5 + 3 = 8. And 60% of 5 is 3, which agrees with the gap.

> The second example is messier: three standards, each with its own tolerance.

The second example is messier, as real work often is. A school canteen has three standards, and each has its own unit and its own tolerance. The manager must decide which deviations need action. The next table gives the data. Read it first, and then follow the steps below it.

:table Canteen standards, results and tolerances (illustrative figures)
| Measure | Standard | Actual | Tolerance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Meals served a day | 400 | 372 | Within 5% of standard |
| Cost of one meal | Tk 38 | Tk 41 | Within 10% of standard |
| Complaints a week | 5 or fewer | 9 | Up to 6 accepted |

> Each measure gets its own deviation and its own test against tolerance.

*Steps.* Meals: 372 − 400 = −28, and −28 ÷ 400 × 100 = −7.0%. This lies outside the tolerance of 5%. Cost: 41 − 38 = +Tk 3, and 3 ÷ 38 × 100 = +7.9%. This lies inside the tolerance of 10%. Complaints: 9 − 5 = +4, and 4 ÷ 5 × 100 = +80%. Nine complaints are above the accepted level of six, so this lies outside the tolerance.

> The answer names the two measures that need action, and the link between them.

*Answer.* Two deviations need action: meals served and complaints. The cost deviation is inside its tolerance, but it is close to the edge. The three may be connected. When fewer meals are served, the fixed costs of the kitchen are shared among fewer meals, so the cost of one meal rises. A manager who finds the cause of the low meal count may therefore improve two measures with one action. *Check.* 400 − 28 = 372, 38 + 3 = 41, and 5 + 4 = 9, so each actual result equals standard plus deviation. Each percentage was divided by its own standard, and so the units agree.

> A clinic shows the same cycle in a new setting, with no baking at all.

The same cycle works in any organisation. Consider a small clinic. Its plan says that 90% of patients should be seen within 30 minutes of their appointment time. This is the standard. The manager measures it from the appointment book, which records both times. In one month, 78% of patients were seen on time. The deviation is 78 − 90 = −12 percentage points, and −12 ÷ 90 × 100 = −13.3% of the standard. The tolerance is 5 percentage points, so the result is outside it.

> The manager tests the five causes in turn and finds that the cause lies in the work.

The manager then tests the five kinds of cause. The standard seems fair, because other clinics of the same size reach it. The measure is sound, since the front desk fills the book at the time of each visit. No outside force has changed, and the plan assumed the same number of patients as before. The remaining cause is the work. The records show that the delays occur on mornings when only one nurse is present. The corrective action is to arrange the nurses' leave so that two nurses work on the busiest mornings. The manager will measure again in the next month. *Check.* Standard plus deviation gives the actual result: 90 − 12 = 78.

> The common slips in these calculations are few and easy to avoid.

Three slips are common. The first is a wrong sign: subtract the standard from the actual result, not the reverse. The second is dividing by the actual result instead of the standard. The third is mixing percentage points with percentages of the standard, as we saw above. If the manager checks that standard plus deviation equals actual, most slips appear at once.

## Types and Tools of Control

### Three Moments for Control

> Work has a start, a middle and an end, and control can come at each moment.

Every piece of work has three parts. Inputs enter, such as flour, workers and money. A process changes the inputs, such as mixing and baking. Outputs leave, such as loaves for sale. Control can be applied at any of these moments. The moment gives its name to the type of control, and each type has its own strengths and weaknesses. The next figure shows the three moments on one line.

:fig Three moments for control, drawn along the path of the work | ch13_fig3_three_moments.svg

> Feedforward control acts before the work begins, to prevent error.

@ Feedforward control | Control that acts before the work starts.

The first type acts before the work begins. We call it **feedforward control**, which means control that acts before the work starts. Its aim is to prevent error, by making sure that the inputs and the plans are right before the process begins. A bakery that checks the flour at delivery uses feedforward control. A hospital that tests the sterility of instruments before an operation does the same. So does a firm that tests new staff before hiring them. Its great strength is that it stops a problem at the cheapest point. Its weakness is that it needs good knowledge of what could go wrong, and it cannot catch errors that start during the work.

> Concurrent control acts during the work, so that correction is immediate.

@ Concurrent control | Control that acts while the work is being done.

The second type acts during the work. **Concurrent control** means control that acts while the work is being done. A baker who watches the oven thermometer and lowers the heat at once uses concurrent control. So does a supervisor who stands at a packing bench and corrects a worker on the spot. Its strength is speed: the error is corrected before it grows. Its weaknesses are cost and pressure. It needs a person or an instrument at the place of work, and workers who are watched closely may feel distrusted.

> Feedback control acts after the work is done and teaches the next plan.

@ Feedback control | Control that checks results after the work is done.

The third type acts after the work. **Feedback control** means control that checks results after the work is done. A monthly profit report is feedback control, as is a customer survey, or a test of finished loaves. Its strength is that it gives the full picture of the result and shows what to change in the next plan. Its weakness is delay. By the time the report arrives, the loaves are sold and the money is spent, and the error cannot be undone.^3

^3: In daily English, feedback often means a person's comment on someone's work. In this chapter it has the narrower meaning of control that acts after the work is done.

> The table compares the three types on timing, strength, weakness and example.

The next table puts the three types side by side.

:table Three types of control compared
| Point | Feedforward | Concurrent | Feedback |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Moment | Before the work | During the work | After the work |
| Aim | Prevent error | Correct at once | Learn and improve |
| Strength | Cheapest point to act | Fast correction | Full picture of results |
| Weakness | Needs good forecasts | Needs presence; may feel like pressure | Late; cannot undo |
| Bakery example | Flour checked at delivery | Oven temperature watched | Monthly profit report |

> A sound system uses all three types, in balance.

No type is best. A sound control system uses all three, in a balance that suits the work. The more costly an error is, the earlier the control should come. A mistake in a medicine is so serious that feedback alone would not be enough, and checks must come before and during the work. A mistake in the colour of a poster is less serious, and a check at the end may be enough. The balance also depends on cost, since feedforward and concurrent control often need more people or instruments.

> In Month 12 the bakery already used all three types, but its feedback was slow.

:case Case file, illustrative. At Month 12 Morning Loaf Bakery has three controls. The ingredient check at delivery is feedforward control. The oven temperature check is concurrent control. The monthly profit report is feedback control. The first two work every day. The third arrives once a month. This difference in timing will matter when we return to the bakery's problem later in this chapter.

### Common Tools of Control

> Tools are the instruments of control; none of them is control itself.

Managers use many tools to carry out the steps of control. A tool collects facts or makes them easy to read. It does not decide what to do. That decision belongs to the manager. We describe seven common tools. The next table lists them with what each checks and a bakery use.

:table Seven common tools of control
| Tool | What it checks | Bakery use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Budget | Money used against money planned | Monthly flour cost against the budget |
| Records and reports | Daily and monthly facts | Count sheet of loaves baked |
| Personal observation | Work as it happens | Owner walks through the kitchen |
| Ratio | One figure divided by another | Waste divided by loaves baked |
| Audit | Honesty and accuracy of records | Check of the sales book by an outsider |
| Control chart | Results over time against limits | Weekly waste plotted against 5% |
| Schedule chart | Tasks against planned dates | Festival-week order timetable |

> A budget is a plan in numbers, so it becomes a standard for money.

The first tool is the budget. In Chapter 6 we defined a budget as a plan in numbers. When the period ends, or during it, the manager compares actual money used with the budget. This is called budgetary control. It works well because money is a common unit: flour, wages and rent can all be compared in taka.

> Records and reports carry the facts from the work to the manager.

Records and reports carry the facts from the place of work to the manager. A good report is short, it arrives in time, and it shows the difference from the standard. A report that is long and late is rarely read. Management should ask of every report: who needs it, and what will they do with it?

> Personal observation gives the manager facts that numbers do not show.

Personal observation means the manager looks at the work. It gives facts that numbers cannot show, such as the mood of the workers or the state of the machines. It also costs the manager's time, and workers may see it as pressure. Many managers combine it with reports: the report points to the problem, and the visit explains it.

> A ratio compares two numbers by division and so allows fair comparison.

A ratio compares one number with another by division. Productivity, which we studied in Chapter 2, is a ratio of output to input. Waste divided by loaves baked is also a ratio. Ratios are useful because they allow fair comparison between weeks, between shops, or between firms of different size.

> An audit is an independent check of records, by a person outside the work.

@ Audit | An independent check of records and accounts.

An **audit** is an independent check of records and accounts. An internal audit is done by staff of the organisation who are not part of the work checked. An external audit is done by outsiders. Audit supports the sixth principle: the checker should be independent of the work. It checks that the records are honest and accurate before managers rely on them.

> A control chart shows results over time with limits drawn on the graph.

@ Control chart | A graph of results over time, with the standard and limits drawn on it.

A **control chart** is a graph of results over time, with the standard and its limits drawn on it. The weekly chart shown earlier in this chapter is a simple example. A point outside the limits calls for action. A run of several points on one side of the standard also calls for action, even if each point is inside the limits, because it suggests a steady drift.^4

^4: Control charts were developed for quality control in factories in the 1920s by Walter Shewhart, an engineer in the United States.

> A schedule chart compares planned and actual dates for the tasks of a project.

The last tool is the schedule chart, which lays out the tasks of a project against time. The planned dates are drawn first, and the actual dates are added as the work proceeds. Where the actual line falls behind the plan, the manager sees at once which task is late. Henry Gantt, a colleague of Taylor, made such charts popular in the early twentieth century. They suit the single-use plans of Chapter 6, such as a festival-week order schedule.

## Barriers and Requirements

### Barriers to Control

> A barrier to control is anything that stops control from working as intended.

@ Barrier to control | Anything that stops control from working as intended.

Control often fails in practice, even when the four steps are known. A **barrier to control** is anything that stops control from working as intended. Some barriers are in the system, some are in the people, and some are in the world outside. The next table lists six common barriers, how each shows itself, and what a manager may do about it. Six short paragraphs then add detail.

:table Six barriers to control and remedies
| Barrier | How it shows | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resistance by people | Hidden errors; changed numbers | Involve staff; explain the purpose |
| Too much control | Many reports; little time to work | Keep checks at critical points |
| Too little control | Problems found late or never | Add checks; shorten reports |
| Wrong measure | Numbers improve; goal does not | Test the measure against the goal |
| Poor information | Action on old or wrong figures | Check the source; speed the report |
| No authority to correct | Problem seen but not solved | Give authority with duty |

> People resist control when they fear blame or loss of freedom.

The first barrier is resistance by people. Workers may fear that control will be used to blame them, or that it will take away their freedom to decide. They may react in several ways. They may hide errors. They may do only what is measured. They may change the figures. A manager reduces resistance by involving staff in setting standards, by explaining why a check exists, and by using results to help the worker and not only to judge. The self-control that Theory Y assumes, which we studied in Chapter 11, is the best remedy of all.

> Too much control wastes effort, and too little lets problems grow.

The second and third barriers are two sides of one problem. Too much control means so many checks that the workers spend more time reporting than working, and the manager cannot see what matters. Too little control means problems grow unseen. The remedy for both is the second principle: place the checks at the critical points, and do not place them everywhere.

> A wrong measure sends people in the wrong direction.

The fourth barrier is the wrong measure, which we mentioned earlier. People tend to do what is measured. If a clinic rewards the number of patients seen, the doctors may hurry. If a shop rewards the number of calls made, the staff may make short and useless calls. The measure then improves while the goal suffers. A manager should ask often: if people do exactly what is measured, will the goal be reached?

> Poor information, whether late or wrong, damages every later step.

The fifth barrier is poor information. If a report arrives late, the problem may have grown. If a report is wrong, the action will be wrong. Managers should know where each figure comes from, who prepared it, and how it was checked. They should also be careful with reports that are always good, since a report that never shows a problem may be hiding one.

> A person who sees the deviation but cannot act has no real control.

The sixth barrier is the lack of authority to correct. A head baker may see that waste is high, but if the head baker cannot change the oven schedule, nothing will improve. This links back to the principles: those who are answerable for a result need authority over the work that produces it, as Chapter 10 explained.

> Some work cannot be measured easily, and standards can become outdated.

Two further barriers are worth noting. Some work is hard to measure, such as creative work or the quality of care. For such work, managers may use indirect measures and judgment, and must accept that control will be less exact. Second, standards may become outdated when the world changes. If the price of flour rises sharply, a cost standard set last year no longer fits. Control must therefore include an occasional review of the standards themselves.

### Requirements of Effective Control

> Effective control meets requirements in three groups: information, design and people.

The barriers suggest what an effective control system must be. We group its requirements in three: the information it uses, the way it is designed, and the people it serves. If a system meets all three, the manager can have confidence in it.

> The information must be timely, accurate and easy to understand.

The first group concerns information. The information must be timely, reaching the manager while action can still help. It must be accurate, because a wrong figure leads to wrong action. And it must be understandable, which means the reader sees quickly what the figure says and what it requires.

> The design must focus on critical points, cost little and bend when needed.

The second group concerns design. The system must focus on critical points and on large deviations, as the second principle said. It must be economical: the cost of the control must be less than the loss that it prevents. And it must be flexible, so that when the plan or the world changes, the standards and the checks can change with them.

> The people must accept the control, find it fair and know who is responsible.

The third group concerns people. The staff must accept the control, which is easier when they helped to design it. They must find it fair, which means the same rules apply to all, from the owner to the newest helper. And they must know who is responsible for each result. Finally, a system should balance numbers with judgment. Figures show what happened. Only a manager who talks with the people can learn why.

## Control in the Whole System

### Control and Planning

> Planning and control form a loop: plans give standards, and results improve plans.

Control does not stand alone. It is joined to planning in a loop. Plans give the standards that control uses. Control gives the facts that improve the next plan. The next figure shows the loop. Plans are made, work is done, results are measured and compared, corrective action is taken, and the lessons return to the next plan.

:fig The planning and control loop | ch13_fig4_planning_control_loop.svg

> Control also tests the premises on which a plan rests.

In Chapter 6 we learned that every plan rests on premises, which are assumptions about the future. Control tests these premises. If a deviation arises because a premise was wrong, then correcting the work will not help. The manager should change the plan. A bakery that planned for flour prices to rise by 5%, and then saw a rise of 15%, would meet deviations in cost that no baker could remove. The right action is a new plan with a new premise.

### Control, Efficiency and Effectiveness

> Control checks efficiency through waste and effectiveness through goals.

In Chapter 2 we kept three ideas apart: productivity, efficiency and effectiveness. Control uses all three. Waste measures show efficiency, which is doing a task with little waste. Output and quality measures against the objective show effectiveness, which is reaching the goal set. A productivity figure may serve as a standard in its own right, as when a shop sets a standard of loaves per working hour.

> Good control cannot make a wrong goal right.

There is a limit. Control checks whether the plan is carried out, and it cannot decide whether the plan is wise. A firm may have excellent control and still be ineffective, if its standards point at the wrong goal. A firm that cuts waste to very low levels while its customers leave for other shops is efficient and ineffective. For this reason managers should review the goals as well as the results.

### Control and Power

> Control needs authority, and control is itself a use of power.

In Chapter 12 we studied power and its bases. Control touches power in three ways. First, the controller needs legitimate power, the right that comes from the position, in order to ask for facts and to require action. Second, results often lead to reward or to punishment, which are reward power and coercive power. If these are used unfairly, people will resist the control. Third, control itself is a use of power over the work of others. It must therefore be exercised with fairness and with open rules.

> The leader's style shapes how control is felt.

Leadership style, also studied in Chapter 12, shapes how control is felt. A leader who uses control only to punish makes people hide facts. A leader who uses it to coach, by discussing deviations and agreeing the next step, makes people report early. In this way control and leadership support one another.

## The Worked Case: Morning Loaf at Month 13

> The case runs the whole cycle on the bakery's figures from earlier chapters.

The worked case follows the layout used for descriptive courses: situation, question, reasoning, conclusion and check. It runs the whole cycle on the figures of the bakery, which were built up over twelve chapters.

> The situation states the standards, the results and the facts so far.

:case Case file, illustrative. *Situation.* In Month 7, the owner agreed three objectives with the staff: 300 loaves a day by Month 12, waste of 5% or less, and 95% of orders or more on time. At Month 12 the results are 280 loaves a day, waste of 8%, and 93% of orders on time. The shop works 26 trading days in a month. Ingredients cost about Tk 24 a loaf. *Question.* How large are the deviations, what is their cost and cause, and what should the owner do?

> The table sets out standards, results and deviations for the three measures.

The next table gives the standards, the results and the deviations.

:table Morning Loaf Bakery at Month 12: standards, results and deviations
| Measure | Standard | Actual | Deviation | Percentage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Output a day | 300 loaves | 280 loaves | −20 loaves | −6.7% |
| Waste | 5% or less | 8% | +3 percentage points | +60.0% |
| Orders on time | 95% or more | 93% | −2 percentage points | −2.1% |

> The calculations show the three deviations and the money cost of the waste.

*Reasoning, part one: the figures.* Output: 280 − 300 = −20 loaves, and −20 ÷ 300 × 100 = −6.7%. Waste: 8 − 5 = +3 percentage points, and 3 ÷ 5 × 100 = +60.0%. Orders on time: 93 − 95 = −2 percentage points, and −2 ÷ 95 × 100 = −2.1%. The waste has a money cost. Three percentage points of 280 loaves is 0.03 × 280 = 8.4 loaves a day. At Tk 24 of ingredients a loaf this is 8.4 × 24 = Tk 201.60 a day. Over 26 trading days it is 201.60 × 26 = Tk 5,241.60 a month.

> The owner checks the five kinds of cause one by one and finds the cause in the work.

*Reasoning, part two: the cause.* The owner tests the five kinds of cause in turn. The standards were agreed with the staff in Month 7, so they are not the problem. The day sheets agree with the sales records, so the measure is sound. No new outside force appears in the records. The plan's premises have not changed in a way that explains the gap. The cause lies in the work. The two ovens are loaded at the same hour, so some trays wait and are baked too long, and the second batch starts late. This one cause explains the low output, the high waste, and part of the late orders.

> The corrective action treats the cause and adds a faster check.

*Reasoning, part three: the action.* The owner changes the oven-load schedule so that the two ovens are loaded at different hours. The owner also asks the head baker to count waste every week. The existing controls remain: the delivery check is feedforward, the oven temperature check is concurrent, and the monthly profit report is feedback. The weekly count shortens the delay of the feedback from one month to one week. The owner and the head baker discuss the weekly figures at their regular coaching meeting.

> The conclusion puts waste first because it is largest in size and has a money cost.

*Conclusion.* Waste is the deviation with the largest relative size and a direct money cost, so it comes first. The oven schedule treats a single cause behind three deviations, and output and punctuality are expected to improve with it. Orders on time are only two points under standard, so they are watched and not yet treated separately.

> The check tests the arithmetic and the logic, and sets the next cycle.

*Check.* Standard plus deviation equals actual in each row: 300 − 20 = 280, 5 + 3 = 8, and 95 − 2 = 93. If waste returns to 5%, it will be 0.05 × 280 = 14 loaves a day, against 22.4 now, and the saving of 8.4 loaves agrees with the cost found above. The next cycle begins at once: the weekly count measures waste again, and after four weeks the owner compares the result with the standard.

> The case uses ideas from every earlier part of the book.

Notice how many earlier chapters stand behind this one case. The standards came from the objectives of Chapter 7. The plan came from Chapter 6. The two ovens were the result of the decision of Chapter 8. The head baker's duty to count waste rests on the authority and delegation of Chapter 10. The monthly team bonus for waste of 5% or less, which came from Chapter 11, gives staff a reason to care about the weekly count. And the coaching meeting comes from Chapter 12. Control does not replace these ideas. It keeps them working together.

## Summary

> Control checks results against plans and corrects any gap, in a repeating cycle of four steps.

Control is the work of checking results against plans and correcting any gap. It serves five purposes: to keep plans on course, to find mistakes early, to protect resources, to inform the next plan, and to show who is accountable. The process has four steps: set a standard, measure the actual result, compare the two to find the deviation, and take corrective action. The cycle then repeats. A tolerance allows small differences, and the cause of a deviation must be found before action is taken.

> The three types, the tools and the barriers help a manager to design and judge a control system.

Control may act before the work, during it, or after it, and a sound system uses all three. Managers use tools such as budgets, reports, ratios, audits, control charts and schedule charts. Barriers such as resistance, wrong measures and poor information weaken control. Effective control needs timely and accurate information, a design that focuses on critical points, and people who accept it. Control joins planning in a loop, checks efficiency and effectiveness, and is itself a use of power.

> You should now be able to say what control is, run the cycle on new figures and name the type of control.

You should now be able to say what control is and how it differs from monitoring. You should be able to run the four steps on a new set of figures, and to name the type of control in a given case. You should also be able to list the barriers, and to explain how control joins planning.

## Think It Through

> The problem asks why a wrong measure may be worse than none.

A clinic wants its doctors to work faster. The manager sets a standard of eight patients seen in each hour, and reports each doctor's figure every week. Within a month the doctors reach nine patients an hour. But the number of patients who return for the same illness rises, and the patients say that the doctors do not listen. Was this measure better than having no measure at all? Write your answer in three or four sentences before you read the next paragraph.

> The answer: a measure steers behaviour, so the wrong one steers people the wrong way.

A measure steers behaviour. When people know what is counted, they give it their effort. Here the count was patients seen, but the plan wanted patients helped. With no measure, the doctors would have used their own judgment about how long each patient needs, and the clinic would at least have been steering by a purpose, if not by a number. With the wrong measure, the clinic steered the doctors away from its purpose, and it also gave the manager false comfort, since the figures looked good. The lesson is to ask first what the plan is trying to achieve, and then to find a measure that reflects it. A better measure here might combine patients seen with the share who return for the same problem.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: control means close supervision of people.

The first mistake is to think that control means close supervision of people. Control checks results against plans, and supervision is only one way of doing it. Many firms use control without watching anyone, as when they read a weekly report. The best control is often self-control, where the worker knows the standard and checks the work personally.

> Mistake two: control happens only after the work is finished.

The second mistake is to think that control happens only after the work is finished. That is feedback control, and it is one of three. A firm that waits for the end of the month to learn that its flour was poor has used the least helpful moment. Feedforward and concurrent control often act at lower cost.

> Mistake three: every deviation means that somebody failed.

The third mistake is to think that every deviation means that somebody failed. A deviation may come from a standard that was too high, from a plan that rested on a wrong premise, or from a change in the outside world. A manager who blames first and asks later will find that people hide the facts. The question is why the gap arose, and not who is at fault.

> Mistake four: more control is always better.

The fourth mistake is to think that more control is always better. Each check costs time and money, and each can make workers feel distrusted. Beyond a certain point, an added check costs more than the loss it prevents. Good control is selective: it watches the critical points.

> Mistake five: a gap of three percentage points is the same as a gap of three percent.

The fifth mistake is a mistake of arithmetic. A change from 5% to 8% is three percentage points, and it is also sixty percent of the standard. These two statements measure different things. A manager who writes "waste rose by 3%" may be understood to mean either. It is safer to say which is meant.

> Mistake six: monitoring and control are the same thing.

The sixth mistake is to treat monitoring and control as one thing. Monitoring watches. Control watches, compares and acts. A firm may have excellent reports and poor control, if the reports are read and nothing is changed.

## In History

> Barings Bank failed in 1995 because one trader both did the work and checked it.

In February 1995 Barings Bank, one of the oldest merchant banks in London, failed. The cause was the trading of one man, Nick Leeson, at the bank's office in Singapore. Leeson traded futures, which are agreements to buy or sell at a fixed price on a later date. He was also in charge of the part of the office that recorded and settled his own trades. In this way the same man did the work and checked it. The sixth principle of control, that the checker should be independent of the work checked, was broken.

> The losses were hidden in an account that London did not examine closely.

Losses on his trades were placed in a little-used account, so the reports that went to London did not show them. London saw profits, and it did not ask how a small office could earn so much. Later inquiries found that the bank's own management had not acted on warnings about this arrangement. Control by feedback was late and weak, and concurrent and feedforward control were almost absent. When the hidden losses came to light, they were larger than the bank's capital, and the bank was sold to the Dutch group ING for a token price of one pound.

> The case shows the cost of missing independence, missing questions and slow feedback.

The case teaches three lessons. A checker must be independent of the work checked. Good news should be questioned as carefully as bad news. And a system that depends on reports from the person being checked is not a control system at all. The loss of a bank that had stood for more than two centuries came from a failure in the oldest and simplest rule of control.
