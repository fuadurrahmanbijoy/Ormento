## Busy, But Going Where?

> A bakery owner works hard all day and still cannot say whether the month went well.

Return to the Morning Loaf Bakery in its seventh month. The plan of Chapter VI is made. Yet at the end of the first week the owner meets a puzzle. The staff have worked from dawn until night. The ovens have never been cold. Was it a good week?

> Effort is not the same as progress.

The owner cannot answer, because the plan names a direction but does not say how far the shop should have come by Friday. The bakers feel they worked well. The rider feels tired. The counter assistant feels that customers were rude. Each person has a different idea of success, and no one is wrong, because no one has been told what success means.

> The missing piece is a clear and shared statement of what must be reached.

The missing piece is the objective: a clear statement of the result to be reached, and the date by which it should be reached. Chapter VI gave the first step of planning as finding a need and the second step as setting the goal. This chapter studies the second step in depth, and then shows how a whole firm can set objectives together.

> You need three ideas from earlier chapters.

You will need three earlier ideas. From Chapter VI, the plan and the six steps of planning. From Chapter II, the four functions. And from Chapter VI again, the planning premise, because objectives are set with the future in mind.

## What Objectives Are

### Goal and Objective

> Everyday speech uses goal and objective as if they meant the same.

In daily speech, people use the words goal and objective for nearly the same thing. In management we keep a small but useful difference. A **goal** is a general result that someone wants to reach: to grow, to serve customers well, to be known for good bread. An objective is a more exact version of a goal. It says how much, and by when.

@ Objective | A specific result to be reached by a set time, stated so that it can be checked.

> An objective is a specific result with a deadline.

An **objective** is a specific result to be reached by a set time, stated so that it can be checked. The goal says: we want to bake more bread. The objective says: we shall bake 300 loaves a day by the end of the twelfth month. The goal gives a direction. The objective gives a place and a date.

> A school exam shows the difference between a wish and an objective.

Think of a student. A student who says, "I want to do well in the examination," has a goal. A student who says, "I shall score at least 70 per cent in the economics paper in June," has an objective. The second statement can be checked on the day, and it shapes what the student does each week.

### The Nature of Objectives

> Objectives have four main features.

Four features describe good objectives. We take them in turn.

> First, objectives are specific.

First, an objective is specific. It names exactly what will be reached. "Better service" is a goal. "Ninety-five orders in every hundred delivered on the day promised" is specific.

@ Measurable | Able to be counted or checked by a number or a plain test.

> Second, objectives are measurable.

Second, an objective is **measurable**. Measurable means that it can be counted or checked by a number or a plain test. If nobody can say how much has been reached, nobody can say whether the objective has been met.

> Third, objectives have a time limit.

Third, an objective has a time limit. "300 loaves a day" has no end point. "300 loaves a day by the end of month 12" has one. The date tells everyone when the check will be made.

> Fourth, objectives are realistic and agreed.

Fourth, an objective is realistic and agreed. An objective that nobody believes can be reached will be ignored. An objective forced on a person without discussion will be resented. The best objectives stretch people a little, and are accepted by those who must reach them.

### Why Objectives Matter

> Objectives serve four uses in a firm.

Objectives serve four uses. The first is direction: they tell each person where to go. The second is coordination: when people share objectives, their work fits together. The third is motivation: people work harder toward a result that they understand and have helped to choose. The fourth is control: objectives give the standard against which results are measured, a link that Chapter XIII follows up.

> Objectives and plans are linked, because a plan is the route to an objective.

Notice how objectives and plans fit. An objective says where to go. The plan says how to get there. A plan without an objective has no destination, and an objective without a plan has no road.

### The Hierarchy of Objectives

> Objectives are arranged in levels, from the whole firm to each person.

A large firm has many objectives. To keep them in order, managers arrange them in a **hierarchy** of objectives. A hierarchy is an arrangement in levels, each below the one above. At the top are the objectives of the whole firm. Below them are the objectives of each department. At the bottom are the objectives of each person.

> Each level serves the one above it.

Each level serves the level above. The firm's objective is divided into parts, and each department takes a part. Each department's objective is divided again, and each person takes a part. If every person reaches the personal objective, the department reaches its objective. If every department reaches its own, the firm reaches its own. The figure below shows the shape.

:fig The hierarchy of objectives, from the firm to the individual | fig_hierarchy.svg

> A hierarchy works only if each level is agreed with the level above.

The hierarchy works only when the levels fit. A department that sets its own objective without looking at the firm's may pull the firm in another direction. A good hierarchy is built both from the top down, which gives direction, and from the bottom up, which gives knowledge of what is possible.

### Where Objectives Are Needed: Key Result Areas

@ Key result area | A part of the firm's work where results matter so much that objectives must be set there.

> A firm cannot set objectives for everything, so it chooses the key areas.

A firm cannot set objectives for every action. It chooses the parts of its work where results matter most. We call these the **key result areas**. A key result area is a part of the firm's work where results matter so much to its success that objectives must be set there.

> Drucker proposed eight areas that fit most firms.

Peter Drucker, whose work we meet later in this chapter, suggested eight key areas for a business.^1 We give them below in plain words, with a bakery example for each.

Read the table down the first and third columns. A bakery would not need an objective for every row at once. A manager looks at the table, decides which areas matter most this year, and sets objectives there. A firm that neglects one area, such as staff attitude, may meet its goals for sales and then lose its best workers.

### Objectives Beyond Profit

> Many people think that a firm has only one objective, which is profit.

Many beginners think that a firm exists for one purpose only, which is to earn profit. Profit matters a great deal, because a firm that loses money cannot survive. Yet a firm that sets only a profit objective may lose its way. A bakery that cuts the size of its loaves to raise profit may find that customers go elsewhere. Profit is better seen as the reward for doing the other things well.

> A firm needs several objectives that support one another.

For this reason, a firm sets objectives in several key areas at once. Some concern customers, some concern products, some concern staff and some concern the community. These objectives support one another over time. Pleased customers return, and returning customers bring profit. Skilled and settled staff bake better bread. The eight key areas of the table above are a reminder that a sound firm looks in many directions.

### Objectives Over Different Periods

> Objectives look different over short, middle and long periods.

Objectives, like plans, cover different lengths of time. Chapter VI separated strategic, tactical and operational plans, and objectives follow the same pattern. A long-range objective states what the firm wishes to be in several years. A middle-range objective states what a department must reach in a year. A short-range objective states what a person must reach this month or this week. The long-range objective gives meaning to the short ones, and the short ones are the steps by which the long one is reached.

> A long objective should be broken into stages.

A long objective that is not broken into stages is hard to manage. If the bakery wishes to reach 300 loaves a day in six months, the owner can add a milestone at the end of each month, such as 10 more loaves a day. A milestone is a mark on the way. It shows whether the work is on schedule, and it allows a correction while there is still time.

### Who Sets the Objectives?

> Objectives may be set from the top, from the bottom, or by both.

There are three ways to set objectives. In the first, top managers set them and pass them down. This gives direction but may ignore what is possible. In the second, each unit sets its own and sends them up. This uses local knowledge but may lose sight of the whole. In the third, both happen together: the top proposes, the units respond, and the two sides agree. Management by objectives uses the third way. It takes more time than the first two, but it combines direction with knowledge, and it gives each person a part in the decision.

> You should now be able to say what an objective is.

You should now be able to say this: an objective is a specific, measurable result with a time limit, objectives are arranged in a hierarchy from the firm to each person, and a firm sets them in its key result areas.

## Setting Objectives

> Objectives must be made with care, and a few simple tests help.

Writing an objective is a skill. A careless objective is vague, and a vague objective does no work. In this section we give a short method and four tests, and then we practise on examples.

### A Method in Four Questions

> Four questions turn a wish into an objective.

A manager can turn a wish into an objective by asking four questions in order. We state them in the form of a method.

> The first question is: what exactly do we want?

The first question is: what exactly do we want? The manager names the result in plain words, as a thing that could be seen. "Happy customers" is hard to see. "Fewer complaints" is easier. "No more than two complaints in a week" can be seen exactly.

> The second question is: how will we measure it?

The second question is: how will we measure it? The manager chooses a number or a plain test. If no sensible number exists, the manager asks what would be true, and could be seen by anyone, if the result were reached.

> The third question is: by when?

The third question is: by when? The manager fixes a date, and if the objective is large, the dates of steps along the way.

> The fourth question is: is it reachable, and who will own it?

The fourth question has two parts. Is the result reachable with the people and resources we have? And who will own it? An objective owned by no one is reached by no one. Each objective should have one person who answers for it.

### Common Mistakes in Writing Objectives

> Five mistakes are common among beginners.

Beginners tend to make the same few mistakes when they write objectives. It helps to name them so that you can avoid them.

> Mistake one: describing an activity and not a result.

The first mistake is to describe an activity and not a result. "Hold weekly meetings" is an activity. "Reduce late orders to five in every hundred" is a result. An activity may be done well and still produce nothing. A result tells you whether the work succeeded.

> Mistake two: stating no time limit.

The second mistake is to leave out the date. Without a date, an objective can be delayed for ever. The date also lets people plan their effort.

> Mistake three: setting too many objectives.

The third mistake is to set too many. When a person has twenty objectives, all seem equally important, and none gets full attention. A person usually does well with a few: perhaps three to six. Fewer, better chosen objectives work better than many.

> Mistake four: setting an objective that the person cannot influence.

The fourth mistake is to ask for a result that the person cannot influence. A baker cannot be held to the price of flour. A baker can be held to the care of the dough.

> Mistake five: setting an objective that is too easy or too hard.

The fifth mistake is to aim too low or too high. An objective that is too easy is reached without effort, and teaches nothing. An objective that is too hard is abandoned early. A good objective is a stretch: hard enough to need effort, but possible for a capable person.

### Four Tests for a Good Objective

> A good objective passes four tests.

When the objective is written, the manager applies four tests. Each test is a question that can be answered yes or no.

> Test one: could a stranger check it?

Test one: could a stranger check whether it was reached? If two people would disagree about whether the objective was met, the objective is too vague. Return and make it more exact.

> Test two: does it serve the level above?

Test two: does it serve the objective at the level above? An objective that helps nobody higher up wastes effort.

> Test three: is it within the person's control?

Test three: can the person who owns it influence the result? To ask a rider to raise sales is unfair, since the rider does not sell. To ask the rider to deliver on time is fair.

> Test four: does it fit with the other objectives?

Test four: does it clash with other objectives? A firm that asks for the highest output and the lowest waste at once should ask whether these pull in opposite directions. We return to this point later in the chapter.

### Practice: Making Vague Goals Measurable

> Practice on vague goals shows how the four questions work.

The best way to learn is to practise. The table gives four vague goals, as a manager might first state them, and a better version of each.

:table From vague goal to objective
| Vague goal | What is wrong | Better objective |
| Improve our service | No measure, no date | At least 95 orders in every 100 delivered on the day promised, by the end of month 12 |

| Sell more | No amount, no date | Raise sales from Tk 400,000 to Tk 450,000 a month by the end of month 10 |

> Each better objective names a measure, an amount and a date.

Look at the right-hand column. Each objective names a result, an amount and a date. A stranger could check each one. Notice also that each objective could be reached in more than one way, so the objective leaves room for the people to choose how.

> You should now be able to turn a vague goal into an objective.

You should now be able to say this: to set an objective, ask what exactly we want, how we will measure it, by when, and who will own it, then test it for checking, fit, control and clashes.

### When Objectives Clash

> Sometimes two good objectives pull in opposite directions.

Objectives do not always agree. A bakery may wish to raise output and also to cut waste. When the ovens work faster, more loaves are burnt or undercooked. When the baker takes more care, output slows. The two objectives are each sensible, and together they pull against each other.

> A manager should find the clash early and decide what matters more.

The manager should find such clashes before the work begins. There are three ways to deal with them. The first is to rank the objectives, and say which matters more if both cannot be reached. The second is to set the objectives together, so that the people who must reach them can say what is possible. The third is to set one objective that joins both, such as the number of good loaves per hour. A joint measure removes the clash.

> A table shows the three ways in use.

The next table sets the three ways in order, with the bakery example.

:table Three ways to settle a clash between objectives
| Way | What the manager does | Bakery example |
| Rank them | Decides which matters more | Waste is no more than 5 per cent, even if output is slightly lower |
| Set them together | Asks the people involved what both allow | The head baker says the ovens can reach 300 with 5 per cent waste |
| Join them | Writes one measure that includes both | Good loaves per staff hour |

## Management by Objectives

### The Idea and Its Origin

@ Management by objectives | A method in which managers and staff agree objectives together and review results at fixed times (MBO).

> Management by objectives is a way of running a firm by agreed objectives at every level.

Setting objectives for a firm is one thing. Running a firm through objectives is another. The second idea is called **management by objectives**, and it is usually shortened to **MBO**. Management by objectives is a method in which managers and staff agree on objectives together, work toward them, and review the results at fixed times.

> Drucker gave the method its name in 1954.

The idea is linked to the writer Peter Drucker (1909–2005). In his book *The Practice of Management*, published in 1954, he argued that a firm should be managed by agreed objectives in every key area, and that each manager should know the objectives of the whole and the objective of his or her own part.^2 Other writers, and some firms, helped to develop the method. We treat Drucker's book as the landmark source.

^2: Check the date, the title and the argument against the book itself before printing.

> MBO changes the usual picture of how objectives are set.

In the older picture, a senior manager decides what each person must do and tells them. In MBO, the manager and the person talk, and agree on the objective together. The manager brings the needs of the firm. The person brings knowledge of what is possible. Out of the talk comes an objective that both accept.

### Four Features of MBO

> MBO has four features that distinguish it from other methods.

Four features mark management by objectives.

> First, objectives are set together.

First, objectives are set together. The manager and the person agree. This agreement is more than a courtesy. A person who helped to set an objective understands it and feels responsible for it.

> Second, objectives cascade through the firm.

Second, objectives cascade. The word cascade means to flow down in steps, like water over a series of rocks. The firm's objectives flow down to the departments, and the departments' objectives flow down to each person, as the figure of the hierarchy showed.

> Third, each person is judged on results.

Third, people are judged on results against the agreed objectives. The manager asks: did you reach what we agreed? The manager does not ask only how many hours were worked. This puts attention on what the work achieves.

> Fourth, there are regular reviews.

Fourth, reviews are held at fixed times. At each review the manager and the person look at the results together, discuss the reasons for any gap, and agree on what to do next.

### An MBO Conversation

> One conversation shows how MBO differs from giving orders.

A short example shows how MBO sounds. The owner meets the person who will become head baker. In the older method, the owner might say: "You will bake 300 loaves a day." In MBO, the owner says: "The shop plans to reach 300 loaves a day by month 12, and to keep waste low. What can the baking team reach, and what will you need?" The candidate replies that 300 loaves are possible if a second oven is bought and a helper is hired, and that waste can fall to 5 per cent. The two agree on the objective, and on the help that will be given. The objective is now a shared promise and not an order.

@! Go slowly here. In MBO the manager also promises something: help and resources.

> In MBO, the manager has duties too.

Notice that the owner promised something: an oven and a helper. In MBO the manager also has duties. A manager who sets an objective and gives no support has used the language of MBO without its spirit.

## The MBO Cycle

@ MBO cycle | The repeating round of agreeing objectives, planning, acting, reviewing and resetting.

> MBO is not a single event but a repeating cycle of five stages.

MBO is not done once. It runs in a repeating round that we call the **MBO cycle**. The MBO cycle is the round of agreeing objectives, planning the action, acting, reviewing progress, and then appraising and resetting. The figure below shows the five stages.

:fig The MBO cycle | fig_mbo.svg

### Stage One: Agree Objectives

> The first stage is to agree objectives from the top down and from the bottom up.

The owner or top manager first sets the firm's objectives, using the key result areas. Then each manager meets each person to agree the person's objectives. The objectives pass the four tests of the last section.

### Stage Two: Plan the Action

> The second stage is to plan how each objective will be reached.

Each person then writes a short plan: the steps, the resources needed and the dates. This stage links MBO to the planning of Chapter VI. The manager reads the plan, discusses it, and agrees on any help that will be given.

### Stage Three: Act, With Support

> The third stage is to do the work, with help from the manager.

The people now do the work. The manager's role is to support: to remove obstacles, to supply what was promised, to coach, and to leave the person room to decide how to proceed. A manager who stands over every task defeats the purpose of MBO.

### Stage Four: Review Progress

> The fourth stage is the review of progress at fixed times.

At agreed intervals, the manager and the person look at the results. They compare actual results with the objective. If the results fall short, they ask why. The cause may be in the person, the plan, the resources, or the objective itself. Chapter XIII treats this comparison as the heart of controlling.

### Stage Five: Appraise and Reset

> The fifth stage appraises the period and sets new objectives.

At the end of the period, the manager and the person appraise the whole. Appraise means to judge the worth of something. They ask what was reached, what was learned and what should change. New objectives are then agreed, and the cycle begins again. Each round starts with better knowledge than the last.

### Holding a Good Review Meeting

> The review meeting is the heart of the cycle, and it needs care.

The review is where MBO succeeds or fails. A poor review feels like a trial, and people hide problems. A good review feels like a shared study of how the work is going. A few habits help.

> The manager should prepare, listen and keep to the facts.

First, prepare. The manager reads the figures before the meeting, and asks the person to do the same. Second, begin with what went well. People who hear nothing but faults stop speaking. Third, listen. The person closest to the work often knows the true cause of a gap. Fourth, keep to facts and avoid blame. The question is not "who failed?" but "what stood in the way?" Fifth, end with agreement on what will happen next, and write it down in a few lines.

> The meeting should also ask if the objective is still right.

A last habit is often forgotten. The manager and the person should ask whether the objective itself is still right. If flour has doubled in price, or an oven has failed, an objective set last month may no longer be fair. A good review is willing to change the objective, and does not insist on a number that has lost its meaning.

### Measuring Aims That Are Hard to Count

> Some important aims resist counting, but a plain test can still be found.

Some aims, such as friendliness, skill or trust, are hard to count. Yet a manager should not drop them. There are three ways to measure them. One is to use a sign that can be counted: the number of customers who return, or the number of compliments received, can stand in for good service. A second is to use a plain test: a customer who orders by telephone is greeted within three rings. A third is to ask the people concerned, through a short question put to customers or staff each month.

> A sign is not the aim itself, and care is needed.

Remember that a sign is not the thing itself. A count of compliments may rise because staff ask customers for praise, and not because the service improved. The manager should look at the sign with some doubt, and check it against what is seen and heard on the shop floor.

### Mini-Case: A Clinic Sets Objectives

> A clinic shows MBO in a service where profit is not the aim.

Consider a small clinic with a doctor, two nurses and a receptionist. The clinic's purpose is to treat patients well, and its income only has to cover its costs. The doctor and staff meet to set objectives for the next six months.

> The staff agree three objectives that can be checked.

Three objectives are agreed. First, no patient with an appointment should wait more than 20 minutes in at least 90 visits in every 100. The receptionist and nurses share this objective. Second, every patient who needs a follow-up visit should be given a date before leaving, in all cases. The receptionist owns this one. Third, the records of every visit should be completed on the same day. Each nurse owns this one.

> The objectives pass the tests, and show a danger.

We apply the four tests. A stranger can check each objective, since waiting times and records can be counted. Each serves the clinic's aim of good care. Each owner can influence the result. The first objective, however, shows a danger. A nurse who wishes to meet the 20-minute limit might hurry a patient. The doctor therefore adds a fourth objective for quality, by asking patients each month whether they felt rushed. The clinic keeps both numbers in view. This shows how a manager can guard against the distortion that a single measure may cause.

> You should now be able to describe the MBO cycle.

You should now be able to say this: management by objectives is a method in which managers and staff agree objectives together, and it runs as a cycle of agreeing objectives, planning, acting, reviewing and resetting.

## Benefits and Weaknesses of MBO

> MBO has real gains and real dangers, and a manager should know both.

No method is perfect. We list the gains first, and then the dangers, and then we show how a careful manager can reduce the dangers.

### The Benefits

> The first benefit is clearer direction.

The first benefit is clear direction. Each person knows what is expected, and can see how it connects to the aims of the firm. People waste less time on work that serves no purpose.

> The second benefit is stronger commitment.

The second benefit is commitment. People who help to set their objectives feel that the objectives are their own. They are likely to try harder than people who obey orders.

> The third benefit is better control and fairer judgement.

The third benefit is better control. Because objectives are measurable, the manager can see where results fall short, and can act early. Judgement of people is also fairer, since it rests on agreed results and not on the manager's mood.

> The fourth benefit is better talk between manager and staff.

The fourth benefit is better communication. The regular talks give people a chance to say what they need and to raise problems. The manager learns what is happening at the working level.

> The fifth benefit is better development of people.

The fifth benefit is development. People who set objectives and review them learn how to plan, to measure and to judge their own work. Many learn to take on larger tasks.

### The Weaknesses

> The first weakness is that objectives can take over from the real purpose.

The first weakness is that objectives may distort behaviour. People tend to do what is measured. If an objective counts loaves baked, people may bake carelessly to raise the count. If it counts speed of delivery, the rider may take dangerous risks. The measure then replaces the purpose that it was meant to serve.

> The second weakness is that what is easy to measure gets attention.

The second weakness is that easy things get attention. Some important aims, such as friendliness or creativity, are hard to measure. Under MBO they may be neglected, because they do not appear in the objectives.

> The third weakness is that it takes time and paper.

The third weakness is the burden of time. Setting objectives with each person, writing plans and holding reviews take many hours. In a large firm, the paper may grow until it hides the work.

> The fourth weakness is that it can be used as a way to push people harder.

The fourth weakness is misuse. A manager may impose the objectives and call it MBO. Then the method has the name but not the agreement, and people feel pressed and not helped.

> The fifth weakness is that a changing world can make objectives out of date.

The fifth weakness is rigidity. If the world changes during the year, the objectives set at the start may no longer make sense. A firm that holds to them can drift away from what it now needs.

### Reducing the Dangers

> Most of the dangers can be reduced by care.

A careful manager can lower these dangers. Set a small number of objectives, so that no one is overloaded. Choose measures that reflect the true purpose. Include some objectives for quality, not only for amount. Review objectives during the year and change them when the facts change. And above all, keep the agreement real: if the person has no voice, the method does not work.

The table below puts the gains and the dangers side by side, with the remedy.

You should now be able to say this: MBO gives direction, commitment, control, communication and development, but it can distort behaviour, favour what is easy to measure, use much time, be misused, and become out of date, and these dangers can be reduced by care.

### MBO and the Other Parts of Management

> MBO connects to the other functions and to later chapters.

Management by objectives is not a separate world. It connects to many of the ideas that appear in other chapters. We name three links here, and later chapters develop them.

> The first link is with planning.

The first link is with planning, which we studied in Chapter VI. An objective is the second step of the planning process, and MBO is a way of carrying that step through the whole firm. Objectives come first, and plans follow. If the objectives change, the plans must change as well.

> The second link is with motivation.

The second link is with motivation. People work harder when they take part in choosing the result and see how it serves a purpose they respect. Chapter XI studies what drives people to work, and it will show that this effect has a long record in research.

> The third link is with controlling.

The third link is with controlling. An agreed objective is a standard, and the monthly review compares results with the standard. This is the process of Chapter XIII. When the comparison shows a gap, the manager acts to close it. MBO may thus be seen as planning, motivating and controlling joined in one repeating method.

### The Cost of Measuring

> Measuring takes effort, and the manager should weigh the cost.

Every measure has a cost. Someone must collect the figure, write it down and read it. A measure that costs more than it is worth should be dropped. At the bakery, counting loaves at the end of the day takes a minute and is worth it. Timing every customer at the counter might take hours and tell the owner little. A good manager measures the few things that matter most, in the simplest way that gives a reliable answer.

> A measure should be reliable and easy to understand.

A good measure has two further qualities. It is reliable, which means that two people counting the same thing would reach the same answer. And it is easy to understand, which means that the people being measured know what is counted and how. Nobody can work toward a number that they do not understand.

### The Ladder: One Idea, Four Settings

> Four settings show the ideas of the chapter at work.

We now apply the chapter in four settings, from the simplest to a case that joins several ideas.

> First setting: one objective with one measure.

*One objective, one measure.* A baker wishes to waste less dough. The goal is vague. The baker states the objective: no more than 5 loaves in every 100 spoiled, by the end of month 9. The measure is the count of spoiled loaves each day. A stranger can check it. This is the clean case: one result, one number, one date.

> Second setting: clashing objectives.

*Clashing objectives.* The owner also asks the baker to reach 300 loaves a day. The baker points out that fast baking spoils loaves. The two objectives clash. The owner and the baker agree to a joint measure: good loaves per staff hour. The joint measure lets the baker work at a pace that gives both output and care.

> Third setting: a charity.

*A charity.* A charity feeds poor families in a town. It cannot count profit, so its measures differ. The director agrees with the volunteers: serve 400 meals a week by the end of month 6, and keep complaints at no more than one in every hundred meals. The measures are not money, yet they can be checked. This setting shows that MBO is not limited to firms that sell for profit.

> Fourth setting: a cascade from owner to staff.

*A cascade.* The owner of a bakery sets a firm-level objective for month 12. The head baker agrees the part for the baking team. The head baker then agrees a part with each baker, who decides how to reach it. The three levels fit like the figure of the hierarchy. If each person reaches the personal objective, the firm reaches its own. The next section shows this cascade in a worked case.

## A Worked Case: Agreeing Objectives at the Bakery

> The case applies the chapter to the bakery's seventh month.

This case has five parts: the situation, the question, the reasoning, the conclusion and a check.

> The case states the facts first.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Month 7. The Morning Loaf Bakery has a plan to reach 300 loaves a day by month 12, described in Chapter VI. The owner decides to turn the plan into objectives and to agree them one by one with the head-baker candidate, the counter assistant and the rider. Three firm objectives are set: 300 loaves a day by month 12, waste of no more than 5 per cent of loaves baked, and at least 95 per cent of orders delivered on time. The owner will review progress with each person every month.

> The question asks how the objectives are agreed and tested.

*Question.* How should the owner agree these objectives with each person, and do they pass the tests?

> The reasoning follows the MBO cycle.

*Reasoning.* Stage one: agree. The owner meets the head-baker candidate first, and proposes the output and waste objectives. The candidate agrees to both on condition that a second oven is bought, which is a matter for the decision of Chapter VIII. The owner then meets the rider and agrees the objective for on-time delivery. The owner meets the counter assistant and agrees that orders will be packed and labelled by an agreed hour each day, since late packing causes late delivery. The last objective is the counter assistant's own part of the on-time objective.

> The reasoning continues through the other stages.

Stage two: each person writes a short plan. The head baker's plan names the oven, the trainee helper and the dates. Stage three: the work begins, and the owner supports it by buying the oven and not by checking every tray. Stage four: at the end of each month, the owner and each person compare results with the objective. Stage five: at the end of the six months, the owner and staff appraise the period and set new objectives.

> We test the objectives against the four tests.

Now we apply the four tests. Could a stranger check each objective? Yes: loaves can be counted, waste can be counted, and deliveries on time can be counted. Does each serve the level above? Yes, each supports the firm's plan. Can each owner influence the result? Yes: the baker controls baking and waste, the rider controls delivery, the counter assistant controls packing. Do any clash? Possibly: higher output may raise waste. The owner therefore asks the baker to watch both numbers together.

> The conclusion states the result.

*Conclusion.* The bakery now has three firm objectives, each owned by a named person, each measurable, each dated, and each agreed in conversation. The objectives are linked to the plan, and a monthly review is fixed.

> The check measures what the waste objective is worth.

*Check.* We test what the waste objective is worth. At 300 loaves a day, 5 per cent waste means 15 loaves, since 300 × 0.05 = 15. If waste were 8 per cent, it would be 24 loaves, since 300 × 0.08 = 24. The difference is 9 loaves a day, or 9 × 26 = 234 loaves in a month of 26 trading days. At about Tk 27 of ingredients a loaf, the saving is about 234 × 27 = Tk 6,318 a month. The objective is worth the effort of measuring it.

## Summary

> The summary restates the main ideas in a few lines.

An objective is a specific, measurable result with a time limit, set so that it can be checked. Objectives are arranged in a hierarchy from the whole firm to each person, and firms set them in key result areas such as market standing, productivity and profitability. A good objective passes four tests: a stranger could check it, it serves the level above, the owner can influence it, and it does not clash with others.

> MBO and its cycle are the central ideas.

Management by objectives is a method in which managers and staff agree objectives together, work toward them, and review the results at fixed times. It runs as a cycle of five stages: agree objectives, plan the action, act with support, review progress, and appraise and reset. MBO gives direction, commitment, control, communication and development. It can also distort behaviour, favour what is easy to measure, use much time, be misused and become out of date. Care in use reduces these dangers.

## Where People Go Wrong

> A first error is to treat a goal and an objective as the same.

*Error:* Any wish is an objective. *Correction:* A goal gives a direction. An objective says how much and by when, and can be checked.

> A second error is to think MBO means the manager sets targets and the staff obey.

*Error:* MBO means the manager sets the targets and the staff reach them. *Correction:* In MBO the objectives are agreed, and the manager owes help and resources as well.

> A third error is to measure only what is easy.

*Error:* If a result is hard to measure, it does not belong in the objectives. *Correction:* Hard-to-measure aims such as quality or friendliness may need a plain test, and a firm that ignores them can win its numbers and lose its purpose.

> A fourth error is to set objectives once and forget them.

*Error:* Once objectives are agreed, they stay fixed for the year. *Correction:* Objectives are reviewed at fixed times and revised when the facts change.

## In History

> Drucker's 1954 book made objectives a central idea of management.

In 1954 Peter Drucker published *The Practice of Management*. In it he argued that a manager should not be judged by effort or by long hours, but by results measured against objectives that the manager had helped to set. He also argued that every business needs objectives in several key areas, and not in profit alone.^3 The book was widely read, and the method of managing by objectives spread to many firms and public bodies in the following decades. Later writers added criticism, and firms changed the method, but the central idea that people should work toward agreed, measurable results remains in wide use.
