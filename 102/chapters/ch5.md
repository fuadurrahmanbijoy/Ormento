:mood What an organisation owes to others, beyond its profit
:desc A firm may obey every law and still harm the people near it. This chapter asks what a firm owes to society, how far its duty reaches, and how a manager can tell right from wrong when the law is silent. It presents the case for and against social responsibility, a model by Keith Davis, the idea of sustainability, a plain test for ethical choices, and four ways in which a firm may answer pressure from society.

> A workshop owner holds a valid licence, yet his neighbours cannot sleep.

Mr Karim owns a small metal workshop in a residential lane. He has a licence from the city authority, and he pays his taxes on time. His workers beat sheets of metal until eleven o'clock at night, because customers want the orders early. A family lives next door. Their eldest daughter studies for an important examination, and the noise comes through the wall until midnight.

> The owner has broken no law, but the neighbours are harmed.

The family asks Mr Karim to stop at nine o'clock. He looks at his licence. It says nothing about the hour. No official has complained, and no rule has been broken. If he stops at nine, he will lose about one fifth of his orders. His work is lawful, and his workers need their wages. Yet the family suffers, and the suffering is a result of his own decisions.

> The puzzle asks whether a lawful act can still be wrong, and what a manager owes to people outside the firm.

Here is the puzzle of this chapter. The law does not forbid what Mr Karim does. Is it enough that an act is lawful? What does an organisation owe to the people near it, and to people not yet born? And how can a manager decide, when no rule gives the answer? These questions lead to three ideas: social responsibility, sustainability and ethics. They are related, but they are not the same, and a manager who mixes them will reason badly about all three.

> The chapter follows from Chapter 4: forces and stakeholders around the firm now ask what the firm should give back.

In Chapter 4 we studied the forces around an organisation and the stakeholders who carry them. That chapter asked how the firm should answer these forces for its own sake. This chapter asks a different question: what does the firm owe to its stakeholders, and to society, whether or not they can force it to pay? The answer will shape the plans of Chapter 6, because a plan that ignores what is owed to others is a plan that may later fail.

## 5.1 Business and Society

> A firm needs society, and society needs the firm, so each affects the other.

An organisation does not stand apart from society. It uses roads, public order, trained workers and the trust of customers, all of which society provides. In return it supplies goods, jobs, taxes and income to the families who work for it. The bakery of our case file gives bread to its customers, wages to its staff and rent to its landlord. It also uses the lane in front of its door, makes smoke and noise, and leaves waste. Each firm therefore gives and takes. The question of this section is how far its duty extends beyond what it gives in the normal course of business.

> Social responsibility is the duty to act for the good of society as well as for profit.

@ Social responsibility | The duty of an organisation to act for the good of society as well as for its own profit.
The name for this duty is **social responsibility**. It means the duty of an organisation to act for the good of society, as well as for its own profit. The words "as well as" matter. Social responsibility does not ask a firm to give up profit. It asks the firm to consider the effects of its actions on others, and to accept some cost when the effects are harmful. A small example is a shop owner who sweeps the street in front of the shop. No law requires it. The owner does it because a clean street is good for everyone.^1

^1: Some writers use the longer name corporate social responsibility, often shortened to CSR. In this book we say social responsibility for any organisation, whether or not it is a company.

> Responsibility has layers: the firm must first earn, then obey the law, then act rightly, then give freely.

How wide is this duty? One useful answer divides it into four layers, which the figure below shows.^2 The first layer is economic: the firm must make goods or services that people want, and it must earn enough to survive. The second is legal: the firm must obey the laws of the country. The third is ethical: the firm must do what is right, even where no law demands it. The fourth is discretionary, which means a matter of free choice: the firm gives time or money to a good purpose because it wishes to, such as a bakery that gives bread to a school.

^2: This four-part division follows Archie Carroll (1979); he later drew it as a pyramid. The wording here is our own.

:fig The four layers of a firm's responsibility | figures/fig5_layers.svg

> The layers are ordered, and a firm that fails a lower layer cannot make up for it by giving at a higher one.

The order of the layers is important. A firm that sells unsafe bread cannot repair the fault by giving money to a charity. The lower layers come first. A firm must be economically sound, or it will not survive to help anyone. It must obey the law, or it injures others and loses its right to trade. Only then can the ethical and discretionary layers be judged. The two upper layers are the field in which Mr Karim's choice lies. His workshop is economic and legal. The question left open is whether it is ethical.

> The idea of social responsibility has two opposed views, and a manager should know the best argument of each.

Not everyone accepts that a firm owes anything beyond profit and law. The debate is old, and each side has serious arguments. A manager should know both. We shall state the case for social responsibility first, then the case against, in the form that each side's defenders would present it. At the end we shall say how most managers settle the matter in practice.

### The case for

> The first argument for is that long-term profit depends on the goodwill of society.

The first argument for is self-interest over a long period. A firm that harms its neighbours, deceives its customers or pollutes its district loses goodwill, which means the good opinion of the people around it. Customers leave, workers do not stay, and officials look at the firm more closely. A firm that treats people well gains trust, and trust is a form of capital that does not appear in the accounts. The gain may take years to arrive, but it is real.

> The second argument is that firms hold power, and power brings duties; the third is that firms can prevent stricter laws.

The second argument is that a firm holds power, and power brings duty. A large employer decides the fortunes of a whole town. A factory decides whether the river stays clean. Those who hold such power affect many people who had no voice in the decision. The third argument is practical. If firms act responsibly of their own will, governments need not pass strict laws to force them. The fourth is that firms have resources and skills that society lacks, and the fifth is that a healthy society is a better place in which to do business. A district with clean water, good schools and public order is a better market than one without them.

### The case against

> The first argument against is that managers are agents of the owners, and owners hire them to produce profit.

The case against begins with a question of duty. Managers act for the owners of the firm. The owners have put their money at risk, and they expect managers to use it to earn a return. Milton Friedman (1970) argued that a manager who spends the firm's money on social aims spends the owners' money without their consent.^3 In his view the responsibility of business is to use its resources to increase profit, within the law and without deceit. Other duties belong to individuals and to governments.

^3: Friedman wrote this in an essay in *The New York Times Magazine*, 13 September 1970.

> Further arguments against concern cost, skill and accountability.

There are three further arguments. The first is cost. Money spent on social aims raises the firm's costs, and costs reach customers as higher prices or fall on workers as lower wages. The second is skill. A bakery knows how to bake, and it may not know how to run a school or to clean a river. The third is accountability, which means being answerable for results. A government is elected and can be removed. A manager who decides social goals for a district has not been elected and cannot easily be called to account.

> The table sets the two cases side by side so that they can be compared point by point.

The table below compares the two cases. Read each row across. The first column names the point at issue. The second gives the argument of those who favour social responsibility, and the third gives the reply of those who oppose it.

:table The case for and the case against social responsibility
| Point at issue | The case for | The case against |
| Profit | Goodwill brings profit over a long period | Managers are paid to earn profit for the owners |
| Power | Those with power over many lives owe a duty | Duties of this kind belong to governments |
| Law | Responsible firms make strict laws less likely | Laws, not managers, should set the limits |
| Cost | Harm to society becomes a cost to all, including the firm | Social aims raise prices and reduce wages |
| Skill | Firms hold money, staff and knowledge | Firms are trained for business, not for social work |
| Answerability | A firm that is open about its acts can be held to account | A manager who sets social goals has not been chosen to do so |

> In practice most managers accept a middle position: do the core work well, obey the law, avoid harm, and give where the firm can.

How do managers settle the debate? Most accept a middle position. They agree with the opponents that a firm must first earn its living and obey the law. They agree with the supporters that a firm must avoid harming others and should deal honestly with all who depend on it. The disagreement that remains is about the upper layer, the free giving of money and effort. There, reasonable people differ, and each firm decides for itself.

> The position is not a rule of nature; exceptions exist, and the best argument depends on the size and power of the firm.

This middle position has exceptions. A very small firm has little power and little money to give, and its chief duty is to avoid harm and deal honestly. A very large firm has great power, and its duty to use the power with care is greater. A firm in a poor district may find that the needs of society are large and the money available is small. In such a case honesty and avoidance of harm are the main duties, and gifts are small. We shall return to power and duty in the next section.

:box IN SHORT
Social responsibility is the duty to act for society's good as well as for profit. It has four layers: economic, legal, ethical and discretionary. Those in favour point to goodwill, power and the cost of harm. Those against point to the duty to owners, the cost, and the question of who has chosen the manager to set social aims.

> A short test shows whether the reader can tell the layers apart.

You should now be able to say: social responsibility means a duty to society beyond profit; it has four layers in a fixed order; and the case for and the case against each rest on strong arguments. As a test, place each of the following in a layer: paying the tax that the law requires, giving bread to a school, selling a good loaf at a fair price, and refusing to sell a product that is known to be unsafe, even though no law forbids it. The answers are legal, discretionary, economic and ethical.

## 5.2 Davis's Model of Social Responsibility

> Keith Davis offered a model that links responsibility to power, and it explains why responsibility grows with the size of a firm.

@ Davis model | A set of propositions linking the social power of a firm to its social responsibility.
The debate in 5.1 asks whether a firm should be responsible. Keith Davis, a professor of management in the United States, asked a different question: how much responsibility goes with how much power? His answer is the **Davis model**, which is a set of propositions linking a firm's social power to its duties.^4 In this chapter we use his model in its common form: one central law and five propositions.

^4: Davis set out five propositions in an article in *Business Horizons* (1975), and developed the ideas with Robert Blomstrom in their book *Business and Society*. The wording of the propositions below is our own paraphrase.

> The central idea is that responsibility rises and falls with social power.

@! Go slowly here.
The central idea is simple. Social power means the ability of an organisation to affect the lives of other people and the state of the places where they live. A firm that employs ten workers has little social power. A firm that employs ten thousand has a great deal. Davis said that responsibility should grow with power. The more a firm can affect society, the more it owes to society. We shall call this the power-responsibility link.

> The iron law says that a firm that fails to use its power responsibly will, over time, lose it.

The model also carries a warning, which Davis named the iron law of responsibility. It says that over a long period, those who do not use their social power in a way society sees as responsible will tend to lose that power.^5 The loss may come through new laws, through lost customers or through public anger. The word "iron" means that the effect cannot be avoided in the long run. Consider a river that a factory pollutes year after year. Sooner or later, neighbours complain, officials act, and the factory may be forced to close or to pay heavily.

^5: Davis put this idea forward in the 1960s.

> The five propositions describe what a firm should do if it accepts that its power brings duty.

We now state the five propositions. Each is followed by an example from the bakery or from a new setting, so that the meaning is clear.

> Proposition one: responsibility arises from social power.

*One: social responsibility arises from social power.* This is the central link. A firm owes more because it can do more. A bakery with four staff and a firm with four thousand staff do not owe the same. A small bakery should not harm its customers or its neighbours. A large textile factory that is the main employer of a district must also think about the safety of its buildings, the quality of the water and the health of its workers, because it can change the lives of all of them.

> Proposition two: the firm should be open to society, listening and telling.

*Two: the firm should work as an open system.* In Chapter 3 we met the idea that an organisation is made of linked parts. An open system is one that takes in information from outside and gives information back. For Davis, a firm should listen to the opinions of the public and should tell the public truthfully what it does. The bakery listens when a customer complains about a label. It tells the truth when the label states the ingredients. A firm that hides its acts, or that does not listen, cannot know what society expects of it.

> Proposition three: the firm should count the social costs and benefits of each act before it begins.

*Three: social costs and benefits should be counted before the firm acts.* A social cost is a harm that falls on people other than the firm and its customers: noise, smoke, waste, risk. A social benefit is a gain that falls on them: jobs, training, cleaner air. Davis said that managers should estimate both before deciding whether to go ahead. Mr Karim's workshop would count the lost sleep of the family and its effect on their studies, along with his own gain from late orders. Such counting is not easy, because many social costs cannot be measured in money. But even a list of costs, without numbers, improves the decision.

> Proposition four: social costs should be priced into the product, so that the buyer pays for the harm the product causes.

*Four: social costs should be built into the price.* If a product causes harm to others as it is made or used, the cost of limiting that harm should be added to the price, so that the buyer pays it. This is sometimes called making the user pay. If the factory does not do this, the cost does not disappear. It falls on others: on the people who drink the river water, or on the government that must clean it. We shall work through a numerical case in 5.3.

> Proposition five: a firm, as a citizen, should help with some social problems outside its usual work.

*Five: firms should take part in some social problems outside their normal work.* Davis argued that a firm is a member of society, as a person is. A firm that has the skill, the money or the staff to help with a serious problem near it, and that is better placed to help than others, should do so. A bakery near a school can supply bread for a midday meal. A construction firm can lend engineers after a flood. Notice the limits that Davis set: the firm should have the means, and the problem should be one that the firm is able to help to solve.

> The table sets out the five propositions with one example each from a bakery and from a textile factory.

The table below sets out the five propositions. Read each row from left to right. The first column gives the proposition in a few words. The second and third give a bakery example and a factory example, so that you can see the same idea in two different settings.

:table The five propositions, with examples
| Proposition | Bakery example | Textile factory example |
| 1. Responsibility rises with power | A small shop avoids harm; a chain of shops owes more | The main employer of a district owes more than a small workshop |
| 2. Be open: listen and tell | Truthful labels; a note for complaints | Publish water and safety reports; meet local people |
| 3. Count social costs and benefits first | List the effect of a new oven's smoke on neighbours | List the effect of a new dye plant on the river |
| 4. Build social costs into the price | Pay for bags that will rot in the soil and add the cost to the price | Pay for water treatment and add the cost to the price of cloth |
| 5. Help with some problems outside the firm's work | Supply bread to a school meal | Lend a building for local training |

:fig Davis's model: power, responsibility and the five propositions | figures/fig5_davis.svg

> The figure shows the model as one chain: power, then responsibility, then the five acts, with the iron law as a warning below.

The figure gathers the model in one picture. Social power stands on the left. The power-responsibility link leads to social responsibility, and the five propositions show what responsibility means in practice. The arrow that runs below warns of the loss of power when responsibility is neglected. Read the figure from left to right, then read the warning.

> Several exceptions and criticisms limit the model.

The model has limits, and a careful reader should know them. First, "social power" is hard to measure. A firm may have great power in one town and little in another. Second, the propositions state what firms should do, but they do not say how much. Proposition five, for instance, gives no amount. Third, those who oppose social responsibility, as in 5.1, reject the model's basis, because they deny that power brings duty beyond the law. Fourth, in some cases the costs in proposition three cannot be known before the act. The model is therefore a guide to reasoning and not a formula that gives a single answer.

> Davis's model fits our case: Mr Karim's workshop, though small, still holds some power over the people next door.

Let us apply the model to Mr Karim. Proposition one says that his responsibility is limited because his power is small. Yet his power over the family next door is large, since he decides how well they can sleep. Proposition two says that he should listen. The family has asked him to stop at nine, and he has heard the request. Proposition three says that he should count the cost: one fifth of his orders on one side, the rest and the studies of a young person on the other. Proposition four suggests that he might build the cost of quieter work into his prices. We shall return to this in the closing pack.

> You should now be able to state the central link, the iron law and the five propositions.

You should now be able to say: responsibility rises with social power; a firm that fails to use power responsibly may in time lose it; and the five propositions describe how a responsible firm acts. Try this on a new case. A large clinic in a small town is the only provider of care for forty kilometres. Which proposition most clearly applies to its power? The answer is the first, because its power over the town's health is great, and its responsibility should be great also.

## 5.3 Sustainability in Organisations

> A village that catches every fish this year will have none next year.

Think of a village beside a large pond. Every family catches fish. In a good year, each family catches more than before, and they sell the surplus. If they catch all the fish, including the young ones, the pond is empty by the next season. Fishing was a good way to live, but it could not continue. The families gained today and lost tomorrow. The same lesson applies to a bakery that uses up its profit, a factory that uses up a river, or a firm that uses up its workers' health.

> Sustainability means meeting today's needs without harming the ability of later generations to meet theirs.

@ Sustainability | Meeting the needs of today without harming the ability of future people to meet their own needs.
The word for this idea is **sustainability**. It means meeting the needs of today without harming the ability of future people to meet their own needs.^6 The word comes from "to sustain", which means to keep going. A practice is sustainable if it can continue for a long time without destroying what it depends on. The village fishing was not sustainable. A rule that each family may take only half the fish each year would be.

^6: This definition follows the report *Our Common Future* (1987), written by a commission of the United Nations led by Gro Harlem Brundtland. We state it in our own words.

> Sustainability looks at time, and social responsibility looks at duty, so the two ideas are related but different.

How does sustainability differ from social responsibility? Social responsibility asks what the firm owes to others. Sustainability asks whether the firm's way of working can continue without harm to the future. A firm can be socially responsible this year and still use methods that cannot last. A firm can also have methods that last and still treat its neighbours badly. The two ideas overlap, and a good manager considers both. We shall keep them apart in our definitions.

> Sustainability has three parts: economic, social and environmental, and the three must be considered together.

Many writers divide sustainability into three parts, as the figure shows.^7 The **economic** part asks whether the firm can earn enough to continue. A firm that loses money for years will close, however good its intentions. The **social** part asks whether the firm treats its people and the community fairly, so that workers are healthy and trained and neighbours are not harmed. The **environmental** part asks whether the firm uses land, water, energy and materials in a way that does not leave them damaged or exhausted.

^7: The idea of three parts is often called the triple bottom line, a phrase from John Elkington (1994). The three-part form is common in teaching books. Our wording is our own.

:fig The three parts of sustainability | figures/fig5_sustain.svg

> The three parts overlap, and the middle is where all three are served.

The three circles in the figure overlap, and the middle is the place where all three parts are served. A firm that sits in the middle earns a profit, treats its people well and keeps its surroundings in good order. Many firms are not there. A firm may gain in two parts and lose in the third. A factory may earn well and treat its workers well, yet pour untreated water into a river. A manager should ask of each choice: which parts does it help, and which does it harm?

> The table gives examples of each part for a bakery and for a textile factory.

The table below gives one practice and one measure for each part. Read across each row. The measures are chosen because a manager can count them. What cannot be counted is hard to improve, and measures turn a general wish into a task for the week.

:table Three parts of sustainability, with practices and measures
| Part | Bakery practice | Bakery measure | Textile factory practice | Factory measure |
| Economic | Keep waste low and prices fair | Waste as a share of loaves baked | Plan orders to keep machines busy | Profit per month |
| Social | Safe ovens; fair hours; honest labels | Injuries; staff who leave | Safe buildings; fair wages | Injuries; hours worked |
| Environmental | Compostable bags; less waste | Bags used; bread thrown away | Treat used water before release | Litres treated per metre of cloth |

> Firms have practical reasons to act on sustainability, apart from their duty.

Why would a firm act on sustainability, apart from duty? There are four practical reasons. First, saving: less waste means lower cost. A bakery that bakes only what it can sell saves flour. Second, supply: a flour mill needs good harvests, and a bakery needs flour, so the health of farmland is part of the bakery's future. Third, customers: many buyers now prefer firms that look after people and land. Fourth, law: rules on waste, water and safety grow stricter over time, and a firm that is ready early meets them at lower cost.

> Sustainable acts have a cost, so we measure it with the same care that we use for any other cost.

Sustainable acts often have a cost. We shall measure two of them with the six-step layout of earlier chapters. The first uses small numbers and the second uses numbers a little less tidy. Both link to Davis's fourth proposition, that social costs should be priced into the product.

> The first example measures the cost of compostable bags in the bakery of the case file.

*Given.* The bakery uses 250 bags a day, one for each loaf. A compostable bag costs Tk 0.80 more than the plastic bag it replaces. The bakery trades 26 days a month. The loaf price is Tk 70.
*Find.* The extra cost per day and per month, and the extra cost as a share of the price of a loaf.
*Formula.* Extra cost per day = bags per day × extra cost per bag. Extra cost per month = extra cost per day × trading days. Share of price = extra cost per loaf ÷ price of a loaf.
*Steps.* Extra cost per day = 250 × Tk 0.80 = Tk 200. Extra cost per month = Tk 200 × 26 = Tk 5,200. Share of price = Tk 0.80 ÷ Tk 70 = 0.0114, which is 1.14 per cent.
*Answer.* The bags add Tk 200 to each day and Tk 5,200 to each month. This is about 1.1 per cent of the price of a loaf.
*Check.* The bakery sells 250 × 26 = 6,500 loaves a month. Tk 5,200 ÷ 6,500 = Tk 0.80 for each loaf. The answers agree.

> The second example is a textile factory, in a new setting with the same method.

*Given.* A textile factory makes 120,000 metres of cloth a month. It runs a plant that cleans its used water before release. The plant costs Tk 24,000 a month to run. Cloth sells for Tk 100 a metre. These figures are made up for study.
*Find.* The cost of cleaning per metre of cloth, and its share of the price.
*Formula.* Cost per metre = monthly cost ÷ metres made. Share of price = cost per metre ÷ price per metre.
*Steps.* Cost per metre = Tk 24,000 ÷ 120,000 = Tk 0.20. Share of price = Tk 0.20 ÷ Tk 100 = 0.002, which is 0.2 per cent.
*Answer.* Cleaning the water adds Tk 0.20 to a metre of cloth, which is 0.2 per cent of its price.
*Check.* Tk 0.20 × 120,000 metres = Tk 24,000. The answers agree.

> Common slips: using the wrong base for a share, mixing days with months, and thinking that an unpaid cost has vanished.

Three slips are common. First, a share must use the right base: here the price of a loaf, not the cost of the bag. Second, a cost per day and a cost per month must not be mixed. Third, and most important, a cost that the firm does not pay has not vanished. If the factory does not clean its water, the Tk 24,000 is not saved. It becomes a cost for the people downstream, who must find clean water elsewhere or become ill. This is exactly what Davis's fourth proposition means.

> A manager can start with a short list of measures and improve it each year.

A firm need not measure everything at once. A small firm can begin with three or four figures that it can count without cost: the bread thrown away each week, the bags used each day, the injuries in a month, and the staff who leave in a year. A short list that is kept up is worth more than a long list that is abandoned. After a year, the manager compares the figures with those of the year before, decides which one matters most, and sets a target for it. Chapter 7 will show how to set such a target in a form that can be checked.

> Sustainability has limits and exceptions: trade-offs between the three parts, and claims that are not true.

Sustainability has limits. The three parts sometimes pull against one another. A cleaner method may cost more and put a small firm out of business, which harms its workers. A cheaper method may harm a river and the future of the village. The manager cannot avoid the choice, but can make it openly, with the costs listed. A second danger is a false claim. A firm may say that its product is kind to the land when it has not checked the claim. Such a claim deceives customers and harms honest firms. Honest claims must be checked, as the bakery's label rule in 5.4 requires.

> You should now be able to define sustainability and give its three parts.

You should now be able to say: sustainability is meeting today's needs without harming the future; it has an economic, a social and an environmental part; and a cost that a firm does not pay does not vanish, for it falls on others. Try this on a new case. A tailoring shop throws away fabric scraps, and a neighbour uses them to make quilts. Which part of sustainability does the shop's choice serve? The answer is the environmental part, since waste is reduced, and the social part, since a neighbour gains work.

## 5.4 Business Ethics

> A baker finds a damp sack of flour, and the law does not say whether to use it.

A baker opens a sack of flour on a wet morning and finds that one corner is damp. The flour does not smell bad. The owner is away, and the day's orders are large. The law forbids selling food that is unfit to eat, but it does not say whether this particular flour is fit. Throwing away the sack costs Tk 1,500. Using it carries a small chance that a customer will be harmed. The baker must decide alone, with no rule to read. How should the baker think?

> Ethics means standards of right and wrong, and business ethics applies them to the work of firms.

@ Business ethics | The standards of right and wrong that guide the conduct of people in business.
The subject of this section is **business ethics**, which means the standards of right and wrong that guide the conduct of people in business. Ethics asks what we ought to do, and not only what we must do or what we can do. An ethical choice is one that people with good judgement would call right, after thinking about the effects on all those affected.^8

^8: The word ethics has two uses. It names the study of right and wrong, and it names the standards that a person or group follows. This book uses the second meaning.

> Ethics, law and social responsibility are three different things, and a table can keep them apart.

Ethics is often mixed with law and with social responsibility. The three are related but different. The law is what the government requires, and the courts enforce it. Social responsibility is what an organisation owes to society, as 5.1 explained. Ethics is the standard of right and wrong that guides a person's choices. The three may agree. They may also disagree, which is why we study each. The table below gives the difference.

:table Law, social responsibility and ethics compared
| Question | Law | Social responsibility | Ethics |
| What is it? | Rules made by government | Duty of the organisation to society | Standards of right and wrong |
| Who enforces it? | Courts and officials | Public opinion, stakeholders, the firm's own aims | Conscience, habit, the example of others |
| Who holds it? | Everyone in the country | The organisation as a whole | Each person, and also the organisation |
| Bakery example | Selling food that is fit to eat | Giving unsold bread to those who need it | Not claiming "sugar-free" on a label unless it is checked |

> An act can be legal and unethical, or ethical and not required by law, so the two must be asked separately.

A legal act can be unethical. Mr Karim's late work is legal, and it may be unethical. A firm may sell a product that is legal but known to harm the buyer if used as sold. The reverse also holds. An act may be right and yet not required by law, such as telling a customer about a fault that the customer has not noticed. There are also rare cases in which a law itself is unjust. Most managers meet these cases seldom. The usual lesson is that the law gives the minimum, and ethics asks for more.

> Ethical standards come from many places, and they differ among cultures, though some are shared widely.

Where do ethical standards come from? They come from the family, from religion, from culture, from schools, from the example of colleagues and from the law. Because the sources differ, standards differ among persons and among places. In one place it may be usual to give a gift to an official who helps a firm. In another, the same gift is a bribe. Even so, some standards are shared widely: be honest, treat people fairly, do not harm those who trust you, and keep promises. These four are the base on which a manager can build.

> An ethical dilemma is a choice in which each option has a right side and a cost.

@ Ethical dilemma | A choice in which each option has something right about it and something wrong or costly.
Managers often face an **ethical dilemma**, which is a choice in which each option has something right about it and something wrong or costly. The baker's choice is one. To throw away the flour protects the customers and costs Tk 1,500. To use it saves the money and risks harm. Most dilemmas are not as plain. Honesty may seem to conflict with loyalty to a colleague, and fairness to one group may seem to conflict with fairness to another. The test that follows is meant for these cases.

> Three ways of judging a choice are by its results, by duties and rights, and by fairness.

There are three common ways of judging a choice.^9 The first looks at **results**: which option does the most good and the least harm to all who are affected? The second looks at **duties and rights**: which option keeps our promises and respects the rights of others, such as the right to truthful information? The third looks at **fairness**: which option treats people equally, or gives to each what is due? The three often agree. When they do not, the manager must think more carefully, and should say which approach decided the choice.

^9: These three correspond to the main schools of moral thought: those that judge by results (Bentham, Mill), by duty (Kant) and by justice. We give them in plain words.

> The ethical test turns the three ways of judging into four short questions that a manager can ask in a few minutes.

For daily use the three ways can be turned into four questions.^10 The first is: is it legal? A choice that fails this question is rejected at once. The second is: who is affected, and how? Here the manager lists everyone affected, including people outside the firm, and notes the help or the harm to each. The third is: is it fair? The fourth is: could I tell those affected, openly, what I decided and why? A choice that one would be ashamed to explain is a warning. The figure shows the four questions in order.

^10: The four-question form is our own summary of common teaching practice; it is a guide to judgement and not a formula.

:fig The four-question ethical test | figures/fig5_test.svg

> The four questions apply to the baker's damp flour.

Apply the test to the damp flour. Is it legal? Selling unfit food is not, and it is not certain that the flour is fit, so the answer is doubtful. Who is affected? The customers may be harmed, the business may lose its name, and the owner loses Tk 1,500 if the flour is thrown away. Is it fair? Customers cannot judge the flour, so they depend on the baker. Could the baker tell the customers? Few would say, "We used flour that we feared was damp." The test points toward throwing the flour away, or at least asking the owner and the supplier. An answer reached in this way can be explained to others.

> People act unethically for common reasons, and managers who know them can reduce them.

If most people know the difference between right and wrong, why do some act unethically? There are common reasons. One is pressure to meet a target. When a manager is rewarded only for a number, some people will reach the number by any means, as Chapter 7 will show. A second is an order from a superior. A third is habit: everyone in the group acts in the same way, so no one asks whether it is right. A fourth is the belief that no one is harmed. A fifth is fear: a person who sees wrong-doing may fear the loss of a post for reporting it.

> Managers can support ethical conduct with a written code, with example, with training and with a safe way to report.

@ Code of ethics | A written statement of the standards of conduct that an organisation expects of its people.
What can a manager do? A **code of ethics** is a written statement of the standards of conduct that an organisation expects of its people. A code gives people something to point to when they are pressed to do wrong. The bakery of our case file has a short code of one rule: no claim on a label that is not checked. A code alone is not enough. People copy what their leaders do, so a manager's own example matters more than any paper. Training helps people to see dilemmas early. A safe way to report a concern, so that a person can speak without fear, lets problems come to light while they are small. Chapter 13 will show how control can check that the code is kept.

> The standards in a code can be tested in the same way: by asking the four questions.

Do not make a code too long. A long code is not read. A code of three to five plain rules, each of which can be applied to a real case, is better than a hundred pages. Each rule should answer a question that the staff really meet: what do we do when a label is wrong, when a customer offers a gift, when a supplier offers a favour. The bakery's single rule answers the first of these. It can grow as new questions appear.

> You should now be able to define business ethics, distinguish it from law, and apply the test.

You should now be able to say: ethics is the standard of right and wrong; it differs from law and from social responsibility; and a manager can test a choice by asking four questions. Try this on a new case. A shop owner knows that a rival's product is safer than his own, but his sales staff are told to say that his is the best. Which of the four questions does the instruction fail most plainly? The answer is the fourth: could the staff tell the customers openly what they know?

## 5.5 Responding to Social, Political and Legal Pressure

> A firm meets pressure from society, from government and from the law, and it must choose how to answer.

Firms do not choose all the demands made on them. Neighbours complain, newspapers report, officials inspect, and new laws arrive. In Chapter 4 we named these forces and the stakeholders who carry them. This section asks how a firm should answer when the pressure is about its duty to others. We call the three kinds **social pressure**, which comes from public opinion, groups and the press; **political pressure**, which comes from government policy and officials; and **legal pressure**, which comes from laws and courts.

> The same pressure can be answered in four ways, which we place on a scale from least to most active.

A firm can answer the same pressure in four ways. We name them obstructive, defensive, accommodative and proactive, and place them on a scale from the least active to the most active.^11 The figure shows the scale. Notice that the four names describe a manner of response, not a person's character. The same firm may be obstructive on one matter and proactive on another.

^11: Several writers describe a scale of this kind, including Archie Carroll (1979), with slightly different names. The four names here are those used in many teaching books on management.

:fig Four answers to pressure, from least to most active | figures/fig5_stances.svg

> An obstructive firm denies the problem and resists the pressure.

@ Obstructive | A stance in which the firm denies a problem and resists those who press it to act.
An **obstructive** firm denies that a problem exists, hides the facts and resists those who press it. If neighbours complain of smoke, an obstructive firm says there is no smoke, or closes its gate to visitors. This stance may save money for a time. Its danger is the iron law of 5.2: those who resist society's wishes may lose their power. The obstructive firm also breaks the second and fourth questions of the ethical test.

> A defensive firm does the legal minimum and argues that it need do no more.

@ Defensive | A stance in which the firm does only what the law requires and argues that more is not its duty.
A **defensive** firm admits the problem but does only what the law requires, and argues that nothing more is its duty. If a rule says that smoke must be below a stated level, the defensive firm brings its smoke exactly to that level and no lower. It acts within the law, so it is not a wrong-doer. It earns little goodwill, and when the law grows stricter it must change quickly and at greater cost.

> An accommodative firm does what the law asks and also what society reasonably requests, when asked.

@ Accommodative | A stance in which the firm meets legal duties and also meets reasonable requests from society when it is asked.
An **accommodative** firm meets its legal duties and also meets reasonable requests from society when it is asked. Neighbours ask the firm to close its doors at nine, and the firm agrees. It does not wait to be forced, yet it does not look for problems. Most responsible small firms stand here for most matters, and the stance is adequate in most cases.

> A proactive firm looks ahead, acts before it is asked, and tries to lead others.

@ Proactive | A stance in which the firm looks ahead, acts before it is pressed, and tries to lead others.
A **proactive** firm looks ahead, finds the problems that its work may cause, and acts before it is pressed. It may meet people who live nearby before a new oven is built and ask what worries them. It may publish its own reports. It may help to make a standard that other firms in its trade then follow. The proactive firm accepts a cost early and hopes for trust and fewer shocks later.

> The table shows how the four stances answer the same event: a complaint about the water released by a textile factory.

The table below shows the four stances on one event. A textile factory is told by neighbours that the water in the stream beside it has changed colour. Read each row from left to right. The first column names the stance, the second states what the firm does, and the third gives the likely result.

:table Four stances applied to one complaint
| Stance | What the factory does | Likely result |
| Obstructive | Denies the change and refuses entry to visitors | Anger grows; officials may act; trade may be lost |
| Defensive | Tests the water; cleans only to the legal level; argues that nothing more is due | Peace for a time; new rules may force costly change later |
| Accommodative | Cleans to the legal level and agrees to neighbours' reasonable requests | Good relations; limited extra cost |
| Proactive | Cleans beyond the legal level; shares the test results; helps other factories do the same | High early cost; trust and a stronger name |

> No stance is always best, because cost, size and the matter at hand differ.

Is the proactive stance always best? No. It is the costliest. A small firm cannot be proactive on every matter, and it need not be. The sensible course is to choose the matters where the firm's power or its harm is greatest, and to be proactive there. On other matters, an accommodative stance is enough. The obstructive stance is rarely wise, for it risks all that the other stances protect. An exception is a demand that is unlawful or plainly unfair, which a firm may rightly resist by lawful means.

> Political pressure can be answered by influence, as Chapter 4 described, but the means must stay open and honest.

Political pressure needs a word of its own. A government may ask firms to follow a policy, such as training young workers, before it makes the policy into law. A firm may answer by adapting, or it may try to influence the policy, as Chapter 4 explained. Influence is acceptable when it is open and honest: a letter, a meeting with officials, a statement through a trade group. It is not acceptable when it uses gifts or threats. The ethical test of 5.4 applies here with full force, and the fourth question is the best guide: could the firm describe its approach to the officials in public?

> Pressure also comes from inside the firm, because staff and owners hold views about what is right.

Pressure does not always come from outside. Staff may ask for safer machines, and owners may ask the firm to give to a cause. These requests arise from the internal environment of Chapter 4, and they deserve the same careful answer. A firm that listens to its own people early often avoids a larger conflict later, since staff who see a wrong are the first to see it.

> A short procedure helps a manager to choose the stance in a reasoned way.

We can now state a procedure for answering pressure, in six steps. A procedure is a fixed order of steps that a manager follows each time.

> Step one is to find who is pressing and what they ask.

*Step 1: Find who presses and what they ask.* Name the stakeholder and state the request in one sentence. Do not guess the request; ask.

> Step two is to check the law, and step three is to apply the ethical test.

*Step 2: Check the law.* Find which rule applies, and whether the firm now meets it. *Step 3: Apply the ethical test.* Ask the four questions of 5.4. The law sets the minimum, and the test shows what lies above it.

> Steps four to six are to count the costs, to choose and tell, and to review the result.

*Step 4: Count the costs.* For each stance, list the cost now and the risk later. *Step 5: Choose a stance, act, and tell the stakeholders openly.* *Step 6: Review.* After some weeks, ask whether the pressure has eased and whether the cost was as expected. Chapter 13 will show how such a review fits into control.

> Pressure from society can cause large changes, as when buyers and unions responded to a building collapse near Dhaka in 2013.

Pressure from society and government has changed whole industries. In April 2013 the Rana Plaza building in Savar, near Dhaka, collapsed, and more than 1,100 people died, most of them garment workers. In the weeks that followed, many foreign buyers and trade unions agreed to an accord on fire and building safety in the country's garment factories. The accord is a case of pressure from society leading to new rules that firms had to follow. Factories that had treated safety as someone else's problem now had to inspect their buildings and repair faults.

> The case shows that the cost of being obstructive can be far greater than the cost of acting early.

We cannot know what would have happened if factories had acted earlier. But we can see the pattern that the iron law describes. Where firms did not give safety the attention that their power over workers demanded, the pressure that arrived later was far stronger than any earlier request would have been. The cost of a proactive stance is real, but it is small beside the cost of a collapse.

> You should now be able to name the four stances and place a response on the scale.

You should now be able to say: pressure on a firm may be social, political or legal; a firm may answer in four stances, from obstructive to proactive; and a manager chooses by the six-step procedure and not by habit. Try this on a new case. Neighbours ask a bakery to stop deliveries before six in the morning. The owner agrees at once, and no law requires it. Which stance is this? The answer is accommodative, since the firm meets a reasonable request when asked.

## Worked Case: Month 5 at the Bakery

> The case file for Month 5 records four actions by the owner and a cost figure for the bags.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Morning Loaf Bakery, Month 5. Unsold bread, about 10 loaves a day, is donated at closing time. Compostable bags cost Tk 0.80 more each, and the bakery uses 250 a day, a cost of Tk 200 a day. Labels that did not match the product were corrected. The owner sets a written ethics rule: no claim on a label that is not checked. Loaf price Tk 70; ingredient cost about Tk 24 before the flour rise of Month 4 and about Tk 26.16 after it; 26 trading days a month; output 250 loaves a day.

### Situation

> Four acts are recorded, and the case file gives no reasons for them, so we show how the chapter's tools describe them.

The case file records four acts. The file does not record why the owner chose them. We therefore use the tools of this chapter to describe each act, and to measure what can be measured. We make no claim about the owner's motives.

### Question

> The questions ask which layer, which Davis proposition, and which stance each act shows, and what the acts cost.

Which layer of responsibility and which of Davis's propositions does each act show? Which stance does each show? What do the bags and the donated bread cost in a month? How does the cost compare with the flour rise of Month 4?

### Reasoning

> The first step sorts each act by layer, by proposition and by stance.

First, sort the acts. The donation of unsold bread is a matter of free choice and falls in the discretionary layer. It shows proposition five, help with a social problem outside the firm's usual work. The compostable bags are a sustainability act in the environmental part, and they show proposition four, since the cost is built into the price of production. The correction of the labels is first a matter of law, because labelling rules came in Month 4, and then of ethics, since the labels did not match the product. The written rule shows proposition two, that the firm should be open, and it is a code of ethics. As for stance, the donation and the bags go beyond the law, so they are accommodative or proactive. The label correction meets a legal duty and, with the rule, goes beyond it.

> The second step measures the donation and the bags with the standard layout.

Second, measure the costs.
*Given.* About 10 loaves a day are donated. After the flour rise of Month 4, the ingredient cost of a loaf is about Tk 26.16. The bags cost Tk 200 a day. The bakery trades 26 days a month.
*Find.* The monthly ingredient value of the donated bread, the monthly cost of the bags, and the monthly total.
*Formula.* Value of donated bread per month = loaves per day × ingredient cost per loaf × trading days. Bag cost per month = bag cost per day × trading days.
*Steps.* Donated bread: 10 × Tk 26.16 × 26 = Tk 261.60 × 26 = Tk 6,801.60, or about Tk 6,802. Bags: Tk 200 × 26 = Tk 5,200. Total: Tk 6,802 + Tk 5,200 = Tk 12,002.
*Answer.* The donation represents about Tk 6,802 of ingredients a month, and the bags cost Tk 5,200 a month. The two together come to about Tk 12,002 a month.
*Check.* Tk 12,002 ÷ 26 trading days is about Tk 462 a day. The parts are Tk 261.60 and Tk 200, which sum to Tk 461.60. The answers agree.

> The third step puts the total beside the flour rise of Month 4, using the figure carried forward from Chapter 4.

Third, compare. In Chapter 4 the flour rise cost the bakery Tk 14,040 a month. The bags alone cost Tk 5,200, which is 5,200 ÷ 14,040 = 0.370, or about 37 per cent of that amount. The bags and the donated bread together are Tk 12,002, which is 12,002 ÷ 14,040 = 0.855, or about 85 per cent. These figures show that the acts of Month 5 are not small beside the pressures of Month 4. The donated bread is a cost only in the sense that it is bread that could not be sold, so the sum is an upper bound on what is given up, not an amount paid.

### Conclusion

> The four acts show different layers, propositions and stances, and they carry a cost that the owner has accepted.

The four acts of Month 5 touch four different parts of the model of this chapter. They go beyond the law in three cases. They carry a monthly cost that is a large share of the flour rise in the same period. The owner has accepted the cost, so far as the case file shows. The chapter offers a way to describe such choices and to examine them, whether or not the owner used it.

### Check

> A check confirms that no act has been left unsorted and that the figures match the case file.

Count the acts: donation, bags, label correction and written rule. All four are sorted. The figures used are those of the case file: Tk 0.80 a bag, 250 bags, Tk 200 a day and about 10 loaves a day. The ingredient cost of Tk 26.16 is derived from Chapter 4, as the case note states.

## Summary

> The chapter's ideas, in order.

Social responsibility is the duty to act for society's good as well as for profit. It has four layers: economic, legal, ethical and discretionary. Arguments for it rest on goodwill, power and the cost of harm. Arguments against rest on duty to owners, cost and answerability. Davis's model links responsibility to social power, adds the iron law, and gives five propositions. Sustainability means meeting today's needs without harming the future, and has an economic, a social and an environmental part. Business ethics is the standard of right and wrong in business; it differs from law and from social responsibility, and a four-question test helps a manager to apply it. A firm answers pressure in four stances, obstructive, defensive, accommodative and proactive, and a six-step procedure helps it to choose.

## Think it through

## Where people go wrong

> Six common errors are stated and corrected.

> Giving is only the top layer of responsibility.
*Social responsibility means giving money to charity.* Giving is only the top layer. The first duties are to do the core work well, to obey the law and to avoid harm.

> The law sets the minimum, and ethics asks for more.
*If it is legal, it is ethical.* The law sets the minimum. Mr Karim's late work is legal and may still be wrong.

> Sustainability has three parts, not one.
*Sustainability means only protecting nature.* It has three parts, and the economic and social parts matter as much as the environmental one.

> An unpaid cost falls on other people.
*A cost that a firm does not pay does not exist.* It falls on others, and in time it may return to the firm.

> A four-question test brings ethical reasoning into the open.
*Ethics is a matter of opinion, so no test is possible.* People differ on some questions, but they agree on many, and a four-question test brings the reasoning into the open.

> The best stance depends on power, harm and cost.
*The proactive stance is always best.* It is the most costly. A firm should be proactive where its power and its harm are greatest and accommodative elsewhere.

## In history

> The Tylenol recall of 1982 is often cited as an example of a firm acting for the public before it was forced to.

In late September 1982, seven people in the Chicago area died after taking capsules of Tylenol, a painkiller made by a subsidiary of Johnson & Johnson. Investigators found that someone had put cyanide in capsules after they left the factory. The company recalled about 31 million bottles across the United States, and later sold the product in new sealed packages. The recall was reported to have cost about 100 million United States dollars.

> The firm told the public, took the loss, and later new rules for safe packaging followed.

Commentators have often praised the firm for putting public safety ahead of short-term profit, for speaking openly to the public and for working with officials. New federal rules for tamper-resistant packaging followed. The case shows several ideas of this chapter: a firm acting before the law required it, and openness of the kind that Davis's second proposition describes. The case also has a limit: the poisoner was never found, and the firm was not at fault for the crime. Its conduct is judged on how it answered.
