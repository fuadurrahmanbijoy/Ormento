## Who Do They Follow?

> Two bakers hear the same instruction, and only one acts on it.
:case Case file, illustrative. Morning Loaf Bakery, Month 12. Until now the owner has decided most questions of work method alone. In Month 12 the owner begins to ask the experienced bakers for their views before deciding. With the new helper the owner stays directive and gives clear orders. The owner also meets the head baker each week to coach.

> Position alone does not explain why people act on an instruction.
Imagine that the owner tells two people, on the same morning, to change the order in which the dough is mixed. The new helper makes the change at once and asks whether it is right. The senior baker, who knows the oven well, frowns, says that the old order gave a better crust, and goes on as before. The owner holds the same position in both cases. The owner has the same authority, the same wage list and the same right to give orders. Yet the two people respond in different ways.

> A right to give orders does not guarantee that people follow.
This small scene raises a question that Chapter 10 could not answer. Authority is the right to give orders, and the owner has it. But a right to give orders does not guarantee that people will follow them, still less that they will follow willingly. Something else decides whether a person's words move others. The helper may follow because the helper is new and wants guidance. The baker may resist because the baker has skill that the owner lacks. To understand the difference, we must study how people come to follow another person.

> This chapter asks what makes people follow, and how a manager can use it well.
The chapter proceeds in six steps. Section 12.1 defines leadership and separates it from management. Section 12.2 examines the five sources of power. Section 12.3 compares the early approaches: traits, and then styles of behaviour. Section 12.4 shows why the best style depends on the situation. Section 12.5 studies emotional intelligence, and section 12.6 describes three newer kinds of leadership. A worked case then follows the owner through Month 12.

## 12.1 What Leadership Is; Leader and Manager

@! Go slowly here. New idea, familiar example first.
> A captain without any formal right can still lead the players.
Think of a school football team. The coach decides who plays, and the teachers decide who may leave early for practice. The captain has none of these rights. The captain cannot choose the team, pay anyone or send anyone home. Yet when the team trails by two goals, the players look to the captain. They follow the captain's calm voice and the captain's way of passing the ball. Nobody ordered them to do so. The captain leads, and the coach, who holds the position, may or may not.

> Influence is the central word in the definition of leadership.
@ Influence | Changing what another person thinks, feels or does, without using force.
The captain's power rests on **influence**. Influence means changing what another person thinks, feels or does, without using force. A player who copies the captain's way of defending has been influenced. The word is the key to the definition that follows.

> Leadership has three parts: a leader, followers and a goal.
@ Leadership | Influencing people to work toward a goal.
**Leadership** means influencing people toward a goal. A **leader** is a person who does so. In the bakery, a baker who persuades the others to start the morning batch early, so that the festival order is ready, is leading. Every case of leadership has three parts: a leader, followers and a goal. Without followers, nobody is led. Without a goal, people may follow a popular person, but they are not led toward anything.

> A manager holds a position, while a leader needs only influence.
@ Manager | A person responsible for the work of others, by virtue of a position.
@ Informal leader | A member who influences a group without holding any official post.
The next question is how a leader differs from a **manager**, a word met in Chapter 1: a person responsible for the work of others. The two words overlap, but they are not the same. A manager holds a position, and the position gives the right to direct resources and people. A leader need not hold any position. The captain in our example leads without one. In an organisation, the same is true of an **informal leader**, which means a member who has influence over a group without holding any official post, as met in Chapter 11, section 11.5.

> Leading is one function of management, and leadership reaches beyond management.
Chapter 2 listed four functions of management, and leading is one of them. A manager therefore must lead, as well as plan, organise and control. The reverse does not hold. A person can lead without managing anything. We may then state the relation in one sentence: leading is part of a manager's work, and leadership is wider than management. The table below sets the two side by side. Read each row from left to right: the first column names a feature, and the other two describe a typical manager and a typical leader.

:table Manager and leader compared
| Feature | Manager | Leader |
| Source of the right to direct | Position in the organisation | Influence over followers |
| Main concern | Order: plans, structure, control | Direction: where people are going and why |
| Main tools | Authority, rules, rewards | Example, trust, ideas |
| Followers | Subordinates who must obey | Followers who choose to follow |
| Typical risk | Compliance without commitment | Enthusiasm without order |

> Obedience without care and care without order both give weak results.
The last row of the table needs care. A manager who cannot lead gets people to obey but not to care, so work is done at the minimum level. A leader who cannot manage may inspire people but fail to give them plans, structure or checks, so effort scatters. The strongest results come when one person does both, and a small business such as the bakery cannot afford to separate them. The owner is a manager and a leader at once.

> The table shows differences of emphasis, not two separate kinds of person.
There are exceptions to the table. Some writers, such as the American scholar John Kotter in 1990, stress the difference sharply. Others reply that in real work the two cannot be pulled apart.^1 Treat the table as a guide to emphasis and not as a division between two kinds of person.

^1: The distinction is discussed in several textbooks and articles. Verify the exact source and date of Kotter's argument before this note is finalised.

> The difference between manager and leader looks different from each seat.
Now view the same point from three seats. To the worker, a manager is the person who assigns tasks and checks them, and a leader is the person who makes the tasks seem worth doing. To the owner, a manager keeps the shop in order, and a leader makes the shop grow. To the customer, none of this is visible, but the result is: a counter staff who care about the customer, and a shop that runs on time.

## 12.2 Power and Its Bases

Consider the head baker of the bakery. Since Month 10 the head baker may buy ingredients up to Tk 5,000 an order, and the owner stays accountable. That is a right written into the job. But when the head baker asks the two bakers to change the order of work, the response depends on much more than the right to buy flour. It depends on whether they respect the head baker's skill, whether they like the head baker, and whether they fear or want what the head baker can give. These sources of influence have a name.

In daily life, *power* often means physical strength, or the force of an engine, or the rule of a government. In management, **power** means the ability to make others act. A baker who can persuade a colleague to stay late has power over that colleague. The word does not say whether the power is good or bad, or whether the organisation has granted it.

Chapter 10, section 10.1, gave a short note on this point, and we can now complete it. Authority is a right given by the organisation. Power is an ability, and it may come from many sources. The two can be found apart. A new manager may have full authority and little power, if the staff ignore the orders. An informal leader may have much power and no authority. The strongest position is to hold both, with authority backing power and power backing authority.

In 1959 two American psychologists, John French and Bertram Raven, set out five bases of power, which are the five sources from which a person's ability to influence others comes. Their list remains the most common one in textbooks.^2 We take each base in turn, with a bakery example.

^2: Raven added a sixth base, informational power, in later work. This book follows the original five. Verify the date and details of the original paper.

**Legitimate power** means influence that comes from holding a position that others accept as having the right to direct. The head baker's right to order ingredients up to Tk 5,000 is legitimate power. It depends on the staff accepting that the role carries that right. It ends when the position ends, and it does not reach beyond the limits of the role.

**Reward power** means influence that comes from the ability to give things that others value. The owner's monthly team bonus of Tk 1,000 for waste of 5% or less is reward power. It also includes shifts, praise and training. Chapter 11 showed that a reward works only if it meets a need that is open. A reward that people do not want gives no power, and a reward that the giver cannot deliver gives none either.

**Coercive power** means influence that comes from the ability to punish or to threaten loss. Dismissal, fines and the least liked shifts are all forms of it. Coercive power is real, and it can work for a short time. But it brings fear. People who fear punishment may hide errors, avoid responsibility and leave when they can. Chapter 11 described such a manager as holding Theory X.

**Expert power** means influence that comes from special knowledge or skill that others need. A baker who knows how to repair the mixer has expert power, whatever the baker's position. It persists as long as the skill is needed and others lack it. It fades if the knowledge becomes old or if others learn it.

**Referent power** means influence that comes from the admiration and liking that others feel for a person, and their wish to be like that person. A baker whom the whole team trusts and copies has referent power. It cannot be ordered or bought, and it takes time to build. It can be lost quickly through one act of unfairness.

The table that follows sets the five side by side. Read each row from left to right: the first column names the base, and the others give its source, a bakery example and its main risk.

:table The five bases of power
| Base | Source | Bakery example | Main risk |
| Legitimate | A position others accept | The head baker's order limit of Tk 5,000 | Ends outside the role |
| Reward | Ability to give valued things | The Tk 1,000 team bonus | Fails if the reward is not wanted |
| Coercive | Ability to punish | Threat of dismissal | Fear, hidden errors, departures |
| Expert | Special knowledge or skill | The baker who repairs the mixer | Fades as others learn |
| Referent | Admiration and liking | The baker the team copies | Lost by one unfair act |

Writers often divide the five into two groups. Legitimate, reward and coercive power belong to the position, because the organisation grants them and can take them back. Expert and referent power belong to the person, because they stay with the individual who holds them. A manager moved to another post keeps the second group and loses the first. This division explains why some leaders are followed after they leave the post, and some managers are forgotten the day they retire.

The bases also lead to different responses from followers. Where power rests on expert or referent bases, followers tend to show **commitment**, which means willing effort that goes beyond the minimum. Where it rests on legitimate or reward power, they tend to show **compliance**, which means doing what is asked, but no more. Where it rests on coercive power, compliance is the best result, and resistance is common. A leader who wants commitment should therefore build personal power, and not rely on position alone.

No leader uses one base only. The owner has legitimate power as the owner, reward power through pay and bonus, coercive power through the right to dismiss, expert power in baking, and, with luck, referent power. The skill lies in the balance. A leader who relies on coercion alone makes a weak team. One who relies on liking alone cannot say no. Return now to the scene at the start of the chapter. The new helper follows the owner at once, because the helper has little skill and values guidance, so the owner's expert and legitimate power are strong. The senior baker has expert power of the baker's own, and for this reason the owner does better to ask than to tell.

Power must also serve the goal and not the private gain of the leader. A manager who uses reward power to favour friends, or coercive power to silence honest criticism, harms the team and the organisation. Chapter 5 studied the ethical tests that apply, and Chapter 13 shows how control systems can guard against misuse.

*Pause and check 2.* Since Month 10 the part-time bookkeeper may require receipts from all staff. Which base of power does this right rest on, and why would it be weak if the owner withdrew support?

*Before moving on.* Power is the ability to make others act. French and Raven named five bases: legitimate, reward, coercive, expert and referent. The first three belong to the position and the last two to the person.

## 12.3 Trait and Behavioural Approaches; Styles of Leading

If you could list the qualities of every great leader, could you hire a leader by checking the list? Early researchers believed so. Their search for the list is the oldest approach to leadership, and its failure taught more than its success.

The **trait approach** means the view that leaders are born with special qualities that other people lack. A *trait* is a lasting personal quality, such as height, energy, intelligence or confidence. From the early years of the twentieth century, researchers measured the traits of people who led and compared them with those who did not.^3 Some traits appeared often: intelligence, drive, self-confidence and honesty among them.

^3: Verify the main studies and dates summarised by later reviews of trait research before this note is finalised.

But the approach met two difficulties. First, no list of traits worked in every setting. A person who led well in a sports club might fail in a hospital, and the traits did not predict which. Second, traits are hard to change. If leaders are born, then training can do little, and experience contradicts this view. Today managers use traits as one piece of information, for example when choosing among candidates, and they do not treat any list as a guarantee.

Researchers then turned from what leaders *are* to what leaders *do*. This is the behavioural approach. It studies a leader's **leadership style**, which means the usual pattern of behaviour that a leader shows toward followers. The best-known early study was made in the United States in the late 1930s by Kurt Lewin with two colleagues, Ronald Lippitt and Ralph White. The details are given at the end of the chapter.

The study named three styles. An **autocratic leader** is one who decides alone and tells the group what to do. A **democratic leader** is one who shares decisions with the group, invites discussion and then guides the result. A **laissez-faire leader** is one who leaves the group to decide and act with little guidance. *Laissez-faire* is a French phrase, now used in English for leaving things alone. The table below compares the three. Read each row from left to right.

:table Three styles of leading
| Feature | Autocratic | Democratic | Laissez-faire |
| Who decides | The leader alone | The leader with the group | The group alone |
| Direction of talk | Mostly from leader to group | In both directions | Little from the leader |
| Speed of decision | Fast | Slower | Varies widely |
| Good when | Time is short; followers are new | Followers know the work and want a voice | Followers are skilled and self-directed |
| Main risk | Resentment; dependence on the leader | Delay; loss of direction | Drift; no one answers for results |

The styles link to the assumptions of Chapter 11. An autocratic leader often holds Theory X, and a democratic leader holds Theory Y. A laissez-faire leader assumes a group that is already skilled and self-directed, and so it fits only a narrow set of cases. None of the three is good or bad in itself. A bakery fire demands an autocratic style, because there is no time to discuss. A discussion of a new product demands a democratic style, because the staff know the customers. A team of very experienced bakers may do well with a light touch.

A second line of research, begun at Ohio State University and the University of Michigan in the 1940s and 1950s, looked at the aim of the leader's actions. It found two themes that appear again and again.^4 The first is concern for task: setting goals, planning schedules and checking results. The second is concern for people: showing trust, respect and warmth. A leader may show much or little of each.

^4: Verify the dates, the names of the main studies and the terms each used before this note is finalised.

In 1964 two American writers, Robert Blake and Jane Mouton, drew the two themes as a grid.^5 Concern for people is shown upward, and concern for task is shown to the right, each on a scale from 1 (low) to 9 (high). The figure shows the five main positions on the grid. A manager at the lower left shows little concern for either and does the minimum. A manager at the lower right cares for output only, and treats people as tools. A manager at the upper left cares for people but neglects results. A manager in the middle balances the two at a modest level. A manager at the upper right is a *team* leader, who shows high concern for both.

^5: Verify the date and the terms Blake and Mouton used for each position.

:fig The two concerns of a leader: five positions on a grid of concern for people and concern for task | fig_12_1_grid.svg

Blake and Mouton held that the team position is the best in all cases. Many writers disagree. They point out that a firm facing an emergency may need a high concern for task and little time for discussion, and that a skilled and willing team may need little push from its leader. The behavioural approach, like the trait approach, therefore leaves a question open: what works depends on whom the leader leads and in what setting. That is the starting point for the next section.

The behavioural studies have a further limit. Lewin's groups were boys' clubs, and findings from them may not carry over to adults at work. Later research gave mixed results on whether democratic leaders always get better output. What most studies suggest is a modest point: people usually prefer to be consulted on matters that affect them, and leaders who never consult pay a price in loyalty.

*Before moving on.* The trait approach looks at what leaders are and finds no list that always works. The behavioural approach looks at what leaders do. Three styles are autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire, and two concerns, for task and for people, describe a leader's aims.

## 12.4 Situational Approaches

In 1958 two American scholars, Robert Tannenbaum and Warren Schmidt, published an article titled "How to Choose a Leadership Pattern" in the *Harvard Business Review*.^6 Managers of the time asked a question that seemed sensible: should I be firm, or should I be democratic? The two writers answered that the question is wrongly put. A manager should not choose a style once for all, but should ask which style suits this task, these people and this moment.

^6: Verify the title, journal, year and authors of this article before this note is finalised.

The **situational approach** means the view that the best way to lead depends on the situation, so that no single style is best in every case. It is the form that the contingency approach of Chapter 3, section 3.4, takes in the study of leadership. We begin with the Tannenbaum–Schmidt model, which is the clearest, and then add three others.

### The leadership continuum

Tannenbaum and Schmidt did not describe two or three separate styles. They drew a line. The **leadership continuum** means the range of leader behaviour, from the boss-centred end, where the leader decides, to the team-centred end, where the group decides within limits. *Continuum* means a line along which there is no sharp break between one point and the next. The figure shows seven points on the line.

:fig The leadership continuum: seven points from boss-centred to team-centred leading | fig_12_2_continuum.svg

We describe the points from left to right, using the bakery's choice of a new mixing order as the example. At the first point, the leader decides and announces: "From Monday the dough is mixed in this order." At the second, the leader decides but explains the reasons, to win acceptance. At the third, the leader presents the decision and invites questions before it is final. These three points keep the decision in the leader's hands.

At the fourth point, the leader presents a tentative decision and is ready to change it after hearing the group. At the fifth, the leader presents the problem, asks the group for suggestions, and then decides. The leader has moved from announcing to asking, but the leader still decides. This is what many writers call a *consultative* style, which means asking for the views of others before deciding.

At the sixth point, the leader states the limits, for example the cost and the date, and asks the group to decide within them. At the seventh, the group acts freely within limits that the leader's own superior has set. The decision now rests with the group. The figure shows that the amount of freedom for the leader falls from left to right, and the amount of freedom for the group rises.

The two writers then asked what should decide the point on the line. They named three groups of forces. The first lies in the **leader**: the leader's beliefs about people, confidence in the group, own habits, and tolerance of risk. A leader who holds Theory X will drift to the left, and one who holds Theory Y to the right. The second lies in the **followers**: their wish for independence, readiness to take responsibility, interest in the problem, understanding of the goal, knowledge and experience, and their past expectations of how the leader behaves. The third lies in the **situation**: the size and form of the organisation, the skill of the group in working together, the kind of problem, and the time available.

The lesson is that the successful leader is not the one who is always firm or always gentle. It is the one who reads the forces correctly and acts on them, and who can move to the left when time is short and to the right when the group is ready. For the bakery, this explains the owner's choices in Month 12. With the new helper, who knows little, the owner keeps to the left of the line. With experienced bakers who know the oven well, the owner moves to the fifth point.

### Three other situational views

Other writers have built situational theories on different foundations. We give three, each in brief.

The American psychologist Fred Fiedler, in 1967, proposed the *contingency model of leadership*.^7 He held that a leader's style is hard to change, and that it is better to fit the leader to the situation than to ask the leader to change. He described the situation by three features: the quality of the leader's relations with followers, how clearly the task is set out, and how much formal power the leader holds. A task-oriented leader, he suggested, does best where the situation is either very favourable or very unfavourable, and a relationship-oriented leader does best in between.

^7: Verify the date, the name of the model and the details of its findings before this note is finalised.

In 1969 Paul Hersey and Kenneth Blanchard proposed the *situational leadership model*.^8 It places the fit between style and the *readiness* of followers, which means their combined ability and willingness to do a task. When readiness is low, the leader should tell. As readiness rises, the leader should sell, then take part, and finally delegate. The figure shows the four steps. A new helper who cannot yet bake needs *telling*. A helper who has learned the basics but lacks confidence needs *selling*, which means explaining and persuading. An experienced baker who is able but unsure may need *participating*, which means sharing decisions. A head baker who is able and willing may be given *delegating*, which means handing over decisions and duty.

^8: Verify the date and names used by the authors for the four styles before this note is finalised.

:fig Four steps of readiness and the style that fits each | fig_12_3_readiness.svg

The third view, set out by the American scholar Robert House in 1971, is called *path-goal theory*.^9 It says that the leader's task is to help followers see the path to their goals and to clear obstacles from it. It links directly to the expectancy theory of Chapter 11: if followers believe that effort leads to reward, they work harder, and the leader's job is to make that belief true. House named four styles, which are directive, supportive, participative and achievement-oriented, and the leader picks among them according to the task and the followers.

^9: Verify the date, the author and the names of the four styles before this note is finalised.

The table that follows compares the four situational views. Read each row from left to right: the first column names the writers, and the others give the main idea, what the leader adjusts and what the situation is judged by.

:table Four situational views of leading
| Writers | Main idea | The leader adjusts | The situation is judged by |
| Tannenbaum and Schmidt | A continuum of styles | Place on the line | Forces in leader, followers and situation |
| Fiedler | Fit the leader to the situation | Little: the situation changes | Relations, task structure, position power |
| Hersey and Blanchard | Style follows readiness | Telling to delegating | Ability and willingness of followers |
| House | Show the path to goals | Four styles of support | Task and followers' needs |

The views differ on how far a leader can change style. Tannenbaum and Schmidt, and Hersey and Blanchard, expect a skilled leader to move along the line. Fiedler doubts that a person can change style easily. In daily life, most managers can adjust somewhat, but not without limit, and a manager who is naturally warm will find strict orders hard to give.

Situational leading also has a risk that connects to Chapter 11. If the owner is directive with one person and consultative with another, the two may compare their treatment, as the equity theory predicts. If the owner cannot explain why the styles differ, the difference may look like favouritism. A sound leader therefore says why: "You will have a say in this, because you know the oven. For this task, I need you to follow the steps exactly while you are learning."

The common message of the four views is plain. There is no single best style. Before acting, the leader should ask three questions. What is the task, and how urgent? Who are the followers, and how ready are they? What is the setting, and what does it allow? The answers decide the point on the continuum, and they may change next month.

*Before moving on.* The situational approach holds that the best style depends on the situation. The leadership continuum runs from boss-centred to team-centred. Fiedler, Hersey and Blanchard, and House offer three other situational views.

## 12.5 Emotional Intelligence

Two managers have equal skill. In a busy week, one notices that the head baker has grown silent and short with the staff, and asks quietly what is wrong. The other sees only that the work is behind, and gives more orders. After a month, the first manager has kept the head baker, and the second has lost one. The difference lies in a quality that the earlier approaches did not name.

**Emotional intelligence** means the ability to read and manage feelings, both one's own and those of other people. Two American psychologists, Peter Salovey and John Mayer, used the term in 1990, and the writer Daniel Goleman made it widely known in 1995.^10 Goleman described five parts. We state each in plain words, with a bakery example.

^10: Verify the names, dates and the exact list of components in the sources before this note is finalised.

*Self-awareness* means knowing one's own feelings and how they affect one's actions. An owner who knows that anger makes the owner unfair will wait before speaking. *Self-regulation* means controlling one's own reactions, and acting in a calm and fair way. *Motivation*, in this list, means an inner drive to reach goals for their own sake and not only for pay, as in the needs-goal model of Chapter 11. **Empathy** means understanding how another person feels, and showing that you do. *Social skill* means handling relations with others, so that people cooperate and trust is built.

These five parts link directly to leadership. The needs in Chapter 11 are felt, and a leader who cannot read feelings will not see an open need. Referent power, from section 12.2, rests on liking and trust, which a leader earns through empathy and fairness. Even the choice of a point on the continuum rests on reading the followers' readiness correctly, which takes empathy as well as facts.

Seen from the follower's seat, emotional intelligence feels like being noticed and treated with respect, even when a hard decision must be made. Seen from the owner's seat, it appears as lower loss of staff and fewer quarrels. The customer sees it only as a shop in which people are at ease.

There are limits. Researchers still dispute how to measure emotional intelligence, and the claims for it are sometimes larger than the evidence. Skill in feelings cannot replace skill in the work itself. A person who reads feelings well may also use the skill to manipulate others, so it must be joined to honesty, a point that Chapter 5 supports. Finally, people in different places show feelings in different ways, and a leader must learn the customs of those who are led.

*Before moving on.* Emotional intelligence is the ability to read and manage feelings, one's own and others'. Its five parts are self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skill. It helps a leader see needs and build trust, but it does not replace skill, and it must be used honestly.

## 12.6 Transformational, Coaching and Super Leadership

Suppose one owner asks each morning only whether the batch is finished. Another owner asks the staff what would make this the best bakery in the street, and then helps them move toward it. Both get loaves baked. But the effect on the people, and on the shop in three years, will differ. The second owner is closer to the newer kinds of leadership described in this section.

### Transformational leadership

The American writer James MacGregor Burns, in 1978, separated two kinds of leader.^11 A **transactional leader** means one who gets work done by an exchange: clear tasks and fair rewards in return for effort. The owner who sets targets and pays the team bonus for reaching them is acting in this way. The exchange is useful, and every organisation needs some of it, but it reaches only as far as the rewards reach.

^11: Verify the date and wording of Burns' distinction, and Bass' later extension of it, before this note is finalised.

A **transformational leader** means one who inspires followers to look beyond their own interest toward a shared purpose, and to change the way they work. The writer Bernard Bass developed the idea in 1985. Four features mark such a leader. The leader holds a clear picture of a better future and shares it. The leader inspires pride and hope. The leader challenges old habits of thought and invites new ideas, as in the creative process of Chapter 11. And the leader gives each person individual attention.

The link to Chapter 11 is close. A transactional leader works mainly at the lower levels of need, with pay and safety. A transformational leader works at the upper levels, with belonging, esteem and growth. The two kinds do not compete. Most leaders need both, since a vision cannot be reached unless the daily tasks are done and paid for.

The danger is plain. A leader who inspires followers can lead them wrongly as well as rightly. Charm without honesty has done harm in business and in politics. A transformational leader must therefore be judged by the aims set, as well as by the following won. Chapter 5 gave the tests that apply.

### Coaching

**Coaching** means developing a person's skill and confidence through regular one-to-one guidance. The aim is for the person to be able to do more, and not for the coach to do the task. A coach follows four steps. First, agree a goal with the person. Second, watch the person at work. Third, give specific feedback, saying what went well and what to change. Fourth, agree the next step and meet again.

The skill of a coach lies partly in asking. A coach who asks "What do you think went wrong with the order?" helps a person to think, while a coach who says "You did it wrong" ends the thinking. Coaching serves needs at the esteem and growth levels of Chapter 11, since it shows that the leader values the person's future. It also makes delegation, from Chapter 10, safer. A manager who is afraid to delegate may find that coaching builds the skill that makes it possible.

In the bakery, the owner coaches the head baker each week. A session might look at how a purchase was made within the Tk 5,000 limit, or how the morning briefing was run. Coaching takes time, and it needs a person who wants to learn. Where the person does not want to learn, coaching becomes nagging.

### Super leadership

**Super leadership** means leading others so that they learn to lead themselves. Two American writers, Charles Manz and Henry Sims, set out the idea in 1989.^12 The word *super* does not refer to a leader of great power. It refers to a leader who makes the followers strong enough that they need less leading.

^12: Verify the date, the authors and the steps they proposed before this note is finalised.

The idea rests on *self-leadership*, which means a person's ability to set goals, check progress and reward oneself without being told. A super leader shows self-leadership by example, guides followers to set their own goals, praises independent action, and builds the team's habit of managing its own work. It links to Theory Y of Chapter 11, and to the delegating step in the readiness model.

Super leadership has limits. It needs followers who are skilled and willing, so it does not fit the new helper at the start. A manager must also accept less control, and Chapter 10 listed the fears that make this hard. And a team without a leader's guidance may drift if goals are unclear. The table compares the three kinds of leadership.

:table Three newer kinds of leading
| Kind | Main aim | The leader's method | Fits best |
| Transformational | Change and a shared purpose | Vision, inspiration, challenge, attention | Times of change; followers ready for more |
| Coaching | Growth of one person | Goals, watching, feedback, next step | A person who wants to learn |
| Super leadership | Self-led followers | Example; guiding goals; praising independence | Skilled and willing followers |

*Before moving on.* A transactional leader exchanges rewards for effort, while a transformational leader inspires a shared purpose. Coaching develops one person at a time. Super leadership helps followers lead themselves.

That is the fifth point, the consultative style. With the new helper, the owner decides and gives clear orders. That is the first or second point, depending on whether reasons are given.

Reasoning, step 2. We name the bases of power from section 12.2. The owner holds legitimate power as the owner, reward power through the Tk 1,000 team bonus, and coercive power through the right to dismiss. The owner also has expert power in baking, which is strong with the helper and weaker with the experienced bakers, who know the oven well. The head baker holds legitimate power up to Tk 5,000 an order, and expert power as the most skilled baker. Asking the bakers first respects their expert power.

Reasoning, step 3. We test the choice against the forces of section 12.4. The experienced bakers are able and willing, so by the readiness model they suit the participating style. The helper has little skill, so the helper suits telling. The head baker, who is both able and willing, may be given more freedom, and the weekly coaching builds the skill that makes delegation safe.

## Summary

Leadership is influencing people toward a goal. A manager holds a position and must lead, but a leader needs only influence. Power is the ability to make others act, and it rests on five bases: legitimate, reward, coercive, expert and referent. The trait approach found no list of qualities that works in every case. The behavioural approach named three styles, autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire, and two concerns, for task and for people. The situational approach holds that the best style depends on the task, the followers and the setting, and the leadership continuum shows a range from boss-centred to team-centred. Emotional intelligence helps a leader read feelings. Transformational leadership inspires a shared purpose, coaching develops one person at a time, and super leadership helps followers lead themselves.

:box IN SHORT
No single style of leading is best. A leader reads the task, the followers and the setting, uses the bases of power with care, and moves along the continuum as people grow.

## Where People Go Wrong

*"A leader is the same as a manager."* A manager holds a position. A leader needs only influence, and may hold no position at all.

*"Power is always bad."* Power is an ability. It is used well or badly, depending on the aims and the means.

*"Leaders are born."* The trait approach found no list of qualities that works in every setting, and skills such as coaching and listening can be learned.

*"Democratic leading is always best."* Short time, new followers or a crisis may call for a more directive style, and very skilled followers may need only light guidance.

*"Changing style means having no principles."* A sound leader keeps the same values and changes the method to suit the people and the task.

## In History

In the late 1930s Kurt Lewin, a German-born psychologist working in the United States, studied boys' clubs with two colleagues, Ronald Lippitt and Ralph White. Adult leaders were trained to act in turn in an autocratic, a democratic or a laissez-faire way toward groups of boys, while observers recorded what happened. The reports said that the autocratic groups worked hard while the leader was present, but showed more hostility, and work fell when the leader left the room. The democratic groups worked a little less in some respects, but with greater originality, and work continued when the leader was absent. The laissez-faire groups did the least. The study was small and the subjects were children, so it cannot settle how adults should be led. It did show that style affects both output and the feelings of the group, which opened the study of leadership by behaviour.

## Sources

French and Raven, "The Bases of Social Power" (1959), named the five bases of power. Tannenbaum and Schmidt, "How to Choose a Leadership Pattern" (*Harvard Business Review*, 1958), set out the continuum. Lewin, Lippitt and White (1939) reported the early style experiments. Blake and Mouton (1964) drew the managerial grid. Fiedler (1967), Hersey and Blanchard (1969) and House (1971) developed the situational views. Burns (1978) and Bass (1985) described transformational leadership. Goleman (1995) made emotional intelligence widely known. Manz and Sims (1989) described super leadership.
