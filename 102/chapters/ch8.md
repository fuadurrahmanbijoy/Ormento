:desc This chapter explains how managers choose between alternatives. It describes the parts of a decision, the three conditions under which decisions are made, and the steps of a rational process. ## Two Stalls, Two Results

> A puzzle: the careful trader loses and the careless trader gains.

Imagine two traders, Karim and Salma, who sell goods from stalls in the same market. Each has Tk 10,000 to spend on goods for the coming month. Each must choose between buying umbrellas and buying sun hats. The monsoon is near, and rain is likely, but no one can promise it.

> Karim gathers facts and chooses umbrellas; Salma checks nothing and chooses sun hats.

Karim studies the last three rainy seasons. He asks two suppliers for prices and estimates how many umbrellas he can sell in a wet month. He compares this plan with the sun hats and decides on umbrellas, for good reasons. Salma checks no figures. She sees a pile of bright sun hats at a low price and buys them.

> The month is dry, so Karim loses and Salma gains.

The month is dry. Rain falls on only two days. Karim sells few umbrellas and ends the month with a loss. Salma sells nearly all her sun hats and earns a profit. Karim made a careful choice and received a poor result. Salma made a careless choice and received a good result.

> The question: who decided better, the one with the better method or the one with the better result?

Now ask a plain question: which trader decided better? Some readers answer "Salma", because her result was better. Others answer "Karim", because his method was better. Both answers seem reasonable, and the difference between them is the subject of this chapter. To settle the matter we must separate three things: the choice, the method of choosing, and the result. We also need words to describe what Karim knew when he chose. He knew a great deal, but he did not know the weather.

> The chapter builds the needed language in five sections and then returns to the traders.

The chapter builds this language step by step. Section 8.1 names the parts of every decision. Section 8.2 shows that decisions are made under three conditions, according to what the chooser knows. Section 8.3 states the seven steps of a careful, logical method. Section 8.4 teaches three tools for comparing chancy alternatives: probability, expected value and the decision tree. Section 8.5 asks how managers really decide, alone and in groups. At the end, we return to Karim and Salma.

> You will need four earlier ideas, each from an earlier chapter.

*You will need.* This chapter uses four earlier ideas: the resources a manager works with (Chapter I), the four functions of management (Chapter II), planning premises (Chapter VI), and objectives (Chapter VII). Each is recalled briefly where it is used.

## 8.1 The Decision Situation

> Everyone decides; managers decide for many people at once.

Every day you make choices. This morning you chose how to travel to your classes: by bus, by rickshaw, or on foot. You compared cost, time and comfort, perhaps without noticing, and then you chose. In this chapter we study the same activity as managers perform it. A manager spends the money, time and effort of an organisation, and so the choice carries more weight.

> A decision is a choice from two or more possible actions.
@ Decision | A choice made from two or more possible actions.

We begin with exact words. A **decision** means a choice made from two or more possible actions. Your choice of bus, rickshaw or walking is a decision. If only one action were possible, there would be no choice to make, and so no decision.

> Each possible action is an alternative, and doing nothing counts as one.
@ Alternative | One of the possible actions from which a choice is made.

Each possible action is an **alternative**. An alternative means one of the possible actions from which a choice is made. Bus, rickshaw and walking are three alternatives. Doing nothing is also an alternative, and managers often forget this. A shop owner who delays a purchase has chosen to delay, and that choice has results.

> Every management function contains decisions, so no manager works a day without choosing.

Chapter I defined management as getting things done through people and resources. Every function in Chapter II contains decisions. Planning asks which goal to pursue and which route to take. Organising asks who will perform which task. Leading asks how to guide a group. Controlling asks what to correct. Some writers therefore call decision making the central part of a manager's work. We need not accept that claim fully to agree that no manager works for a day without choosing.

> A manager's decision uses the organisation's resources, affects others, and must be explained.

A manager's decision differs from your own in three ways. It commits resources that belong to the organisation, not to the manager. It affects other people: staff, customers, suppliers and owners. And it must often be explained afterwards to those who are affected. These features make the quality of the method important, and not only the quality of the result. The opening puzzle pointed to this idea, and we shall return to it.

> Every decision has four elements, which we now name one by one.

Every decision, large or small, contains four elements. We name them one at a time and use Karim's choice as the example.

> The first element is the person or group who chooses and is accountable.
@ Decision maker | The person or group that chooses and is accountable for the choice.

The first element is the **decision maker**. A decision maker means the person or group that chooses and is accountable for the choice. Chapter I defined accountability as being answerable for results. In the opening puzzle, the decision maker is Karim. In a large firm, it may be a committee.

> The second element is the goal, which gives a standard for judging alternatives.

The second element is the goal. Chapter I defined a goal as a result someone wants to reach. The goal gives the decision maker a standard for judging. Karim wants the highest profit from Tk 10,000. Without a goal, no alternative can be called better than another. Chapter VII showed how a vague goal becomes a measurable objective, and a measurable objective is the best standard of all.

> The third element is the set of alternatives: umbrellas, sun hats, or neither.

The third element is the set of alternatives, which we have defined. Karim's alternatives are umbrellas, sun hats, or buying nothing. A decision can be only as good as the best alternative on the list, so the list needs care. Section 8.3 returns to this point.

> The fourth element is the outcome, and each outcome has a payoff.
@ Outcome | What happens after an alternative is chosen.
@ Payoff | The value of an outcome to the decision maker, usually measured in taka.

The fourth element is the outcome. An **outcome** means what happens after an alternative is chosen. The same alternative can lead to different outcomes, because events outside the decision maker's control also act. If Karim buys umbrellas, the outcome in a wet month is good and the outcome in a dry month is poor. Karim controls his choice. He does not control the weather. A **payoff** means the value of an outcome to the decision maker, usually measured in taka. If the umbrellas bring a loss of Tk 3,000, the payoff of that outcome is −Tk 3,000.

The table below summarises the four elements. Read each row from left to right: it names the element, states its meaning, and shows the element in the traders' case.

> Not every decision needs the same effort: routine choices can be settled in advance.

Not every decision needs the same effort. Some decisions repeat often, such as ordering the usual flour each week. A manager can settle these in advance with a policy or a procedure, as Chapter VI described, so that no new thought is needed each time. Other decisions are new, large, or hard to reverse, such as buying an oven. These deserve the full effort that this chapter describes. Two questions help the manager to judge: how costly would a mistake be, and can the choice be undone? The higher the cost and the harder the reversal, the more care the decision deserves.

> The same decision looks different from the owner's, the employee's and the customer's seat.

The same decision looks different from different seats. From the owner's seat, it is a use of savings. From the employee's seat, it is a change that may affect work and pay. From the customer's seat, it is a price or a service that suits or does not suit. A good decision maker pictures all three seats before choosing.

> Top, middle and first-line managers decide matters of different size.

Decisions also differ by level. Top managers decide matters that shape the whole organisation for years, such as entering a new market. Middle managers decide how to divide a budget among departments. First-line managers decide daily matters, such as who covers for an absent worker. Chapter I described these three levels. The method in this chapter serves all three, but the effort grows with the size of the matter.

> Recap: a decision is a choice, and it has four elements.

*Before moving on.* A decision is a choice among alternatives. Every decision has a decision maker, a goal, alternatives, and outcomes with payoffs. The decision maker controls the choice but not the outside events that shape the outcome.

## 8.2 Conditions: Certainty, Risk and Uncertainty

> What a decision maker knows about outcomes changes how the choice is made.

Return to Karim's umbrellas. Karim knew the price from his supplier. He knew roughly how often it rained in past years. He did not know whether the coming month would be wet. He knew each of these three facts to a different degree, and the degree decides which method of choosing is suitable. This section gives a name to each degree.

> Begin with a journey: the fare is known, the delay has a known chance, the new road is unknown.
@! Go slowly here. Three new terms follow, one idea at a time.

Begin with a journey. Suppose you must travel to a friend's house. If the bus fare is fixed at Tk 20, you know the cost before you leave. If one road is blocked on about one day in five, you know the chance of delay on that road. If you have never travelled on a road, and no one can describe it, you know almost nothing about the delay. These are three different positions, and managers meet all three.

> Certainty means that the outcome of every alternative is known in advance.
@ Certainty | The condition in which the outcome of each alternative is known in advance.

The first position is certainty. **Certainty** means that the decision maker knows exactly what outcome each alternative will bring. Suppose a baker may buy flour from supplier A at Tk 60 per kilogram or from supplier B at Tk 63, with both prices fixed in writing for this week. The cost of each alternative is known before the choice. The decision is easy: if quality is equal, choose the lower cost.

> Risk means that outcomes are known and their chances can be stated.
@ Risk | In daily life, danger. In decisions, a condition in which the outcomes are known and their chances can be stated.

The second position is risk. In daily life, risk means danger. In decision making, **risk** means that the decision maker knows the possible outcomes and can state the chance of each. Imagine a shop owner with ten years of records. The first week of June was wet in seven of those ten years. The owner cannot know whether next June will be wet, but the owner knows the chance. We measure such a chance with a number called a probability, which section 8.4 teaches.

> Uncertainty means that the chances, and perhaps the outcomes, are not known.
@ Uncertainty | The condition in which the chances of outcomes cannot be stated, and some outcomes may be unknown.

The third position is uncertainty. **Uncertainty** means that the decision maker cannot state the chances of the outcomes, and may not know all the possible outcomes. A firm that launches a new kind of product in a city where it has never sold has no records to guide it. Customers may welcome the product, ignore it, or react in a way that nobody foresaw.

> One question separates the three conditions: how much does the decision maker know?

One question separates the three conditions: how much does the decision maker know about the outcomes? Under certainty, the decision maker knows everything about them. Under risk, the decision maker knows the outcomes and their chances. Under uncertainty, the decision maker knows little or nothing about the chances.

> The condition belongs to the decision maker's knowledge, not to the world.

Notice that the condition belongs to the decision maker's knowledge, and not to the world. The same decision may be a risk for an experienced trader, who has records, and an uncertainty for a newcomer, who has none. Gathering information can therefore move a decision from uncertainty toward risk. This is one reason why Step 3 of the process in section 8.3 matters so much.

The figure below shows the three conditions. In each picture the square is the choice. Each line leads to a possible outcome, and the label beside the outcome says what is known about its chance.

The table below compares the three conditions. Read across each row: it states what the decision maker knows, gives one example, and names the usual method of choosing.

| Certainty | The outcome of each alternative | Two suppliers with written prices | Compare the outcomes and choose the best |

> Under certainty the task is simple comparison, but true certainty is rarer than it looks.

Under certainty, the task is to compare known outcomes against the goal. Even so, true certainty is rarer than it looks. A written price can change, a supplier can fail to deliver, and a customer can cancel. Most business decisions contain at least some risk, and a careful manager asks what could change.

> Under risk, each payoff is weighted by its chance, as section 8.4 shows.

Under risk, the decision maker weighs each possible payoff by its chance. Section 8.4 shows exactly how. The key idea is that a large payoff with a small chance may matter less than a modest payoff with a large chance.

> Under uncertainty, the first task is to reduce the uncertainty.

Under uncertainty, the first task is to reduce the uncertainty. The manager can collect information, ask people with experience, and run a small trial before a large commitment. The manager can also state assumptions openly. A planning premise, which Chapter VI described, is such an assumption.^1 By agreeing a premise, planners turn an unknown future into a working basis, while accepting that the premise may prove wrong.

> When uncertainty remains, managers use judgement, often by a hopeful or a cautious view.

Some uncertainty always remains, and then judgement is needed. Two simple views are common. Suppose alternative P may bring +Tk 20,000 at best and −Tk 5,000 at worst. Suppose alternative Q may bring +Tk 8,000 at best and +Tk 2,000 at worst. A hopeful decision maker compares the best outcomes, 20,000 against 8,000, and chooses P. A cautious decision maker compares the worst outcomes, −5,000 against +2,000, and chooses Q. The data are the same. The choices differ because the attitudes differ.

> Neither view is correct; a third approach keeps the choice flexible.

Neither view is correct. Each describes an attitude, and the right attitude depends on what the organisation can afford to lose. A third approach is to prefer alternatives that keep the choice flexible. For example, a firm may rent a shop for one year before it buys a building. If the trial goes badly, the firm has lost little and can change direction.

> Real decisions lie along a scale between certainty and uncertainty, and delay is itself a choice.

In practice the three conditions are not sharp boundaries. Real decisions lie along a scale from certainty to uncertainty. One warning applies to every point on the scale: uncertainty does not excuse a manager from deciding. Waiting for more information is itself an alternative, and it has costs.

> Pause and check: name the condition in each of three cases.

*Pause and check.* Name the condition in each case. (a) A student knows that the fee for a course is Tk 5,000. (b) A farmer knows from long records that floods damaged the crop in two years of every ten. (c) A company enters a country whose laws and customers it has never studied. The answers are at the back of the book.

> Recap: the three conditions differ by what is known about outcomes and their chances.

*Before moving on.* Certainty, risk and uncertainty differ by what the decision maker knows about outcomes and their chances. The condition decides the method. You should now be able to say: "Under certainty I know the outcomes, under risk I know their chances, and under uncertainty I do not."

## 8.3 The Rational Decision Process

> A method gives a group a record of its reasons.

Suppose the debating society of a college must choose a place for its annual dinner. Forty members will attend, and the committee has Tk 12,000. The committee could argue for an hour and accept the first idea. It could also follow a method. A method gives the committee a record of its reasons, and a record is valuable when members disagree afterwards.

> The rational decision process is a series of logical steps from problem to chosen action.
@ Rational decision process | A series of logical steps that leads from a problem to a chosen action.
@! Go slowly here. Seven steps follow; we use one example throughout.

Management writers describe such a method as a **rational decision process**. A rational decision process means a series of logical steps that leads from a problem to a chosen action. In daily life, rational means sensible. Here it has a narrower meaning: each step follows from the one before, and the final choice can be justified by the goal. The process has seven steps. We follow the society's committee through all of them.

> Step 1 states the gap between what is and what should be, and separates symptom from cause.

*Step 1: identify the problem or opportunity.* A decision begins when someone sees a gap between what is and what should be. The gap may be a problem, such as falling sales, or an opportunity, such as a new group of customers. State it exactly, in one sentence, and separate the symptom from the cause. Falling sales are a symptom. The cause may be a higher price, poorer quality, or a new competitor. A decision that treats only the symptom leaves the cause untouched. The society states its problem in this way: choose a venue for forty members, within Tk 12,000, on a date already fixed.

> Step 2 sets the criteria, the standards that every alternative will be judged by.
@ Criterion | A standard used to judge an alternative; the plural is criteria.

*Step 2: set the criteria.* A **criterion** means a standard used to judge an alternative; the plural is criteria. Criteria come from the goal and, inside an organisation, from its objectives, as Chapter VII explained. A good criterion is measurable. The word "cheap" is vague, but the phrase "costs no more than Tk 12,000" can be checked.^2 Criteria are of two kinds: those that every alternative must meet, and those that make one alternative better than another. The society sets three must-have criteria: total cost not above Tk 12,000, seats for at least forty, and a distance of not more than 5 km from the college. Among venues that meet all three, the lower cost is better.

> Step 3 gathers information and builds a list of several alternatives, including doing nothing.

*Step 3: gather information and develop alternatives.* Information costs time and money. A manager gathers enough to judge, and stops when more would cost more than it helps. The manager also builds a list of several alternatives and does not accept the first idea. The list should include doing nothing, because it shows what the other alternatives must beat. Chapter XI, section 11.6, treats the making of new ideas. The society finds three venues. The college hall costs Tk 4,000 to hire, and catering costs Tk 6,500; it seats 60 and stands on the campus. A restaurant charges Tk 11,800 in total, seats 45, and lies 3 km away. A member's rooftop needs only catering, at Tk 7,000; it seats 30 and lies 1 km away.

> Step 4 compares each alternative with the criteria, and first removes any that fail a must-have.

*Step 4: evaluate the alternatives.* The manager now compares each alternative with the criteria. The table below sets each venue against the three must-have criteria. Read each row across; the last column states the result.

:table The three venues against the criteria
| Venue | Total cost (Tk) | Seats | Distance | Meets all three? |
| College hall | 10,500 | 60 | On campus | Yes |
| Restaurant | 11,800 | 45 | 3 km | Yes |
| Rooftop | 7,000 | 30 | 1 km | No: too few seats |

> The rooftop is the cheapest, but it fails a must-have criterion and leaves the list.

The hall's total cost is Tk 4,000 + Tk 6,500 = Tk 10,500. Two venues pass. The rooftop is the cheapest, but it fails on seats and so leaves the list. This shows a first rule of evaluation: remove any alternative that fails a must-have criterion, and only then compare the rest.

> Step 5 chooses the alternative that best serves the goal; a choice must be made.

*Step 5: choose.* The manager chooses the alternative that best serves the goal. The hall and the restaurant both pass. The hall costs Tk 11,800 − Tk 10,500 = Tk 1,300 less, and it has more seats. The society therefore chooses the hall. Sometimes no alternative is perfect. The manager then chooses the best of the imperfect ones, or improves one by combining features of two. A choice must be made, because delay is itself an alternative, with its own costs.

> Step 6 puts the choice into action: who does what, by when, and who is told.

*Step 6: implement the choice.* A choice changes nothing until someone acts on it. The manager decides who will do what, by what date, with which resources. The manager also tells the people who are affected, and explains the reasons. People support a choice that they understand, and they resist one that arrives without explanation; Chapter XI studies this further. The society's treasurer books the hall, and its secretary informs the members.

> Step 7 compares results with the goal and feeds what is learned back to Step 1.

*Step 7: follow up.* After the dinner, the committee compares the results with its goal. Did the cost stay within Tk 12,000? Did the members attend? This comparison is the same logic that Chapter XIII calls control: measure the result, compare it with the standard, and correct. If the result is poor, the committee asks why. The information may have been wrong, the criteria may have been weak, or the action may have been poor. Follow-up also turns experience into learning, and the learning improves the next decision.

> The figure below shows the seven steps as a chain with a return arrow.

The figure below shows the seven steps as a chain. The arrow on the right returns from the last step to the first. It shows that the results of one decision become information for the next.

:fig The rational decision process: seven steps, with feedback from the results to the next decision. | fig_8_process.svg

> The steps overlap and loop; they guide the manager and do not bind.

The seven steps are a guide, and they do not bind. They often overlap and loop. New information in Step 3 may send the committee back to change a criterion in Step 2. Not every decision needs all seven steps in full. A routine decision may need only a glance at Step 4, while an expensive and permanent decision deserves every step. The effort should match the cost of a mistake, as section 8.1 explained.

> The process works under all three conditions; only the evaluation step changes.

The process works under all three conditions of section 8.2, but Step 4 changes. Under certainty, the manager compares known outcomes. Under risk, the manager uses the tools of section 8.4. Under uncertainty, the manager relies on judgement after reducing the uncertainty as far as possible.

> A good process raises the chance of a good result but does not guarantee it.

One more point returns us to the opening puzzle. A rational process improves the chance of a good result. It does not guarantee one. Karim followed the steps well and still received a poor result, because the weather was beyond his control. The process also assumes that the decision maker has time, information and a clear goal. Section 8.5 asks what happens when these are missing.

> Recap: the process has seven steps, from the problem to the follow-up.

*Before moving on.* The rational decision process has seven steps: identify the problem, set the criteria, gather information and develop alternatives, evaluate, choose, implement, and follow up. You should now be able to say: "Each step builds on the one before, and the last step feeds the first."

## 8.4 Tools for Choosing Under Risk

> Step 4 is easy under certainty, but chancy outcomes need tools.

Step 4 of the process, evaluation, is easy when outcomes are certain: compare the numbers. It is harder under risk, because each alternative has several possible outcomes, each with its own chance. Three tools help. Probability measures chance. Expected value combines chances and payoffs into one number. The decision tree shows the whole situation as a picture. A fourth tool, the decision support system, applies the first three by computer to large problems. We take them in this order, with small numbers first.

### Probability: Measuring Chance

> The mathematics of chance began with a question about a game.

The mathematics of chance began with a question about a game. In 1654, two French scholars, Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat, exchanged letters about how to divide the money fairly when a game of chance stops early.^3 Their reasoning grew into the theory of probability, which managers now use to weigh uncertain outcomes.

> Toss a fair coin: each face has a chance of one in two, written 0.5.
@ Probability | A number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely an outcome is.
@! Go slowly here. Probability is new; read each example twice.

Begin with a coin. Toss a fair coin. It can land heads or tails, and neither is favoured. We say that each face has a chance of one in two. **Probability** means a number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely an outcome is. The probability that a fair coin lands heads is 0.5. The same chance can be written as 50 per cent, because 0.5 × 100 = 50.

> Three rules: probabilities lie between 0 and 1, 0 means impossible, and all outcomes total 1.

Three rules govern probabilities. First, a probability is never below 0 and never above 1. Second, a probability of 0 means the outcome cannot happen, and a probability of 1 means it is certain. Third, the probabilities of all the possible outcomes of one event total exactly 1. For the coin, 0.5 + 0.5 = 1. If the chance of rain is 0.7, the chance of no rain is 1 − 0.7 = 0.3.

> The figure below shows the scale from 0 to 1.

The figure below shows the scale. Read it from left to right: the further right a point lies, the more likely the outcome.

:fig The probability scale: 0 means impossible, 1 means certain, and 0.5 means an even chance. | fig_8_probability.svg

> Probabilities come from records, or from careful judgement when no records exist.

Managers obtain probabilities in two ways. When records exist, the manager counts how often the outcome occurred and divides by the number of times it could have occurred. When no records exist, as under uncertainty, an experienced person makes a best estimate. The estimate is imperfect, but it is better than a hidden guess, because it can be recorded, discussed and corrected.

> Worked example 1: given, find and formula for a stall owner's sell-out chance.

We now work an example, in the standard order. *Given.* A stall owner counted her market days. Over 40 days, she sold out of her goods on 10 days. *Find.* The probability that she sells out on a day, and the probability that she does not. *Formula.* In words: probability equals the number of times the outcome occurred, divided by the number of times it could have occurred. In symbols: P = n ÷ N, where n is the number of times it occurred and N is the number of times it could have occurred.

> Worked example 1: steps, answer and check.

*Steps.* The probability of selling out is P = 10 ÷ 40 = 0.25. She did not sell out on 40 − 10 = 30 days, so the probability of not selling out is 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75. *Answer.* The chance of selling out is 0.25, or 25 per cent, and the chance of not selling out is 0.75. *Check.* The two probabilities must total 1, and 0.25 + 0.75 = 1. Both lie between 0 and 1.

> Common slips: writing 6 for 0.6, totals that miss 1, and too few records.

Three slips are common. Students write 6 when they mean 0.6, and so break the first rule. They give probabilities that do not total 1, which shows an error or a missing outcome. And they trust too few records: three sell-outs in four days gives 0.75, but four days is too small a sample to rely on.

> Recap: a probability is a number from 0 to 1, and all outcomes of one event total 1.

*Before moving on.* You should now be able to say: "A probability is a number from 0 to 1, and the probabilities of all the outcomes of one event total 1."

### Expected Value: One Number for a Chancy Choice

> A coin game poses the puzzle: should you play once, or many times?
@! Go slowly here. This idea is new and abstract.

A friend offers you a coin game. If the coin lands heads, you win Tk 100. If it lands tails, you lose Tk 40. Should you play? A single game is a gamble. But suppose you could play 100 times. About half the games, 50, would give +Tk 100, and about half would give −Tk 40. You would win about 50 × 100 = Tk 5,000 and lose about 50 × 40 = Tk 2,000. The net gain is Tk 3,000, or Tk 30 per game on average. This average is the idea we need.

> Expected value is the average payoff if a decision were repeated many times.
@ Expected value | In daily life, expected means thought likely. In decisions, an average payoff found by weighting each payoff by its probability.

The average has a name. **Expected value** means the average payoff of a decision if it could be repeated many times: each payoff is multiplied by its probability, and the results are added together. In daily life, "expected" means what you think will happen. Here it means a weighted average, and it may never occur in a single trial.

> The formula: multiply each payoff by its probability and add the results.

The formula is as follows. In words: expected value equals the sum of each payoff multiplied by its probability. In symbols: EV = (p1 × v1) + (p2 × v2) + …, where p1 is the probability of the first outcome, v1 is its payoff, and the dots show that the sum continues for every outcome. For the coin game, EV = (0.5 × 100) + (0.5 × (−40)) = 50 − 20 = Tk 30.

> In one game you never receive the expected value; it describes the average of many games.

In one game you will never receive Tk 30. You win 100 or you lose 40. The expected value describes the average of many games. Managers face many decisions over time, so choosing the highest expected value each time tends to give the best total result. For one very large decision that cannot be repeated, expected value is a guide and not a promise.

> Worked example 2: given, find and formula for two stalls at a school fair.

*Given.* A student club may run one stall at a school fair. Alternative A is cold drinks. Alternative B is snacks. The weather is sunny with probability 0.5 and rainy with probability 0.5. Cold drinks earn Tk 1,000 on a sunny day and Tk 200 on a rainy day. Snacks earn Tk 700 on a sunny day and Tk 400 on a rainy day. *Find.* The expected value of each alternative, and the better choice. *Formula.* EV = (p1 × v1) + (p2 × v2).

> Worked example 2: steps, answer and check.

*Steps.* For A: 0.5 × 1,000 = 500, and 0.5 × 200 = 100, so EV(A) = 500 + 100 = Tk 600. For B: 0.5 × 700 = 350, and 0.5 × 400 = 200, so EV(B) = 350 + 200 = Tk 550. *Answer.* Cold drinks have the higher expected value, Tk 600 against Tk 550, so the club chooses A. *Check.* Each expected value must lie between its worst and best payoffs: 600 lies between 200 and 1,000, and 550 lies between 400 and 700. The probabilities total 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.

> Expected value ignores the spread of payoffs and the decision maker's attitude to risk.

Notice what the expected value does not show. Cold drinks may earn as little as Tk 200 or as much as Tk 1,000, while snacks stay between Tk 400 and Tk 700. A club that cannot bear a poor day might prefer snacks, although their expected value is lower. Expected value ignores the spread of the payoffs and the attitude of the decision maker toward risk. It is a strong tool, and it is not the only consideration.

> Worked example 3: a messier case with three sites, three levels of demand and a fee for each site.

Real data are messier. *Given.* A food-cart owner must choose one site for next month. Each site has a fee, and demand may be high, medium or low, with different chances at each site. Site X, at a university gate, has a fee of Tk 4,000, chances of 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2, and profits before the fee of Tk 30,000, Tk 20,000 and Tk 6,000. Site Y, at a bus terminal, has a fee of Tk 9,000, chances of 0.4, 0.4 and 0.2, and profits before the fee of Tk 36,000, Tk 22,000 and Tk 4,000. Site Z, at a weekly market, has a fee of Tk 2,000, chances of 0.3, 0.5 and 0.2, and profits before the fee of Tk 28,000, Tk 18,000 and Tk 10,000. *Find.* The expected value of each site.

> Worked example 3: first subtract each fee, because payoffs must be net.

*Steps, part one.* The payoff must be the net amount, so we subtract each fee first. For X: 30,000 − 4,000 = 26,000; 20,000 − 4,000 = 16,000; 6,000 − 4,000 = 2,000. For Y: 36,000 − 9,000 = 27,000; 22,000 − 9,000 = 13,000; 4,000 − 9,000 = −5,000. For Z: 28,000 − 2,000 = 26,000; 18,000 − 2,000 = 16,000; 10,000 − 2,000 = 8,000.

> Worked example 3: multiply each net payoff by its chance and total.

*Steps, part two.* For X: (0.5 × 26,000) + (0.3 × 16,000) + (0.2 × 2,000) = 13,000 + 4,800 + 400 = Tk 18,200. For Y: (0.4 × 27,000) + (0.4 × 13,000) + (0.2 × (−5,000)) = 10,800 + 5,200 − 1,000 = Tk 15,000. For Z: (0.3 × 26,000) + (0.5 × 16,000) + (0.2 × 8,000) = 7,800 + 8,000 + 1,600 = Tk 17,400.

> The table below gathers the best, worst and expected values.

The table below gathers the results. Read each row across: it shows the best and worst payoffs at the site, and the expected value.

:table Expected value of each site (Tk per month)
| Site | Best outcome | Worst outcome | Expected value |
| X (university gate) | 26,000 | 2,000 | 18,200 |
| Y (bus terminal) | 27,000 | −5,000 | 15,000 |
| Z (weekly market) | 26,000 | 8,000 | 17,400 |

> Answer and check: site X has the highest value, and the chances sum to 1 at every site.

*Answer.* Site X has the highest expected value, Tk 18,200. *Check.* The chances at each site total 1: 0.5 + 0.3 + 0.2 = 1; 0.4 + 0.4 + 0.2 = 1; 0.3 + 0.5 + 0.2 = 1. Each expected value lies between its worst and best payoffs. Y is the only site with a possible loss, and it has the lowest expected value, so rejecting it is easy.

> Testing the estimates: a small change in one chance can reverse the ranking of X and Z.

The choice between X and Z deserves a second look. They differ by only Tk 800. Suppose the owner doubts the estimate for X, and changes the chances to 0.4, 0.4 and 0.2. Then EV(X) = (0.4 × 26,000) + (0.4 × 16,000) + (0.2 × 2,000) = 10,400 + 6,400 + 400 = Tk 17,200. Now Z, at Tk 17,400, is higher. A change of 0.1 in one chance reversed the ranking. When two alternatives lie this close, the estimates matter more than the arithmetic, and the safer worst case of site Z may decide the choice.

> Common slips: forgetting costs, mixed periods, and treating the expected value as a result.

Several slips are common here. Students forget to subtract costs before multiplying. They mix periods, such as a monthly payoff beside a yearly one. They compare payoffs measured in different units. And they treat the expected value as the result that will happen, when it is only the average of many trials.

> Recap: expected value is each payoff multiplied by its probability, then totalled.

*Before moving on.* You should now be able to say: "Expected value is each payoff multiplied by its probability and then added together; it is an average of many trials, and not a promise for one."

### The Decision Tree: Seeing the Whole Choice

> When choices have many branches, a picture is easier to follow than a list.
@ Decision tree | A branching diagram that shows choices, chance events, outcomes and payoffs.
@! Go slowly here. We draw the school-fair choice as a tree.

As choices grow more complex, numbers in a list become hard to follow. A picture helps. A **decision tree** means a branching diagram that shows choices, chance events, outcomes and payoffs. The figure below draws the school-fair choice of worked example 2 as a tree. We read it from left to right, in time order: first the choice, then the chance event, then the payoff.

:fig A decision tree for the school-fair stall. The square is a choice, the circles are chance events, and the figures at the right are payoffs in taka. | fig_8_tree_fair.svg

> Squares show where the decision maker chooses, and circles show where chance decides.
@ Decision node | A point on a tree, drawn as a square, where the decision maker chooses a branch.
@ Chance node | A point on a tree, drawn as a circle, where an outside event decides which branch follows.

A tree has two kinds of point. A **decision node** is a point, drawn as a square, where the decision maker chooses between branches. A **chance node** is a point, drawn as a circle, where an outside event decides which branch follows. Each branch that leaves a chance node carries a probability, and the probabilities leaving one chance node total 1. At the end of each branch we write the payoff.

> Solving the tree: calculate each chance node, compare at the square, and mark the rejected branch.

We solve the tree by moving backwards, from the payoffs toward the choice. First, we write the probabilities on the branches and the payoffs at the ends. Second, we calculate the expected value at each chance node and write it above the node. Third, at the decision node we compare these values and choose the largest, or the smallest if the payoffs are costs. Fourth, we mark each rejected branch with two short strokes. In the figure, EV(A) = (0.5 × 1,000) + (0.5 × 200) = 600, and EV(B) = (0.5 × 700) + (0.5 × 400) = 550. We choose A, as before, because the tree is the same calculation drawn as a picture.

> Advanced note: a tree may hold a second choice after a chance event; defer it if needed.

*Advanced note, which a beginner may defer.* A tree may contain a second decision node after a chance event. For example, an owner may first decide whether to pay for a survey, and then, after seeing the result, decide whether to buy. The method is the same: we move backwards from the payoffs, and calculate each node in turn.

> Pause and check: draw and solve a tree for a stall and a certain wage.

*Pause and check.* Draw a decision tree for this choice, and find the better alternative. Alternative 1: open a stall at a fair. If the crowd is large, with probability 0.4, the profit is Tk 9,000. If the crowd is small, with probability 0.6, the profit is Tk 2,000. Alternative 2: work as a paid helper for a certain Tk 4,000. The answer is at the back of the book.

> Recap: a tree shows choices as squares and chance as circles, and we solve it backwards.

*Before moving on.* You should now be able to say: "A decision tree shows choices as squares and chance events as circles, and I solve it by moving backwards from the payoffs."

### Decision Support Systems

> Computers apply the same tools to large problems quickly.
@ Decision support system | A computer tool that helps a manager study alternatives by using data and models.

Real decisions often involve many alternatives, many outcomes and large amounts of data. Calculating by hand then becomes slow. A **decision support system** means a computer tool that helps a manager study alternatives by using data and models. A simple example is a spreadsheet in which a bakery owner types flour prices and expected demand, and the sheet calculates the expected profit of each plan.

> The main strength is the "what if" question, answered in seconds.

The main strength of such a system is the "what if" question. What if flour costs 10 per cent more? What if the chance of strong demand is 0.5 and not 0.6? Each time, the system recalculates in seconds. The test that we performed by hand on site X, earlier in this section, becomes quick and easy. A manager can then see which estimates matter most, and can spend effort on improving those.

> A system supports the decision but does not make it, and its answers depend on its inputs.

The system has limits. It supports a decision, and it does not make one. Its answers are only as good as the data and assumptions put into it. A mistaken probability, entered by a person and handled by a machine, gives a confident but wrong answer. The user must therefore understand the model well enough to question it. Such systems also cost money, for equipment, software and training, and a small firm should compare that cost with the benefit. The information that a system stores and processes is one of the resources of Chapter I.

> Recap: the four tools of this section answer different needs.

*Before moving on.* Probability measures chance from 0 to 1. Expected value turns chances and payoffs into one number. A decision tree draws the same calculation as a picture. A decision support system performs it quickly on a computer.

## 8.5 How Managers Really Decide

> The rational process assumes a perfect decision maker, and no one is perfect.

Section 8.3 described a decision maker who knows the goal, finds every alternative, gathers all the information, and weighs everything perfectly. Does any manager decide in this way? Almost none does. This section asks why, and what managers do instead. It also asks whether one person or a group should decide.

> Bounded rationality is the set of limits that stops anyone from weighing every alternative.
@ Bounded rationality | The limits of time, information and mental capacity that stop a person from weighing every alternative.

Herbert Simon, an American scholar who studied how organisations decide, argued from 1947 onward that human beings cannot decide in the perfect way.^4 He called the limit **bounded rationality**. Bounded rationality means the limits of time, information and mental capacity that stop a person from weighing every alternative. The word "bounded" means limited, as a field is limited by a fence. The decision maker still tries to be rational, but works inside the fence.

> Four limits bound a decision: information, time, the mind and the future.

Four limits form the fence. First, information is often incomplete, late or costly. Second, time is short, because decisions have deadlines. Third, the mind is limited: a person can hold only a few factors in attention at once, and memory fails. Fourth, the future is unknown, and the goals of different people may conflict. Because of these limits, no manager can list every alternative or foresee every outcome.

> Satisficing means choosing the first alternative that is good enough.
@ Satisficing | Choosing the first alternative that meets all the criteria, instead of searching for the best.

Because of these limits, managers often do not search for the best alternative. They search until they find one that is good enough. Simon named this habit **satisficing**, a word that joins "satisfy" and "suffice". Satisficing means choosing the first alternative that meets all the criteria, instead of searching for the best. In section 8.3, two venues passed the criteria. A committee short of time might have booked whichever venue it examined first.

> Satisficing is often sensible because search costs time, but low criteria give poor choices.

Satisficing is not laziness. Search has a cost, and after a point the cost of searching exceeds the gain from a slightly better alternative. A manager who satisfices wisely has judged that point. The danger lies elsewhere: if the criteria are set too low, the first alternative that passes may be a poor one. The criteria of Step 2 therefore deserve the most care.

> Experience gives managers a sense of judgement that helps when time is short but can mislead.

Experienced managers also use judgement. After many years, a manager may sense that a plan is unsound before being able to say why. This sense comes from many past cases stored in memory. It is valuable when time is short. It can mislead when the new situation differs from the old ones. The best managers combine analysis with judgement, and use each to check the other.

> Three habits of thought distort decisions: anchoring, sunk costs and overconfidence.

Some habits of thought distort decisions, and managers should know them. The first is *anchoring*, which means relying too heavily on the first number heard. An early price quotation then sets a standard that it does not deserve. The second is the sunk cost error. A *sunk cost* is money already spent that cannot be recovered. A manager who continues a failing project because so much has been spent lets the past control the future. The third is *overconfidence*, which means feeling surer than the evidence allows. A written record of the reasons for a choice helps to correct all three.

> Rules, culture, law and competitors also limit which alternatives a manager can choose.

Decisions are also shaped by the organisation and its surroundings. Rules, culture and politics may rule out an alternative that the manager prefers. Law may forbid it, as Chapter IV explained when it described the external environment. A budget may not allow it. The decision maker therefore chooses within limits set by others, and a skilled manager learns these limits early.

> Many decisions are made by groups, which raises the question of when a group is better.

Many decisions are made by one manager, and many by groups, such as committees, teams and boards. Which is better? The answer depends on the situation. The table below lists the strengths and the limits of group decisions. The two columns are separate lists: the rows are not pairs, and each row stands alone.

:table Group decisions: strengths and limits
| Strengths | Limits |
| More information and more skills are present | Discussion takes more time and costs more |
| More alternatives are suggested | Pressure to agree can silence doubts |
| Members understand and support the choice | One strong member may dominate |
| Errors are more likely to be noticed | Compromise may give a weak choice |
| Responsibility is shared | No one may feel accountable |

> A group gains information and acceptance but loses time and may suffer from pressure to agree.

Read the strengths first. A group holds more knowledge than one person, and so it can suggest more alternatives and notice more errors. People also support a choice that they helped to make, and so implementation in Step 6 is easier. Now read the limits. A meeting of eight people for two hours uses 8 × 2 = 16 hours of staff time, and that time has a cost. Members may stay silent to avoid conflict, or one forceful member may control the discussion. A compromise that pleases everyone may be weaker than the best choice. And when everyone shares responsibility, no one may feel accountable.

> One person suits routine matters, emergencies and cases where one person holds the knowledge.

These strengths and limits suggest a guide. A group suits a complex problem, a choice that needs the support of many people, and a case in which time allows discussion. One manager suits a routine matter, an emergency, and a case in which one person holds all the needed knowledge. A manager may also consult: ask the group for views, then decide alone and remain accountable. How far a leader should involve others is a question of leadership style, which Chapter XII studies.^5

> Groupthink is the group's habit of seeking agreement so strongly that ideas are not tested.
@ Groupthink | The habit of a group of seeking agreement so strongly that its members stop testing ideas.

The most serious limit deserves a name. Irving Janis, an American psychologist, studied failed group decisions and described the problem in 1972. He called it **groupthink**. Groupthink means the habit of a group of seeking agreement so strongly that its members stop testing ideas. Members who have doubts keep silent, and silence is taken for agreement. The group then feels more confident than the evidence allows. The section on history, at the end of this chapter, describes a well-known case.

> Groups can guard against groupthink by protecting disagreement in the way they work.

A group can guard against groupthink by building disagreement into its method. The leader can speak last, so that members are not influenced by the leader's view. Each member can write a view before the discussion begins. One member can be given the task of questioning the plan. People from outside the group can be invited to comment. The group can divide into two subgroups, work separately, and then compare. Chapter XI, section 11.6, describes methods for producing ideas in groups.

> Recap: real decisions are bounded, managers satisfice, and groups bring strengths and limits.

*Before moving on.* Real decision makers face bounded rationality, and so they often satisfice. Groups add information and acceptance but cost time and risk groupthink. You should now be able to say: "A rational process is an ideal, and real managers work inside limits."

## A Worked Case: Choosing the Second Oven

> The bakery faces a choice under risk and settles it with a tree.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Month 8. Demand for Morning Loaf Bakery has grown, and the single oven limits output. The owner has two alternatives. Alternative A is to buy a second oven for Tk 180,000. Alternative B is to extend the opening hours. The case file records a probability of 0.6 that demand is strong and 0.4 that demand is weak. Under A, the monthly gain is Tk 30,000 if demand is strong and Tk 8,000 if it is weak. Under B, the monthly gain is Tk 15,000 if demand is strong and Tk 9,000 if it is weak.

> The question: which alternative should the owner choose, and how soon does the oven repay its cost?

*Question.* Which alternative should the owner choose, and how long would the oven take to repay its cost? This worked case applies the idea of the chapter to a situation. It is not a model answer to any examination, and it shows one reasoning that you can adapt.

> Reasoning begins with the condition: risk, because the chances are recorded.

*Reasoning: the condition.* The case file records the chances of strong and weak demand, and the possible gains are known. The owner knows the outcomes and their chances, so the condition is risk and not uncertainty. The tools of section 8.4 therefore apply. The goal is the highest expected monthly gain, and the criteria are the expected value and the time needed to repay the cost.

> The figure below shows the owner's choice as a tree.

The figure below draws the choice as a tree. The square is the owner's choice, the circles are the two chance events, and the figures at the right are the monthly gains.

:fig The owner's choice at Morning Loaf Bakery. Payoffs are monthly gains in taka. | fig_8_tree_bakery.svg

> The expected values are Tk 21,200 for A and Tk 12,600 for B.

*Reasoning: the expected values.* For A: (0.6 × 30,000) + (0.4 × 8,000) = 18,000 + 3,200 = Tk 21,200. For B: (0.6 × 15,000) + (0.4 × 9,000) = 9,000 + 3,600 = Tk 12,600. Alternative A is higher by Tk 21,200 − Tk 12,600 = Tk 8,600 per month. The expected value compares monthly gains only, and the oven also costs Tk 180,000 at the start. The next step takes that cost into account.

> The payback period is the cost divided by the expected monthly gain.
@ Payback period | The time that a purchase takes to earn back its cost.

*Reasoning: the payback period.* The **payback period** means the time that a purchase takes to earn back its cost. In words: payback equals the cost divided by the monthly gain. For alternative A, the payback is 180,000 ÷ 21,200 = 8.49, or about 8.5 months.^6 The result depends on demand. If demand is strong, the payback is 180,000 ÷ 30,000 = 6 months. If demand is weak, it is 180,000 ÷ 8,000 = 22.5 months. The owner can therefore see both the likely benefit and the cost of being wrong.

> The owner consulted two staff members before choosing, and remained accountable.

*Reasoning: the people.* The case file records that the owner spoke with the head baker and the counter lead before choosing. These two staff members know the kitchen and the counter better than anyone else, and so they add information that section 8.5 values in a group. The choice still belonged to the owner, who stays accountable for the result.

> Conclusion: alternative A is chosen.

*Conclusion.* The owner chooses alternative A, the second oven. It has the higher expected gain, Tk 21,200 against Tk 12,600, and an expected payback of about 8.5 months. In the strong case, A beats B by Tk 15,000 a month. In the weak case, A falls below B by only Tk 1,000 a month. Step 7, the follow-up, belongs to the control of results, which Chapter XIII describes.

> Check: the chances total 1, each value lies in its range, and the payback is consistent.

*Check.* The chances total 0.6 + 0.4 = 1 on every branch. Each expected value lies between its worst and best gains: 21,200 lies between 8,000 and 30,000, and 12,600 lies between 9,000 and 15,000. The payback is consistent: 21,200 × 8.5 = 180,200, which is close to the cost of 180,000.

## Summary

> A decision is a choice among alternatives, with a decision maker, a goal, and outcomes.

A decision is a choice among alternatives. Every decision has a decision maker, a goal, alternatives, and outcomes with payoffs. The decision maker controls the choice but not the outside events that shape the outcome.

> The condition of the decision depends on what is known: certainty, risk or uncertainty.

Decisions are made under three conditions. Under certainty, the outcomes are known. Under risk, the outcomes and their chances are known. Under uncertainty, the chances are not known. The condition belongs to the decision maker's knowledge, and the best first response to uncertainty is to gather information.

> The rational process has seven steps, and it raises the chance of a good result without guaranteeing one.

The rational decision process has seven steps: identify the problem, set the criteria, gather information and develop alternatives, evaluate, choose, implement, and follow up. The process raises the chance of a good result. It does not guarantee one.

> Probability, expected value and the tree help to choose under risk; computers apply them quickly.

Probability measures chance from 0 to 1. Expected value multiplies each payoff by its probability and adds the results. A decision tree draws the same calculation, and we solve it by moving backwards from the payoffs. A decision support system performs such work by computer, but it supports the decision and does not make it.

> Real decisions are bounded, and groups bring both strengths and risks.

Real decision makers face bounded rationality and often satisfice. Groups add information and acceptance, but they cost time and risk groupthink. A group can guard against groupthink by protecting disagreement in its method.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake 1: judging a decision only by its result.

*Mistaken idea.* "A decision is good if it produces a good result." *Correction.* A result depends on the decision and on events outside the decision maker's control. A fair judgement looks at the information available and the method used, as well as the result.

> Mistake 2: believing that the expected value is the amount you will receive.

*Mistaken idea.* "The expected value is the amount I will receive." *Correction.* The expected value is an average over many repeated trials. In a single trial, you receive one of the possible payoffs, and it may be far from the average.

> Mistake 3: believing that certainty, risk and uncertainty belong to the world.

*Mistaken idea.* "Each decision is certain, risky or uncertain by its nature." *Correction.* The condition depends on how much the decision maker knows. Gathering information can move a decision from uncertainty toward risk.

> Mistake 4: believing that a rational process needs all possible information.

*Mistaken idea.* "A rational process means collecting every fact before choosing." *Correction.* Information has a cost, and time is limited. A rational manager gathers enough information to judge, and stops when more would cost more than it helps.

> Mistake 5: believing that more people always give a better decision.

*Mistaken idea.* "A group always decides better than one person." *Correction.* A group adds information and acceptance, but it also costs time and risks groupthink. The situation decides which is better.

> Mistake 6: arithmetic slips, such as costs not subtracted or chances that do not total 1.

*Mistaken idea.* "The arithmetic is not important if the method is right." *Correction.* A forgotten cost or a set of chances that does not total 1 can reverse the ranking of alternatives. Always check that the chances total 1 and that costs are subtracted.

## In History

> A famous failure of a group decision, studied to learn how groups can fail.

In April 1961, a few months after John F. Kennedy became President of the United States, a force of about 1,400 Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs, on the southern coast of Cuba. The American Central Intelligence Agency had trained and equipped them. The plan aimed to start a revolt against the Cuban government. No revolt followed, and Cuban forces defeated the landing within about three days.

> Janis argued that the advisers failed to test the plan, and drew the lesson of groupthink.

Irving Janis studied the meetings in which the plan was approved. He argued, in a book of 1972, that the group failed to test the plan. Members who had doubts kept them private, and the group took silence for agreement. Janis used this case to describe groupthink. He contrasted it with the meetings of October 1962, during the Cuban missile crisis, when the President changed the method. In some meetings the President did not attend, so that members could speak freely. The advisers were also divided into subgroups, and outside views were invited.

> The lesson: a better method, not better people, improved the later decisions.

The lesson for managers is practical. The advisers of 1962 were largely the same people as those of 1961. The quality of the decision improved when the method protected disagreement. The remedy lies in the process of the group, as section 8.5 described, and not only in the character of its members.
