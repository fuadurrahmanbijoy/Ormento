:chapter X | Authority, Delegation and Span
:mood How much may one person decide, and for how many?
:desc Structure gives people places; authority gives them power to act in those places. This chapter explains authority and responsibility, the three kinds of authority, the art of delegating, the number of people one manager can direct, and the choice between deciding at the top and deciding far below.

> A mother gives her son a task but not the means to do it.

A mother is expecting guests for dinner. She tells her sixteen-year-old son to cook the meal. He agrees, and then finds that the store cupboard is locked and the housekeeping money is in her purse. He cannot buy rice. He cannot even start. The task was given, but the means were kept back.

> The mother then makes the opposite mistake and checks every step.

The next week she tries again. This time she gives him the key and the money. But she stands in the kitchen and corrects every cut of the knife, every pinch of salt. After twenty minutes he leaves, saying that she may cook it herself. Now the means were given, but the freedom to use them was taken away.

> Both failures come from one problem: matching the task to the power to do it.

Both failures have one cause. The mother did not match the task she handed over to the power she handed over with it. In a family, the cost is a spoiled evening. In a business, the same mistake costs sales, wasted time and employees who stop caring. Three questions follow. How much power should go with each job? How many people can one manager direct? And which decisions should be taken at the top, and which far below?

> The chapter answers these questions with five ideas.

This chapter answers the three questions in turn. It begins with authority and its partners, responsibility and accountability. It then separates three kinds of authority, teaches the steps and obstacles of delegation, and shows how to judge the number of people one manager can direct. It ends with the choice between centralising and decentralising decisions.

## Authority and Responsibility

> A plan, a job and a chart do not by themselves let anyone act.

@! Go slowly here.

Chapter 9 gave every job a place on the chart. A place on a chart does not let the holder buy, hire, refuse or approve. Something more is needed, and that something is the subject of this section. Three ideas work together here, so we take them one at a time and then join them.

> Authority is the right to give orders and use resources.

@ Authority | The right to give orders and to use resources, given by one's position.
@ Subordinate | A person who reports to a manager.

The first idea is **authority**. Authority means the right to give orders and to use resources, given by one's position. A shop manager may order stock and tell the counter employees which tasks to do. The right belongs to the position, not to the person: when a new manager takes the post, the right passes to the new person. A person who reports to a manager is a **subordinate**, and the manager's authority is the right to direct the subordinate's work.

> Responsibility is the duty to do the assigned task.

@ Responsibility | The duty to do the task that has been assigned.

The second idea is **responsibility**. Responsibility means the duty to do the task that has been assigned.^1 When the head baker is told to see that the bread is ready by six o'clock, she holds a responsibility. It is an obligation toward the person who gave the task. It is also an obligation that cannot be forgotten, since the task still waits if she does nothing.

> Accountability means answering for results; it stays with the person who delegates.

The third idea was met in Chapter 1: accountability, being answerable for results. It differs from responsibility. Responsibility looks forward, to the task to be done. Accountability looks back, to the result that was reached, and it asks who must explain it. The head baker is responsible for the bread; the owner remains accountable to customers and lenders for all the bread the bakery sells. The figure below shows how the three pass along the chain of command.

:fig How authority and duty pass down the chain of command, while answerability passes up. | ch10_fig1_delegation.svg

> Authority and responsibility must be matched.

The three ideas must fit together. The rule is that authority should match responsibility. The mother's first failure gave responsibility without authority; the son could not do what he was told to do. Her second failure gave authority and then took it back. When responsibility is larger than authority, the holder is blamed for things beyond control, and morale falls. When authority is larger than responsibility, the holder may act carelessly, because no duty follows the act. Good organising keeps the two in step.

> Authority has four sources, and each sets a limit.

Where does a manager's authority come from? There are four common sources. The first is ownership: the owner of a business may direct it, and may pass part of that right to others. The second is law, which gives certain officers, such as a company's directors, rights and duties that no one may remove. The third is delegation from above, which is how most managers receive their authority: the owner gives it to a general manager, the general manager gives part of it to a department head, and so on down. The fourth is custom, the habits of an organisation that people have come to accept. Each source also sets a limit. An owner may not order what the law forbids. A manager may not give a subordinate more authority than the manager holds. And custom can be changed only slowly.

> Authority flows down the chain of command, one level at a time.

Authority flows down the chain of command that Chapter 9 described, from the top box to the bottom box. This path is sometimes called the scalar chain, meaning the ladder of ranks. Orders are expected to travel along it, level by level, and answers are expected to travel back up. The path has a practical use: it tells every employee who the manager is, and where to turn when a question is too large for them. It has a cost too, because a message that passes through five levels is slow and may change on the way. Fayol (1916) accepted that a short cut may sometimes be wise, but he asked that the managers on either side agree to it, so that nobody is surprised.

> Authority has natural limits that every manager should know.

Authority is never unlimited. Law limits it, since no order may require an unlawful act. Ethics limits it, since an order to deceive customers should be refused, as Chapter 5 explained. Resources limit it, since a manager may order a task only for which the money, people and time exist. The authority of the position limits it, since a counter supervisor has no right to change the price of bread. And acceptance limits it, as Barnard showed. A new manager who forgets these limits will soon find that an order on paper is not an order in practice.

> Authority is more effective when its limits are stated.

For this reason, a good organisation states the limits of each position in writing: what the holder may decide alone, what needs approval, and what is not the holder's business. A stated limit does two things. It protects the holder, who knows how far to go. And it protects the organisation, which knows that no single person can commit it beyond a known sum. The bakery case later in this chapter shows such a limit in use.

> Unity of command protects authority from confusion.

Authority works best when each person knows one manager. Chapter 3 gave this idea the name unity of command. Suppose two managers each give the same employee an order, and the orders conflict. The employee must disobey one of them, and both managers lose authority. In the opening scene, imagine that the father and the mother had both given the son different orders about the dinner: he could not obey both. Organisations avoid this by drawing the chain of command so that each box has one line above it. Chapter 9 showed the matrix, which breaks the rule on purpose, and explained the price: confusion unless the two managers agree in advance.

> Authority differs from power, a topic that Chapter 12 treats in full.

Authority is not the same as power. Authority is a right that comes from the position and is accepted by those below. Power, taught in Chapter 12, means the ability to make others act, and it can come from sources other than position, such as knowledge or personal respect. A junior engineer with rare skill may have power without authority. A new manager whom nobody respects may have authority without much power. Chester Barnard (1938) argued that authority in practice depends on whether subordinates accept an order.^2 An order that most people refuse to obey has little authority behind it, however high the position.

> The family and the chart show the same three ideas in different words.

Return to the dinner. The mother held authority over the kitchen and, as host, was accountable for the evening. When she told her son to cook, she should have handed over a share of authority (the key and the money) and assigned responsibility (the meal), while remaining accountable to her guests. That is the whole pattern of this chapter in a single scene.

> A recap that fixes the three ideas.

*Before moving on.* Authority is the right to give orders and use resources. Responsibility is the duty to do the assigned task. Accountability is answering for the result. Authority should match responsibility, and accountability stays with the one who delegates. Power is a separate idea, taught in Chapter 12.
*You should now be able to say:* "Authority is the right to direct, responsibility is the duty to do, and accountability is answering for the result."

## Line, Staff and Functional Authority

> A manager's right may be to command, to advise, or to rule on one subject.

In Chapter 9 we met line positions, which do the core work, and staff positions, which advise. The two kinds of position hold different kinds of authority. A third kind arises when a specialist has a right that reaches across departments. The three are taught together because they are easily confused.

> Line authority is the right to direct the work of subordinates.

@ Line authority | The right to direct subordinates and to give them orders.

**Line authority** is the right to direct subordinates and to give them orders. It runs down the chain of command, from owner to manager to employee. A line manager may assign tasks, approve leave and judge performance. Line authority is complete within its area, which means that the person who holds it also decides.

> Staff authority is the right to advise.

@ Staff authority | The right to advise and support the line, but not to command it.

**Staff authority** is the right to advise and support the line, but not to command it. A legal adviser may tell a manager that a contract is unsafe. The manager may take the advice or refuse it. Staff authority usually takes one of three forms. Advisory staff recommend, as a lawyer does. Service staff supply a service, as a transport pool does. Control staff check that rules are followed, as an inspector does. In every form, the staff member does not direct line employees.

> Functional authority is a right to rule on one subject across other departments.

@ Functional authority | The right of a specialist over a process outside the specialist's own line.

**Functional authority** is the right of a specialist over a process outside the specialist's own line. The specialist cannot direct a department's work in general, but may require that one process be done in one way. An accounts officer may require receipts from every department before money is repaid. A safety officer may stop a machine that is unsafe. The right is narrow: it covers one subject, in every department. Chapter 9 described the older idea of several specialist bosses; functional authority is a limited, safer version of it.

> The three kinds of authority are compared in the table and the figure.

The table and the figure below compare the three kinds. Read each row as a question with three answers.

> Line and staff often quarrel, and clear roles reduce the quarrel.

Friction is common. Line managers may think that advisers have never faced real pressure. Advisers may think that line managers ignore good ideas. Four habits help. State in writing what each position may and may not do. Require line managers to listen to advice before deciding. Expect advisers to explain and win support, not to order. Allow the line manager to decide, and to answer for the decision. The same care applies to functional authority: it should be kept narrow, because every extra right reduces the unity of command that Chapter 3 recommended.

> A hotel shows all three kinds of authority at work in one place.

Consider a hotel with a general manager, a front-office manager, a kitchen manager and a housekeeping manager. Each of the three managers has line authority over their own team: the front-office manager directs the receptionists, the kitchen manager directs the cooks, and the housekeeping manager directs the cleaners. The hotel also employs a lawyer, who advises on guest contracts; that is staff authority. And the hotel's safety officer has the right to close any kitchen burner that fails an inspection, whichever manager protests; that is functional authority, limited to safety. If the safety officer began to tell the kitchen manager how to plan the menu, the officer would be passing the limit of functional authority.

> The test of any right is to ask three questions.

A simple test sorts any case. Ask first whether the person may direct the daily work of others. If so, the authority is line. Ask next whether the person may only advise. If so, it is staff. Ask finally whether the person may rule on one subject across several departments. If so, it is functional. A person may hold more than one kind: the safety officer has staff authority over most matters, for advice, and functional authority over safety rules.

> Both staff and functional roles can be abused, and managers should watch for it.

Misuse is possible in both directions. A staff adviser who is close to the owner may begin to give orders by hinting that the owner agrees; this is staff power grown beyond staff authority. A line manager may ignore a rule from a functional specialist by arguing that the work is urgent. Both habits damage trust. A clear written statement of each right, and a way for the owner to settle disputes, prevents most of the trouble.

> The need for staff and functional roles grows with size and with complexity.

Why do organisations add staff and functional roles at all? The reason is that one line manager cannot know everything. A bakery of four employees needs no lawyer; a bakery chain with forty shops does. As the firm grows, the rules of tax, safety, hiring and accounting multiply, and no line manager can master them all. A specialist who knows one subject well can serve many managers at once, which is cheaper than training every manager. The cost is the friction already described, and the risk that the specialist's rule may be applied without regard for the needs of the work. The gain and the cost must be weighed, as with every design choice in this book.

> A recap of the three kinds of authority.

*Before moving on.* Line authority commands. Staff authority advises. Functional authority rules on one subject in other departments. Friction is reduced by clear roles, and the line manager keeps the final decision.

## Delegation and Its Obstacles

> A manager who does everything personally limits the firm to what one person can do.

@! Go slowly here.

Return to the mother. If she cooks every dinner herself, the family can enjoy only what one pair of hands can make. If she teaches her son to cook, the family gains a second cook, and she gains time. A manager faces the same choice. A manager who does everything personally limits the organisation to what one person can do. That choice leads to delegation.

> Delegation means passing authority and duty to a subordinate.

@ Delegation | Passing authority and duty for a task down to a subordinate.

**Delegation** means passing authority and duty for a task down to a subordinate. It is not the same as dumping work. When a manager gives a task without the means to do it, as the mother did first, that is not delegation. Delegation has a plan: it chooses the task, the person, the power and the way to check progress. The figure above shows the direction of travel.

> Delegation follows four steps, taken in order.

Four steps make a good delegation. First, assign the duty: state the task and the result wanted, in plain words. Second, grant the authority: give the right to act, and the resources needed, within limits that are stated. Third, create the obligation: make clear that the subordinate must answer for the result to the manager. Fourth, follow up: agree when and how progress will be checked, and then check, without taking the task back. Each step answers a question: what, with what power, answerable for what, and checked how.

> Delegation brings gains to the manager, the subordinate and the firm.

Delegation has three kinds of gain. The manager gains time for planning and for work only a manager can do. The subordinate gains skill, trust and a reason to care about the result. The firm gains speed, since decisions are made close to the work, and gains managers for the future, because people learn by deciding. These gains explain why successful managers delegate more than they like to.

> Obstacles to delegation come from managers, subordinates and the organisation.

If delegation brings so many gains, why do managers hold back? The obstacles fall into three groups. Some lie with the manager. A manager may fear losing control, may believe that "I can do it faster myself", may fear that a subordinate will do the job better, or may not know how to explain the task. Some lie with the subordinate. A subordinate may fear blame for mistakes, may lack confidence, may lack information or tools, or may see no extra reward for extra duty. Some lie with the organisation. Duties may be poorly defined, a culture of blame may punish every error, and there may be no simple way to check results.

> Each obstacle has a matching cure.

Each obstacle has a cure. A manager who fears losing control can set limits and agree dates for review, so that control is kept without interference. A manager who thinks it faster to do the work alone should count the cost of repeating that choice every week. A subordinate who fears blame is helped by a rule that honest mistakes are treated as lessons. A subordinate who lacks tools is given them. And the organisation can define duties in writing and reward those who take on more. Trust is built in small steps: start with a small task, check it, and then give a larger one.

> Delegation may be given in different degrees, from cautious to free.

Delegation is not an all-or-nothing act, and managers can choose a degree. In the cautious degree, the subordinate studies the matter and recommends, and the manager decides. In a middle degree, the subordinate decides and acts but tells the manager at once. In a free degree, the subordinate decides and acts, and reports only at the regular review. A manager who knows a subordinate well may move from the first degree toward the third as trust grows. A manager who is new to the task, or whose subordinate is untried, should begin with the first degree.

> Some tasks should not be delegated.

Some tasks belong to the manager and should not be passed down. They include setting the goals of the whole unit, because no one else sees the whole picture; deciding disputes between subordinates; matters of discipline and dismissal; matters that are confidential; and crises that threaten the whole firm. A manager who delegates these tasks has not delegated but abandoned them. The test is to ask whether the task needs the authority of the manager's own position, and not merely the skill of whoever does it.

> The hotel supervisor example applies the four steps in order.

Take the hotel again. The front-office manager wishes to delegate the handling of guest complaints to a supervisor. *Assign:* the manager states the duty, which is to settle complaints about rooms on the same day, and gives the result wanted, which is that no guest leaves unhappy. *Grant:* the manager gives authority to offer a free night's breakfast, or a room change, up to a stated value, without asking. *Create the obligation:* the supervisor agrees to answer to the manager for the number and the outcome of complaints each week. *Follow up:* the manager reads a short weekly note, and meets the supervisor once a month. The delegation fits the four steps, and the manager has not taken the task back.

> Control and trust must be balanced in delegation.

Every delegation sets control against trust. If the manager keeps too much control, by checking every step, the subordinate has no real authority, and the mother's second failure is repeated. If the manager gives too much trust, with no review, errors may grow unseen. The balance is reached by agreeing in advance on the result wanted, the limits of authority and the dates of review. Within those limits, the subordinate chooses the method. Chapter 13 will show that this is control by results, in which the manager watches the outcome and not every movement.

> Four rules guide delegation.

Four rules summarise good practice. Authority should match responsibility, as the first section showed. Each subordinate should have one manager, which protects unity of command. Delegation should pass down the chain, not skip a level. And accountability cannot be delegated: the manager who passes a task down still answers for its result. The last rule is the one most often forgotten, and the final question of this chapter returns to it.

> A short recap of delegation.

*Before moving on.* Delegation means passing authority and duty for a task down to a subordinate. Its four steps are assign, grant, create the obligation and follow up. Obstacles come from managers, subordinates and the organisation, and each has a cure. The manager stays accountable.
*You should now be able to say:* "Delegation passes authority and duty down, but accountability stays with the manager."

## Span of Management

> A manager can direct only so many people before quality falls.

@ Span of management | The number of subordinates who report directly to one manager.

A manager with one subordinate has time for every detail. A manager with fifty has time for none. Between those extremes lies a question that every organisation must answer: how many people should report to one manager? The number is called the **span of management**. Span of management means the number of subordinates who report directly to one manager. A manager with three direct reports has a span of three.

> The span fixes the number of levels in the organisation.

The span decides the shape of the structure. If the span is small, many managers are needed, and the structure has many levels. If the span is large, fewer managers are needed, and the structure has few levels. The next paragraph names the two shapes, and a worked calculation then shows the difference in numbers.

@ Tall structure | A structure with many levels and small spans.
@ Flat structure | A structure with few levels and large spans.

> A tall structure has many levels; a flat structure has few.

A **tall structure** has many levels and small spans. A **flat structure** has few levels and large spans. The figure below shows both, using the same eight operatives, so that the effect of span is the only change.

:fig The same eight operatives in a tall structure (span 2) and a flat structure (span 4 below the top). | ch10_fig3_tall_flat.svg

> A calculation shows how span changes the number of managers.

Suppose a workshop has 64 operatives. *Given:* 64 operatives. *Find:* the number of managers needed if each manager has a span of 8, of 4, and of 2. *Method:* divide the number of people at one level by the span to find the managers needed to direct them, and repeat upward until one person is left. *Steps for span 8:* 64 ÷ 8 = 8 managers; 8 ÷ 8 = 1 manager. Total managers 8 + 1 = 9, in 2 levels. *Steps for span 4:* 64 ÷ 4 = 16; 16 ÷ 4 = 4; 4 ÷ 4 = 1. Total 16 + 4 + 1 = 21, in 3 levels. *Steps for span 2:* 64 ÷ 2 = 32; 32 ÷ 2 = 16; 16 ÷ 2 = 8; 8 ÷ 2 = 4; 4 ÷ 2 = 2; 2 ÷ 2 = 1. Total 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 63, in 6 levels. *Answer:* 9, 21 and 63 managers. *Check:* each answer is the sum of a chain that ends at one person.

> The numbers show a trade-off between cost and control.

The numbers show a trade-off. Span 2 needs 63 managers, far more than the 9 needed at span 8. More managers cost more, and orders must pass through six levels, so decisions are slow and messages may change on the way. Span 8 is cheap and quick, but each manager has less time for each person. Neither extreme is right. The question is which span suits the work.

> Span depends on six situational factors.

Six factors help to decide the right span. A manager can direct more people when the work is routine, because less explanation is needed; and fewer people when the work is complex. A larger span is possible when subordinates are skilled and trained, because they need less guidance. A larger span is possible when duties are clearly defined, and when plans and standards are in writing. A manager whose subordinates work close together can direct more than one whose subordinates are spread across the city. A manager with helpers, such as an assistant, can direct more. And a manager who must spend much time on other duties, such as planning, can direct fewer.

> No single number is correct, but beginners often prefer a range.

Writers have offered numbers, but none fits all cases. Some suggested that about five or six subordinates suit a top manager, and that larger spans suit supervisors of routine work, who may direct a dozen or more. These figures are guides, not laws. The factors above matter more than any number.

> A second calculation shows how span links to the number of people reached.

The calculation may be turned round. *Given:* a manager has a span of 5, and each of those five subordinates also has a span of 5. *Find:* how many operatives are reached at the second level. *Steps:* 5 × 5 = 25. A third level reaches 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. *Answer:* two levels below the top reach 25 people, and three levels reach 125. *Check:* 125 ÷ 5 = 25 and 25 ÷ 5 = 5, which returns to the manager's span. The result shows how quickly a structure widens: each new level multiplies the number of people by the span.

> Span also affects how messages travel.

Span affects the speed and accuracy of messages. In a tall structure, a message from the top passes through many managers, and each manager may shorten, soften or alter it. In a flat structure, the message passes through few hands and arrives nearly as sent, but each manager has less time to explain it. The right balance depends on how complex the message is. A simple order, such as the day's production target, travels well through many levels. A complex change, such as a new quality method, is better explained face to face by few managers.

> A clinic shows how the span factors work in practice.

Take a clinic with a manager and twelve employees: two doctors, three nurses, a pharmacist, two receptionists, a laboratory technician, a cleaner and a part-time accountant. If the manager directs all twelve, the span is twelve, which is large for work that varies from patient to patient. If the manager directs only the doctors, the head nurse and the head receptionist, the span is four, and the head nurse and head receptionist direct the rest. The second arrangement suits skilled but varied work. The first might suit a clinic in which the tasks were routine and the employees experienced.

> Tall and flat structures each carry their own risks.

Each shape carries risks. A tall structure slows decisions, raises cost, and lets messages change as they pass many levels. It also gives close supervision, which suits new or unskilled employees. A flat structure speeds decisions and gives subordinates room to act, but it demands skilled employees and strong delegation, since the manager has little time to watch. Many firms have made their structures flatter in recent decades, partly because communication is faster, but they have not removed the trade-off.

> The span also shapes the manager's style and the subordinate's freedom.

The span also shapes how people work. A manager with a span of two can watch both subordinates closely, and subordinates may feel supervised. A manager with a span of twelve cannot watch everyone, and subordinates must decide more for themselves. This explains why a firm that wants initiative from its employees often widens spans, and why a firm with new or untrained employees often narrows them. The span is therefore not only a count of people but also a choice about how much freedom to give.

> A recap of span.

*Before moving on.* Span of management means the number of subordinates who report directly to one manager. Small spans make tall structures, and large spans make flat ones. The right span depends on the work, the people, the clarity of duties, the closeness of the group, the help available and the manager's other duties.
*You should now be able to say:* "A smaller span needs more managers and more levels; a larger span needs fewer of both."

## Centralisation and Decentralisation

> Every decision has a level at which it is made, and the level can be moved.

Chapter 8 taught how to make a decision. This section asks a different question: who should make it? In every organisation some decisions are taken by the owner, and others by people lower down. How far decisions are gathered at the top is a design choice, and it has a name.

@ Centralisation | Keeping decision authority at the top of the organisation.
@ Decentralisation | Spreading decision authority down to lower levels.

> Centralisation holds authority at the top; decentralisation spreads it down.

**Centralisation** means keeping decision authority at the top of the organisation. **Decentralisation** means spreading decision authority down to lower levels. They are not two boxes but the ends of a scale, like the two design models in Chapter 9. An organisation is more or less centralised, and the degree may differ from one kind of decision to another. The figure below shows the scale.

:fig The centralisation scale; positions are illustrative. | ch10_fig4_centralisation.svg

> Decentralisation is wider than delegation and differs from it.

Take care to separate decentralisation from delegation. Delegation is what one manager does with one task and one subordinate, and the manager can take the task back. Decentralisation is a policy for the whole organisation: it spreads decision authority across many positions as a matter of design, and it is made permanent in the structure. Decentralisation is delegation carried widely, but delegation can happen in a very centralised firm.

> Centralisation brings uniformity and control, but also delay and distance from the facts.

Centralisation has strong arguments. Decisions are consistent across the firm. Top managers see the whole picture. Costs fall when buying or hiring is done once for all. Control is easier, and crises can be met with one voice. It also has costs. Top managers become overloaded. Decisions wait for those far from the facts. Lower managers have no chance to learn by deciding, and their interest fades.

> Decentralisation brings speed and learning, but also risk of inconsistency.

Decentralisation has opposite strengths. Decisions are quick, because they are taken where the facts are known. Lower managers grow by deciding, which prepares them for senior posts. Top managers are free for the long view. Its risks are inconsistent decisions, duplicated effort, and a loss of control if lower managers pursue their own aims. The history at the end of this chapter shows an army that chose carefully between the two.

> Seven factors help decide the degree of centralisation.

Seven factors guide the choice. Size: large firms tend to decentralise, because the top cannot decide everything. Importance and cost of a decision: costly decisions tend to stay at the top. Need for uniformity: a brand that must look the same everywhere is held centrally. Skill of lower managers: skilled managers can be trusted with more. Speed of communication: fast systems let the top keep more. The environment: a changing or varied market favours local decisions. And history: a firm that grew from one owner often keeps the habit of deciding at the top.

> Three questions help measure how centralised a decision is.

How can we tell how centralised an organisation is? Three questions help. How many decisions are taken at the top, compared with lower down? How important are the decisions that lower managers may take, measured by the money or the risk involved? And how much checking must lower managers go through before they act: do they act freely, report afterward, or ask first? A firm in which lower managers may spend only small sums, and must ask first, is more centralised than one in which they may spend large sums and report at the end of the month.

| Point | Centralised | Decentralised |

| Consistency | high | may differ between units |
| Use of local knowledge | low | high |
| Load on top managers | heavy | lighter |

> The table gives the points side by side.

The table above gives the points side by side. Read each row as one aspect of a decision and the two ways it can be handled. No row favours one design in every case, which is why the choice must be made with the situation in view.

> A chain of shops shows why the choice differs by decision.

Consider a chain of four bakeries. The chain wants every shop to sell loaves at the same price, so price is set at the head office; this is centralisation, for the sake of uniformity. Each shop manager, however, chooses the number of loaves to bake each morning, because only the manager knows the weather and the local events; this is decentralisation, for the sake of local knowledge. The chain is therefore both centralised and decentralised, according to the decision. This is the usual case, and it explains why the question should always be asked about a particular decision.

> Departmentalisation affects the degree of centralisation.

The basis of grouping from Chapter 9 also matters. A firm grouped by product or territory usually gives each group its own manager and so tends to be more decentralised. A firm grouped by function tends to be more centralised, because the functions must be joined at the top. A firm may centralise some decisions, such as pricing or accounts, and decentralise others, such as local buying. The question is asked decision by decision.

> Two firms with the same size may choose different degrees of centralisation.

Size alone does not decide the degree. Consider two firms of equal size. One sells a standard product through shops that must look alike, and so holds most decisions at the centre. The other sells custom work, in which each customer's needs differ, and so gives each team wide freedom. Their sizes are the same, yet their degrees differ, because the need for uniformity and the variety of the work differ. This is another reason to treat every factor listed above as one among several, and not to expect any single factor to settle the matter.

> Moving the level of decisions should be done in steps.

A firm that wishes to change the degree of centralisation should do so in steps. A firm that decentralises all at once may find that lower managers lack the skill or the information, and may then pull everything back to the top, which damages trust. A better path is to begin with decisions that are small and frequent, such as ordering routine supplies, and to widen the limit as lower managers prove their judgement. The bakery case follows this path: it begins with buying up to a stated sum, not with buying without limit.

> A recap of centralisation.

*Before moving on.* Centralisation keeps decision authority at the top, and decentralisation spreads it down. Delegation is one manager's act; decentralisation is a policy. Size, cost of the decision, need for uniformity, skill, communication, environment and history guide the degree.

## Worked Case: The Bakery at Month Ten

> The case applies the chapter's ideas to one growing business.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Month 10. *Situation.* Morning Loaf Bakery has eight employees in three departments, and the owner is the only person who buys ingredients and sets prices. The owner spends the early morning on small orders. The head baker, who knows the stock best, waits for approval each day. The owner also needs advice on records, because receipts are missing for some purchases.

> The question asks what authority to give, to whom, and where decisions should rest.

:case *Question.* What authority should the bakery give the head baker, what should it do about the records, and how many people should each manager direct?

> The reasoning applies delegation, the three authorities and span.

:case *Reasoning.* First, delegation. The owner assigns the head baker the duty of keeping the kitchen supplied. The owner grants authority to buy ingredients up to Tk 5,000 per order. At Month 4 prices, a day's flour is 60 kg × Tk 69 = Tk 4,140, which is within the limit; two days' flour is Tk 8,280, which needs the owner's approval. The head baker answers to the owner for supply, and the owner follows up weekly. The owner stays accountable to customers and lenders for the whole business. Second, the three authorities. The owner engages a part-time bookkeeper at Tk 8,000 a month. The bookkeeper is a staff role: an adviser who does not direct the bakers. The owner also gives the bookkeeper functional authority to require receipts from every employee who spends money. The bookkeeper may not tell the bakers how to bake.

> The reasoning continues with span and centralisation.

:case *Reasoning, continued.* Third, span. The owner directs the head baker, the counter lead and the rider, a span of 3. The head baker directs two bakers and a helper, a span of 3. The counter lead directs two counter employees, a span of 2. The structure is flat, with three levels, and the spans are small enough for close help. Fourth, centralisation. Price setting stays with the owner, because prices must be the same at the counter and in delivery, and because they decide profit. Buying within the limit moves to the head baker, who knows the facts. The bakery is therefore partly decentralised.

> The conclusion and the check complete the case.

:case *Conclusion.* The head baker holds delegated authority to buy up to Tk 5,000 per order; the bookkeeper advises and holds functional authority over receipts; spans are 3, 3 and 2; price setting is central and routine buying is delegated.

> The check counts the people and tests each rule.

:case *Check.* The head baker's authority matches her duty to keep the kitchen supplied. The owner stays accountable. The spans cover 3 + 3 + 2 = 8 employees, which matches the employees counted in Chapter 9, and the part-time bookkeeper is outside the chain of command. Each employee still has one manager.

## Summary

> A summary of the chapter.

Authority is the right to give orders and use resources; responsibility is the duty to do an assigned task; accountability is answering for the result, and it stays with the one who delegates. Authority should match responsibility. Line authority commands, staff authority advises, and functional authority rules on one subject across departments. Delegation passes authority and duty down in four steps, and its obstacles lie with managers, subordinates and the organisation. Span of management sets the number of levels, and the right span depends on the work, the people and the situation. Centralisation and decentralisation are the ends of a scale, and the right degree is chosen decision by decision.

## Think It Through

> Responsibility cannot be passed away completely, and the reason matters.

A restaurant owner tells the head cook to buy fish each morning, with a limit of Tk 10,000. One day the cook buys fish that is not fresh, and several customers fall ill. The owner says, "I delegated it, so it is the cook's problem." Is the owner right? Consider who customers will blame, what the owner can still do, and what the owner must have done before the day. Why can responsibility never be fully delegated, and what part of it can be?

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake: authority and power are the same.

*"Authority and power are the same."* Authority is a right that comes from a position. Power is the ability to make others act, and it may come from knowledge or respect. A person can have either without the other.

> Mistake: delegating a task also delegates accountability.

*"If I delegate, I am no longer answerable."* The subordinate becomes responsible for the task, but the manager remains accountable for the result.

> Mistake: staff may give orders to the line.

*"Advisers may command."* Staff authority is the right to advise only. Functional authority gives a specialist a rule on one subject, not general command.

> Mistake: a larger span is always better.

*"A wider span is always more efficient."* A wide span saves managers but can leave subordinates without help. The right span depends on the factors above.

> Mistake: decentralisation means no control.

*"Decentralisation means giving up control."* Decentralised firms usually keep control of results, standards and key decisions, and give freedom over the means.

> Mistake: delegation and decentralisation are the same.

*"Delegation and decentralisation are the same thing."* Delegation is one manager's act with one subordinate; decentralisation is a lasting policy of the whole structure.

## In History: Mission Orders in the Prussian Army

> A famous army found a way to centralise the aim and decentralise the means.

In the nineteenth century the Prussian army faced a problem that every large organisation knows. Armies had grown too large, and battles too fast, for one commander to direct each unit. Helmuth von Moltke the Elder, chief of the Prussian General Staff from 1857 to 1888, taught his officers a different method.^3 A commander stated the aim of a task and its limits, and left the choice of means to the officer on the spot.

> The method gave subordinates freedom within a clear aim.

The method kept the aim central and made the means local. The officer who knew the ground decided how to reach the aim, and was expected to act without waiting for orders when conditions changed. It required officers who were well trained, trusted and answerable for results. Prussian forces won decisive wars in 1866 and in 1870 to 1871, and later armies copied the idea of mission orders.

> The method had a risk, and the army took steps to control it.

The method carried a risk, which the army knew. Officers who act on their own may pull in different directions. The army met the risk in three ways. It trained its officers in the same ideas, so that independent decisions were likely to agree. It required every order to state the aim, so that an officer who acted differently still served the aim. And it judged officers by the result against the aim, not by the exact steps they took. In the language of this chapter, the aim was centralised, the means were decentralised, and training and a shared aim served as the means of coordination from Chapter 9.

> The story illustrates delegation, accountability and decentralisation together.

The story shows several of this chapter's ideas at once. The commander delegated authority and duty but stayed accountable for the campaign. Authority matched responsibility. Training and trust removed the obstacles to delegation. And decisions were decentralised where the facts were known, while the aim stayed central.

## Sources

> Fayol, 1916, first set out authority and responsibility as a pair.

Fayol, Henri (1916), *Administration industrielle et générale*, which stated that authority and responsibility belong together and that each person should have one manager. It is the origin of the pair as management teaching.

> Barnard, 1938, treated authority as dependent on acceptance.

Barnard, Chester I. (1938), *The Functions of the Executive*, which argued that authority rests on the subordinate's acceptance of the order. It changed how writers think about the source of authority.

Weber, Max, for authority as a right attached to a position, in the work cited in Chapter 9. For the Prussian method, histories of the General Staff and of Moltke's writings.
