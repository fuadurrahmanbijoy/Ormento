## A Question Before We Begin

> One woman, ten loaves, and no questions to ask.

Imagine a woman in a quiet street in Dhaka who bakes ten loaves each morning in her own kitchen. She buys the flour, mixes the dough, bakes the bread and sells it to her neighbours at the door. Nobody asks who decides what is baked, or when, or at what price. She does. Every decision is hers, and every task is hers, so the question never arises.

> Two years later, the bread sells so well that she needs help.

Two years later her bread is known across the district. Each morning she must now produce two hundred loaves. One oven and one pair of hands cannot do it, so she hires four helpers. At once, new questions appear. Who mixes the dough, and who bakes? Who stands at the counter? Who buys the flour, and who pays for it? What happens when the oven fails at five o'clock in the morning?

> The recipe has not changed; the work of arranging the people has.

The loaves are the same, and so is the recipe. What has changed cannot be baked or sold. It is the work of deciding, arranging and checking, so that five people reach one result. This work has a name, and the people who do it have a name. Both are the subject of this chapter.

> The chapter asks four plain questions, one after another.

We ask four plain questions. Why do people join together to work? What is the work of guiding such a group? Why does that work matter? And who does it, using what? By the end, you will be able to answer each one in your own words.

> This chapter needs nothing from earlier pages.

*You will need:* nothing. This is the first chapter, and it starts from everyday life. Each idea is built from things you have already seen: a household, a school club, a market stall.

## 1.1 Why People Form Organisations

> Some tasks cannot be done alone, however hard one tries.

Start with a heavy wooden table in one room that must move to another. Alone, you can push one end a few centimetres. With a friend, you lift it together and carry it through the door. Two people have done what neither could do alone. This small fact explains why groups of people work together, and it explains the whole of this chapter.

@! Go slowly here.
> A family is the first group most people belong to.

Now think of a household. A family has one shared aim: to eat well, to pay the rent and to send the children to school. One person earns, another shops, a third cooks. Each month they talk about money and agree what to spend. Nobody calls this a business. Yet it already contains the parts we will meet again and again: an aim that everyone shares, several people, a part for each person, and some agreement about who does what.

@ Goal | A result someone wants to reach. Example: the rent paid by the first of the month.
> A goal is a result that people want to reach.

We give the shared aim a name. A **goal** means a result that someone wants to reach. The family's goal may be to pay the rent by the first of the month. A baker's goal may be two hundred loaves by seven o'clock. A goal always points to something that does not exist yet and that people wish to bring about.

@ Resource | Anything a group uses to do its work. Example: money, tools, flour, time, skill.
> A resource is anything a group uses to do its work.

People also pool what they have. The family's wages, the kitchen, the food in the cupboard and the cook's knowledge are all used to reach the goal. Each such thing is a **resource**. A resource means anything that a group uses to do its work. Money is a resource. So are time, tools, materials and skill. Section 1.4 shows the main kinds in more detail.

@ Organisation | Word in use. In daily life, "she is well organised" means tidy. Here, a group working for shared goals.
> A group with a shared goal is an organisation.

A household is a small group, but the idea reaches much further. An **organisation** means a group of people working together for shared goals. Our family is one example. A school football club is another. Suppose fifteen students form a club. Their goal is to play matches and win some. Their resources are a ball, a field, an hour after lessons and the permission of a teacher. Each student plays a position, and the club agrees who plays where.^1

@! Go slowly here.
> Three tests tell an organisation from a mere crowd.

How can you tell an organisation from other groups? Three tests help. First, the people share a goal. Second, they work together, and not only side by side. Third, there is some agreement about who does which part. Consider a crowd waiting at a bus stop. The people stand in the same place, but each has a separate aim. They do not work together, and nobody has agreed a part for anyone. A crowd is not an organisation. A football team is.

> People gain three things by joining a group.

Why do people form organisations? Look back at the heavy table. The first reason is strength: a group can do what one person cannot. The second reason is skill. In a group, each person can do the part that suits them best, and the work improves. The third reason is the pooling of resources. One baker cannot buy a large oven, but five people who share the cost can. In each case, the group reaches a goal that the individual cannot reach alone.

> A group without agreed parts can fail even when everyone is willing.

Suppose five neighbours agree to cook for a wedding feast. Each is willing, and each has a pot. None of them agrees who cooks what. By evening, there is too much rice, there is no water, and nobody has bought onions. The goal was shared and the people were willing. The third test, an agreement on who does which part, was missing. The group wasted much of its effort. This is why a group of any size soon needs someone to settle who does what.

> Each person in a group also has a private goal.

People in an organisation also keep goals of their own. A baker wishes to earn wages and to learn the trade. The owner wishes to earn a living and to build the shop. A customer wishes for fresh bread at a fair price. These goals are not the same as the shop's goal, and they may not agree with each other. Part of the work of guiding a group is to make the shop's goal and the people's own goals meet, so that people are glad to work toward it.

> Who belongs to an organisation can sometimes be uncertain.

The edge of an organisation is not always sharp. A baker who works every day plainly belongs to the bakery. A supplier who delivers flour three times a week works closely with it but belongs to another organisation. Customers deal with the bakery daily, yet they are outside it. For this chapter, the organisation means the people who work together inside it for its goals. Chapter IV returns to those outside.

> Organisations are everywhere, and not only in business.

Organisations are everywhere. Families, school clubs, mosque committees, hospitals, shops, factories and government offices are all organisations. Some, like a shop, work to earn money. Others, like a school or a charity, work for different goals. This difference matters little for our purpose. Wherever a group of people works for a shared goal with shared resources, the same questions arise. The ideas in this book apply to all of them.

> The bakery from the opening page fits all three tests.

Return to the bakery from the opening page. The goal is two hundred loaves each morning. The people are the owner and four helpers. The resources are money, flour, an oven and the owner's recipe. Each person has a part. The bakery passes all three tests, so it is an organisation. It also faces a new problem: the group is large enough that nobody can carry everything in one head.

> Before moving on: a group with a shared goal is an organisation.

*Before moving on.* A goal is a result people want. A resource is anything they use to reach it. An organisation is a group that works together for a shared goal. A crowd at a bus stop shares a place but not a goal, so it is not an organisation.

> You should now be able to say what an organisation is.

*You should now be able to say:* "An organisation is a group of people who work together, with agreed parts, for goals they share."

> Test yourself before the next section.

*Pause and check.* A group of passengers sits on the same bus. Do they form an organisation? Then consider the bus crew: a driver and a conductor who share the aim of reaching the terminus on time. Does the crew pass the three tests? Give a reason for each answer.

## 1.2 What Management Is

> At five o'clock, two bakers, one oven, and one question.

Picture the bakery at five in the morning. Two bakers have arrived. There is one oven, and each baker wants to use it first. One has the largest order. The other needs the oven for a short bake and has come early. The goal is clear, the people are willing and the flour is ready. Still, the bread will not be baked until someone decides who goes first.

> Willing people and resources are not enough on their own.

This small scene shows a large truth. A goal, people and resources do not by themselves produce results. Someone must decide, arrange and check. If nobody does, willing people may waste time, resources may be wasted and the goal may be missed. The work of deciding, arranging and checking is the work we are about to name.

### The Meaning of Management

@ Management | Getting things done through people and resources to reach goals.
> Management is getting things done through people and resources.

The name for this work is **management**. Management means getting things done through people and resources to reach goals. This one sentence has four parts, and each part matters. We take them one at a time.

> The first part: management is about results, not about effort alone.

*Getting things done.* Management is about results. A manager who works very hard but reaches no goal has not managed well. The test is what was done, not how busy people were. At the bakery, the test is whether the loaves are baked, sold and paid for.

> The second part: managers work through other people.

*Through people.* A manager does not do everything alone. A manager gets results by working with and through others. The bakery owner who bakes every loaf personally is working as a baker. The owner who arranges the work of five people so that two hundred loaves are ready is managing.

> The third part: people are not the only thing a manager uses.

*And resources.* People need money, flour, an oven and a recipe. A manager must also see that these are bought, kept safe and used well. Managing people while wasting materials still fails.

> The fourth part: all of this is done for goals, not for its own sake.

*To reach goals.* The goal gives the work its direction. Without a goal, there is nothing to decide, because every choice would be as good as any other. Management always points toward a result that people have agreed to seek.

@ Manager | A person responsible for the work of others.
> A manager is the person who answers for others' work.

We now name the people who do this work. A **manager** means a person who is responsible for the work of others. Responsible here means that the person must see that the work is done and must answer if it is not. A manager of a shop answers for the shop's workers and its results. A school head answers for the school.^2

> Three ideas often get mixed: doing, owning, managing.

Three ideas are often mixed, so we keep them apart. A person may do the work, own the business or manage the work. The same person can do all three, as in the one-woman kitchen. In a larger firm, the three are often separate. The table sets out the difference, starting with the question each role answers.

:table Three roles that are often confused
| Role | The question it answers | Bakery example |
| Worker | What do I make or do today? | A baker shapes the loaves |
| Owner | Whose money started the business, and who receives its gains? | The person whose savings paid for the oven |
| Manager | Who sees that the work reaches its goal? | The person who decides tomorrow's order and the work of each helper |

> The table shows that one person may play one, two or all three roles.

Read the table row by row. A worker answers a daily question about tasks. An owner answers a question about money and gains. A manager answers a question about results through others. An owner who hires a manager and goes home has stopped managing but still owns. A manager who owns nothing, such as an employee hired to run a shop, still manages. The roles overlap, but they are not the same.

> The figure shows the order in which these three ideas arise.

Figure 1 shows the order in which the three ideas arise. A goal calls for an organisation, because one person cannot reach it alone. An organisation calls for a manager, because a group of people needs someone who answers for the work. Read it from left to right, and notice that the bakery example appears under each box.

> A morning at the bakery shows the four parts together.

Return to the bakery at five in the morning. The owner sees that only one of the two bakers has arrived. The owner decides that the counter worker will help with shaping the dough until seven. This is *getting things done*. The owner explains the change to each person, so the work goes *through people*. The owner checks that there is enough flour for two hundred loaves, which is care of *resources*. And the owner keeps one result in mind: bread on the shelf when the doors open at seven, which is the *goal*. In a few minutes, all four parts of the definition were at work.

> The word has three uses, but this book keeps to one.

The word management is used in three ways. It can name the activity of getting things done. It can name the group of people who do that work, as in the phrase "the management has decided". It can name a field of study, as in "she studies management". To keep one word to one meaning, this book uses management only for the activity and for the field of study. For the people, it says *managers*. Some books use the word *administration* for the same activity. This book uses management throughout.

### Management in Everyday Life

> A school fair shows management at work outside business.

Management is not found only in business. Suppose a school plans a fair to raise money for a new library. The goal is Tk 100,000 in one day. The people are thirty students and five teachers. The resources are the school hall, some tables, a small amount of money and the time of everyone involved. One teacher arranges all of this. She decides which class runs the food stalls and which runs the games. She sees that the tables arrive before the doors open. At the end of the day, she asks how much has been raised. She sells nothing herself, yet she is a manager.

> Each part of the definition can be found in this example.

Look at the four parts of the definition in turn. She *gets things done*: the tables, stalls and games are ready by nine. She works *through people*: she does not run every stall herself. She watches the *resources*: the hall, the tables and the money. And she works for a *goal*: the library fund. If one part is missing, the work suffers. Without a goal, the day has no purpose. Without people, nothing happens. Without resources, the plans stay on paper.

> The word "through" separates managing from doing the work oneself.

A note on the word *through*. It does not mean *over*. A manager does not stand above others and push them. A manager works through others in the sense that results arrive by means of their efforts. The teacher at the fair succeeds when the students succeed. Her own hands may sell nothing. This is why a good manager spends more time arranging, explaining and checking than doing the tasks that workers do.

> Management looks different from four different seats.

Management also looks different from different seats. The *owner* sees management as the protector of the money. The *manager* sees it as the daily work of making things happen. The *worker* sees it as the way that tasks, hours and pay are fixed. The *customer* does not see it at all, and sees only its results: bread that is fresh and a counter that is open. All four views are partly correct. A manager who understands all four is better placed to decide well.

### The Nature of Management

> Nature means the features that all management shares.

What is management like? Writers describe its **nature**, which means the main features that management shows wherever it is found. Six features are enough for a beginner. Each can be checked against the bakery.

> Management is always aimed at a goal.

Management is *goal-directed*. It exists to reach goals, and its quality is judged by whether they are reached. A manager who cannot say what the goal is cannot say whether the work is going well. In the bakery, the goal is bread that is baked on time, sold and paid for.

> Management is found in every kind of organisation.

Management is *universal*. A school, a hospital, a cricket club, a charity and a government office all need people who answer for the work of others. The details differ, but the need does not. A shop with one worker and a hospital with one thousand both need it, though in different amounts.

> Management never finishes; one task leads to the next.

Management is *continuing*. It is not done once and then put aside. Each day brings new decisions: what to order, who does what, whether yesterday's results were good. The work is made of several kinds of activity that repeat in a cycle. Chapter II names them: planning, organising, leading and controlling.

> Management always involves dealing with people.

Management is *people-centred*. Machines and money matter, but the manager's central task is to work with people who have their own needs, fears and abilities. Two bakers with the same oven may produce different results because of how they feel about their work. A manager who forgets this will meet surprises.

> Management is shared by a group of managers.

Management is a *group activity*. In a large firm, many managers do the work together, each in a different place and on a different part of the task. No single person manages a whole hospital. Section 1.5 shows how these managers are arranged.

> Management cannot be seen directly, but its results can.

Management is *intangible*. You cannot hold management in your hand, as you can hold a loaf. You see it in what it produces: bread that is ready, workers who know their tasks, a shop that runs without confusion. When management is poor, you also see the results: delays, waste and disorder.

> Chapter III asks whether management is a science or an art.

Writers also ask whether management is a science, an art or a profession. That question is taken up in Chapter III, after you have seen what managers do.

### The Purpose of Management

> Purpose means why management exists at all.

Why does management exist? Its **purpose** means the reason it is done, the result it is for. Four purposes can be named. They overlap, but each adds something.

> The first purpose: to reach the group's goals.

The first purpose is to *reach the goals of the organisation*. This is the main one. All other purposes serve it. A bakery that does not bake its bread has failed in the one thing it was formed to do.

> The second purpose: to use resources well and avoid waste.

The second is to *use resources well*. Money, time and flour are limited. A manager must see that they are used with little waste. A bakery that throws away one loaf in ten wastes money, flour and the baker's work. Chapter II gives precise measures for this.

> The third purpose: to keep people working together.

The third is to *keep people working together*. People can pull in different directions, and good intentions do not prevent it. If the counter seller promises delivery by noon and the baker bakes by two, both worked hard and the customer is still disappointed. Management links the parts so that they fit.

> The fourth purpose: to help the group adapt to change.

The fourth is to *help the group meet change*. Prices move, customers change their tastes, and rules are altered. An organisation that cannot adjust will lose ground. Chapter IV shows how outside forces reach inside an organisation and how managers respond.

> Management is not the same as owning, ordering or leading.

Three cautions end this section. Management is not *ownership*: the table showed that a person can own without managing, and manage without owning. Management is not only *giving orders*: orders are one tool among many. And management is not the same as *leadership*, which means influencing people toward a goal; Chapter XII compares the two carefully. Keeping these ideas apart will save you from many confusions later.

> Before moving on: management is getting results through people and resources.

*Before moving on.* Management means getting things done through people and resources to reach goals. A manager is the person responsible for the work of others. Management is goal-directed, universal, continuing, people-centred, a group activity and intangible. Its purposes are to reach goals, use resources well, keep people working together and meet change.

> You should now be able to define management in your own words.

*You should now be able to say:* "Management is the work of getting results through other people and the resources they use, so that the group reaches its goals."

## 1.3 Why Management Matters

> Two bakeries with the same oven can have very different results.

Consider two bakeries on opposite sides of the same road. Each has one oven, the same flour supplier and the same price for bread. One makes a steady living and its customers return. The other is often late, has no flour on Fridays and loses its helpers within months. The ovens are equal and the flour is equal. What differs is the way the work is guided. This is the first reason management matters.

> Reason one: groups with good management reach their goals more often.

Management matters first because it decides whether goals are reached. Good intentions, hard work and good equipment are all needed, but none is enough alone. Management joins them. Where it is weak, the other three are often wasted. Where it is strong, even modest resources can produce good results.

> Reason two: resources are limited, and management reduces waste.

It matters second because resources are limited. Money runs out, time passes and flour spoils. Every organisation must choose what to do with what it has. A manager who spends carefully can do more with the same resources than one who does not. Over a year, a small saving each day becomes a large one.

> Reason three: management makes the parts of the group fit together.

It matters third because people and tasks must fit. A baker, a seller and a rider each do a different part. If the parts do not meet at the right time, the customer sees only the failure. Management sets who does what and when, so that the parts join into one result.

> Reason four: management helps a group meet change.

It matters fourth because the world changes. A new competitor opens nearby. The price of flour rises. A rule is altered. Someone must notice these changes and decide how the organisation will respond. Without management, an organisation tends to continue as before until the change harms it.

> Reason five: society benefits when organisations work well.

Last, it matters beyond the organisation. A well-managed firm gives its workers steady pay, gives its customers goods they can trust and pays what it owes to the government. A poorly managed firm may fail, leaving workers without jobs and suppliers unpaid. A country in which many organisations are well managed is usually a country in which many people live better.

> Poor management shows itself in everyday signs.

Poor management is easy to recognise by its signs. Customers wait too long. Workers are unsure what is asked of them and ask the same question several times. Materials are bought twice or not at all. The same mistake repeats month after month. Good management shows itself in the opposite way: tasks are clear, work flows and problems are met early and not late. Few of these signs need a report. A visitor to a shop often feels them within ten minutes.

> Management matters differently in small and large organisations.

Management matters in small and in large organisations, but it shows itself differently. In a small shop, the owner sees everything and may repair a mistake within the hour. In a large hospital, no single person sees everything. Many managers must pass information to one another, or an error in one ward may go unnoticed for days. The larger the organisation, the more its results depend on the quality of its management.

> Management matters in hospitals and schools as much as in shops.

The same is true outside business. In a hospital, poor management can mean that a patient waits hours for a bed or that a medicine is not in the ward when it is needed. In a school, it can mean that a class has no teacher for a week. The cost is then not lost money but lost health or lost learning. Wherever people depend on an organisation, its management matters to them.

@! Go slowly here.
> A manager is answerable to several groups at once.

A manager's work also carries duties. Managers act for other people. They spend money that others have given, they direct the work of employees, they serve customers and they operate within the law. For this reason, a manager is answerable to several groups. We now give a name to this idea.

@ Accountability | Being answerable for results.
> Accountability means being answerable for results.

**Accountability** means being answerable for results. A person who is accountable must be able to explain what was done and what happened, and must accept the consequences. If a bakery's loaves are always late, the manager cannot say, "The bakers did that." The manager is accountable for the work of the bakers. Chapter X explains how this idea works when authority is passed down to others.

> Managers owe different things to different groups.

Which groups does a manager answer to? Four are enough to begin with. To the *owners*, a manager owes careful use of their money and honest reports on the results. To the *employees*, a manager owes fair treatment, safe conditions and clear instructions. To the *customers*, a manager owes goods of the quality that was promised and at an honest price. To the *public*, a manager owes obedience to the law and care for the people and places the work touches.

> These duties can pull against each other; managers must weigh them.

These duties do not always agree. A lower price pleases customers but may reduce the owner's gains. A higher wage helps employees but raises costs. A manager's responsibility is to weigh these claims fairly and to explain the choice. Chapter V returns to this question and treats the firm's duties to society in full. The groups that depend on an organisation, and are affected by it, also receive a precise name in Chapter IV.

> Management cannot guarantee success; it only improves the chances.

A caution is needed. Good management does not guarantee success. A well-managed bakery may still fail if a flood closes the road, or if a rival opens next door with lower prices. Good management also cannot create resources that do not exist: it cannot bake without flour. What management does is improve the chances. Managers who think carefully, arrange the work well and check the results will, over time, do better than those who do not.

> Before moving on: management matters for results, resources, fit, change and society.

*Before moving on.* Management matters because it brings goals within reach, reduces waste, makes the parts fit together, helps a group meet change and benefits society. A manager is accountable, which means answerable, to owners, employees, customers and the public.

> You should now be able to give reasons why management is important.

*You should now be able to say:* "Management matters because good intentions and good equipment are wasted without someone who joins them toward a goal, and because managers answer to many groups."

## 1.4 The Resources Managers Work With

> Everything a manager uses falls into five kinds.

A manager does not work in empty space. Every day, a manager uses things: people to do the work, money to pay for it, flour and tools to make the product, and knowledge to guide the whole. We have already met the word resource in section 1.1. It means anything an organisation uses to do its work. Now we divide resources into five kinds.

> People are the first resource and the most important.

*People.* The first resource is the people who work in the organisation: their time, their skill, their strength and their ideas. People are different from the other four kinds, because they have wishes of their own. Money does not leave the bank because it is tired, but a worker may. At the bakery, the people are the owner, the bakers, the counter worker and the rider.

> Money is the second resource: it buys the others.

*Money.* The second resource is money. It buys materials, pays wages, covers rent and keeps the organisation running while it waits for its own income. Money may come from the owner's savings, from loans or from sales. Without enough of it, the other resources cannot be obtained or kept.

> Materials are the third resource: what the product is made of.

*Materials.* The third is the raw stuff that the organisation turns into something else. For a bakery, these are flour, yeast, salt, sugar and the bags in which bread is sold. Materials are used up in the work, so they must be bought again and again. A shortage stops the work at once.

> Equipment is the fourth resource: what is used again and again.

*Equipment.* The fourth is the tools, machines and buildings that serve the work over a long period, not a single day. The bakery's equipment includes the oven, the mixer and the shop premises. Unlike flour, equipment is not used up in a single batch, though it wears and needs repair. It costs money to buy, and it often limits how much an organisation can produce.

> Information is the fifth resource: knowledge needed to decide.

*Information.* The fifth is what people know and can find out: orders received, prices, recipes, the customer's address, yesterday's sales. A manager decides on the basis of information, so poor information leads to poor decisions. At the bakery, the order sheet and the price list are information. So is the owner's knowledge of which loaves sell best on Friday.

:fig The five resources a manager works with, with a bakery example for each. | ch1_fig2.svg

> The figure places the manager among the five resources.

Figure 2 places the manager in the centre. Each line shows one resource flowing to the manager's hands. The manager's work is to bring these five together in the right amounts and at the right time. The bakery examples in each box show that every organisation uses all five, though in different amounts.

> The five kinds are separate, but they depend on each other.

The five kinds depend on each other. Money buys materials and equipment, and pays people. People use equipment and materials, and produce goods that bring money. Information tells the manager which of the other four is short. A shortage of any one can stop the work, which is why a manager watches all five. Now we can say the definition of management with the word *resources* in its place: management is getting things done through people and other resources to reach goals.

> One resource can sometimes replace another.

Resources can sometimes replace one another. Money can buy time, as when a bakery pays for a faster oven. A machine can replace the work of several people. A better price list, which is information, can save materials by showing which loaves waste the most. A manager's skill lies partly in seeing which exchanges are worthwhile. Each exchange has a cost, and the cost must be weighed against the gain.

> The mix of resources changes as an organisation grows.

The mix of resources changes as an organisation grows. A one-woman kitchen depends mostly on one person's skill and a small amount of money. A shop with five workers needs much more information, because several people must know what is ordered and what is done. A large factory depends heavily on equipment and on money, and the handling of information becomes the task of a whole department. A manager must therefore ask, from time to time, which resource is now the scarcest.

> People are different from the other resources in one important way.

Return to the first resource for a moment. Flour obeys the manager's decisions, and so does the oven. People respond with their own thoughts. A baker who feels treated unfairly may work slowly, and a baker who feels trusted may suggest a better method. This fact is the reason that Chapters XI and XII study motivation and leadership. A manager who treats people as if they were flour will lose the benefits that only people can give.

> Resources are limited, so a manager must choose how to use them.

Resources are always limited. A bakery cannot buy unlimited flour, hire unlimited bakers or run an oven twenty-five hours a day. A manager must decide which use of each resource brings the best result. This is one reason why Chapter II measures how well resources are used.

> Before moving on: five resources, each needed, none unlimited.

*Before moving on.* The five resources are people, money, materials, equipment and information. They depend on each other, and they are limited.

> You should now be able to name and describe the five resources.

*You should now be able to say:* "A manager works with people, money, materials, equipment and information, and must use them well because they are limited."

## 1.5 Levels and Kinds of Managers

> Some managers decide for the whole, others for a small team.

In a large organisation, many people carry the title of manager. A bank may have a managing director, a branch manager and a counter supervisor. They are all managers, but their work is not the same. To see how they differ, we sort managers in two ways: by *level*, which means how far they are from the daily work, and by *kind*, which means what they direct.

@! Go slowly here.
> Level means a manager's distance from the daily work.

Start with level. Imagine the organisation as a stack, with the people who do the daily work at the base. Managers stand above them in steps. Those on the lowest step are close to the daily work. Those on the highest step are far from it, and look at the organisation as a whole. Most organisations have three steps.

@ Top manager | A manager who directs the whole organisation.
> Top managers direct the whole organisation.

At the highest step are the **top managers**. A top manager is a manager who directs the whole organisation. Typical titles are chief executive, managing director and chairman. Top managers decide what the organisation will try to do over the coming years, which markets to enter and how much money to spend. They also speak for the organisation to the public and to the government. They see little of the daily work, but their decisions shape it.

@ Middle manager | A manager who runs a department or branch and answers to top managers.
> Middle managers convert direction into department work.

Below them are the **middle managers**. A middle manager is a manager who runs a department or a branch and reports upward to a top manager. A bank's branch manager and a factory's production manager are typical examples. Middle managers take the direction given by the top and convert it into work for their departments. They also pass information back up. They stand between those who set the direction and those who do the daily work.

@ First-line manager | A manager who directly guides the daily work of workers.
> First-line managers guide the daily work of workers.

At the lowest step are the **first-line managers**. A first-line manager is a manager who guides the daily work of workers who manage no one. Common titles are supervisor, team leader and head baker. They explain tasks, check quality, solve small problems on the spot and report to the middle manager. They are closest to the work and to the people who do it.^3

> Figure 3 shows the three steps above the workers.

Figure 3 shows the three steps above the workers. Notice that the workers themselves do not appear as a manager level. They do the work and manage no one. Notice too that the stack narrows toward the top. A large organisation has many first-line managers, fewer middle managers and fewer still at the top. Small organisations may have one person standing on all three steps.

> The table compares the three levels by their main concern.

The table compares the three levels. Read it across one row at a time: each row describes one level, its main concern, how far ahead it looks and the titles it commonly carries. The titles are examples; organisations use many different names for the same work.

:table The three levels of managers compared
| Level | Main concern | Looks ahead | Common titles |
| Top | Direction of the whole organisation | Years | Managing director, chief executive |
| Middle | Turning direction into work for a department | Months | Branch manager, department head |
| First-line | Daily work of the workers | Days and weeks | Supervisor, team leader |

> The levels differ in concern and time, not in importance.

The levels differ in concern and in how far ahead they look. They do not differ in importance. An organisation with excellent top managers and poor first-line managers will fail at the counter, where customers meet it. An organisation with excellent first-line managers and no clear direction will work hard and achieve little. All three levels are needed, and they depend on each other.

> Each level makes different kinds of decisions.

An example of decisions makes the levels concrete. In a chain of bakeries, a top manager decides whether to open ten new shops next year and how to find the money. A middle manager decides how many loaves each shop will bake next month and which shop needs a new oven. A first-line manager decides who works at the counter on Friday and what to do when the mixer stops. The three kinds of decision differ in size and in time. A mistake at the top can affect every shop for years. A mistake at the first-line level is usually small and quick to repair, yet repeated small mistakes can damage the whole.

> Managers in hospitals and schools follow the same pattern.

The three levels are not found only in business. A hospital has a chief executive at the top. Heads of departments, such as surgery or nursing, stand in the middle. Ward supervisors guide the daily work. A school has a head teacher at the top, heads of departments in the middle, and senior teachers of each year group nearest to the daily work. The titles change from place to place, but the pattern of three steps is common.

@! Go slowly here.
> Kind means what a manager directs.

Now consider kind. Two managers on the same level may be in charge of very different things. A manager's *kind* depends on the area of work, not on the level. Two kinds are met most often.

@ Functional manager | A manager who runs one specialist area of an organisation.
> A functional manager runs one specialist area.

A **functional manager** is a manager who runs one specialist area of an organisation. Examples are a production manager, who runs the making of goods; a sales manager, who runs the selling; and a finance manager, who runs the handling of money. Each is expert in one area. A functional manager may stand at any level. The sales manager of a small firm and the head of sales of a large one are both functional managers.

@ General manager | A manager who runs a whole unit, with all its specialist areas.
> A general manager runs a whole unit and all its areas.

A **general manager** is a manager who runs a whole unit and all of its specialist areas. The manager of a hotel, for example, answers for rooms, food, accounts and employees together. The unit may be a whole firm or one branch of it. A general manager must see how the areas fit, so this kind of manager needs knowledge of many fields and not only one.

> The two kinds are not opposites; they depend on each other.

The two kinds work side by side. In a factory, a general manager runs the whole site, and the production, sales and finance managers each run one area under that manager. The general manager joins what the functional managers do. The functional managers bring the deep knowledge of each area that the general manager may lack.

> A title does not always show the level.

Titles can mislead. A person called "manager" in a small shop may be a first-line manager, and a person called "supervisor" in another firm may hold a larger role. To decide the level, ignore the title and ask three questions. Whom does this person direct? How far ahead must the person look? Does the person answer for a department or for the whole organisation? The answers show the level.

> Level and kind are separate ways of sorting the same manager.

Level and kind are separate ways of sorting. The same manager can be described by both. The table shows four examples from a chain of bakeries. In each row, the third column gives the level, and the fourth gives the kind.

| Job | What the person directs | Level | Kind |
| Managing director of a bakery chain | All the shops | Top | General |
| Head of production at the chain | The making of bread in all shops | Middle | Functional |
| Manager of one shop | That shop and all its sections | Middle | General |
| Head baker of one shop | The bakers on one shift | First-line | Functional |

> Read the table row by row to see why each job is placed as it is.

Read the table row by row. The managing director directs the whole chain, so the level is top and the kind is general. The head of production directs only the making of bread, so the kind is functional. In a chain with many shops, we place this job at the middle level. The shop manager directs one whole unit, so the kind is general, yet in the chain this is a middle role. The head baker directs a daily task, so the level is first-line and the kind is functional. The same word, manager, covers all four jobs.

> Most managers begin at the first-line level and move upward.

Most managers begin at the lowest level and move up. A skilled baker may become head baker, then manager of a shop, then head of production. At each step, the work changes. The head baker spends most of the day with workers. The shop manager spends more time on orders, money and the results of the whole shop. The head of production spends more time with other managers. A person who rises must learn to let others do the work that the person once did well. Chapter II shows which skills each level needs most.

> In a small firm, one person may be all of these at once.

A small organisation may have only one manager. The owner of a small bakery sets the direction, arranges the work of the departments and guides the daily work of the workers. That one person stands on all three levels at once. As the bakery grows, the owner will pass some of these duties to others. Later chapters follow that change.

> Before moving on: managers differ by level and by kind.

*Before moving on.* Managers are sorted by level into top, middle and first-line, and by kind into functional and general. Level shows distance from the daily work. Kind shows what the manager directs.

> You should now be able to place a manager by level and kind.

*You should now be able to say:* "A top manager directs the whole organisation, a middle manager runs a department, and a first-line manager guides daily work; a functional manager runs one area, and a general manager runs a whole unit."

> Test yourself before the next section.

*Pause and check.* A hospital has a chief executive, a head of surgery and a ward supervisor. Place each one by level. Which of them is most likely a functional manager?

## Worked Case: Morning Loaf Bakery

> The case file records the bakery's first month in plain facts.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Month 1. Morning Loaf Bakery is a single shop in Dhaka. Its owner opens it as the only owner, with start capital of Tk 600,000 from personal savings. The first oven cost Tk 150,000. Rent is Tk 40,000 a month. The shop trades 26 days a month. A loaf sells at Tk 70. Ingredients cost about Tk 24 a loaf, of which flour is 0.24 kg at Tk 60 a kilogram. Four workers are employed: two bakers, one counter worker and one rider. Output is 200 loaves a day, and the four workers together work 32 hours a day. The owner is the only manager.

### Question

> We ask which resources the shop uses and at which levels the owner manages.

The case asks us two things. First, which of the five resources does the shop use, and what is the evidence for each? Second, at which of the three levels of management does the owner work?

### Reasoning

> Step one: find each resource in the given facts.

*Step one: resources.* We take the five kinds in turn and look for a fact that matches each. *People:* the owner and four workers. *Money:* the start capital of Tk 600,000, of which Tk 150,000 is now held in the oven, and the rent of Tk 40,000 a month. *Materials:* flour and other ingredients. At 200 loaves a day and 0.24 kg a loaf, the shop uses 200 × 0.24 = 48 kg of flour a day. At Tk 60 a kilogram, that costs 48 × 60 = Tk 2,880 a day. *Equipment:* the oven and the shop itself. *Information:* the price of Tk 70 a loaf, the daily order list and the owner's knowledge of how much to bake.

> Step two: find the levels at which the owner works.

*Step two: levels.* We ask what the owner does and match each task to a level. When the owner decides to buy a second oven or to change the price, the owner is directing the whole shop. That is the top level. When the owner decides that the bakers start at four and the rider leaves at six, the owner is arranging the parts of the shop to fit. That is the middle level. When the owner shows a new baker how to shape loaves, the owner is guiding daily work. That is the first-line level.

### Conclusion

> The shop uses all five resources, and one person works at all three levels.

The shop uses all five kinds of resources. The owner, as the only manager, works at all three levels at once. This is common in a small organisation. As the shop grows, the owner will pass some of these levels to other people. Later chapters follow that growth month by month.

### Check

> A quick test confirms each answer against the facts.

To check the resources, we remove each one in turn and ask whether the shop could still make its bread. Without flour, no loaves are made. Without the oven, none is baked. Without workers, nobody makes or sells. Without money, none of these can be bought. Without information about orders and prices, the work has no direction. Each resource is therefore needed. To check the levels, we confirm that each of the three tasks above was a task done by the same person: the owner. The count of managers is one, and the count of levels is three.

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas, in short.

People form organisations because a group can do what one person cannot. An organisation is a group of people working together, with agreed parts, for shared goals. Management is getting things done through people and resources to reach those goals. A manager is the person responsible for the work of others. Management is goal-directed, universal, continuing, people-centred, a group activity and intangible. Its purposes are to reach goals, use resources well, keep people working together and meet change.

> Management matters, and managers are accountable.

Management matters because it brings goals within reach, reduces waste, makes the parts fit, helps an organisation meet change and benefits society. Managers are accountable, which means answerable, to owners, employees, customers and the public. They work with five resources: people, money, materials, equipment and information. They stand on three levels, top, middle and first-line, and they are of two main kinds, functional and general.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: thinking that management means giving orders.

*"Management means giving orders."* Orders are one tool. Management is the whole work of getting results through people and resources, which includes deciding, arranging, explaining, listening and checking. A manager who only gives orders, and never asks whether the work is going well, is managing poorly.

> Mistake two: thinking that only businesses need management.

*"Only businesses and large firms need management."* Every organisation has goals, people and resources, so every organisation needs management. A school, a hospital, a mosque committee and a football club all have managers. Size changes the amount of management, not the need for it.

> Mistake three: thinking the owner is always the manager.

*"The owner is the manager."* Not always. Ownership concerns whose money is in the business. Management concerns who answers for the work. They may be the same person, as in a small shop, but they may also be different, as in a firm that employs a manager to run its branch.

> Mistake four: thinking only top managers are managers.

*"Only the people at the top are managers."* A first-line manager who guides ten workers is as much a manager as a chief executive. The levels differ in concern and in how far ahead they look. They do not differ in whether the person is a manager.

> Mistake five: judging a manager by how hard the manager works.

*"The hardest-working manager is the best."* A manager is judged by results, not by effort. A manager who works fourteen hours a day but whose bakery is always late has not managed well. A manager who arranges the work so that the bread is ready on time, with no one overworked, has.

## In History

> A shipyard in Venice shows goals, people and resources organised on a large scale.

Long before modern firms, an organisation in Venice showed what managing at scale could mean. The Arsenal was the state shipyard of the city. Its beginnings are traditionally dated to the year 1104.^4 Its goal was plain: to build and keep ready the ships on which Venice depended for trade and for defence. By the sixteenth century, many historians regard it as one of the largest industrial sites in Europe before the Industrial Revolution.

> The Arsenal divided the building of a ship into stages and used standard parts.

The Arsenal's resources included timber, rope, iron and the money of the state. Its people were thousands of skilled workers, known as the Arsenalotti. Some sources give a figure as high as sixteen thousand at its peak, around 1600. Ships were built in stages, with hulls moved from one work area to the next, and with standard parts made ahead of time and kept in store. When war threatened, an officer of the Arsenal could order more of these parts to be made. Each of these arrangements shows decisions made by people who answered for the result.

> Historians disagree on how modern the Arsenal's methods were.

Historians disagree on how closely the Arsenal resembled a modern factory. They also record that it had trouble with its workers, including a dispute over working hours in 1569. These facts do not weaken the lesson. They support it. A large goal, many people and valuable resources required people to decide and to answer for the outcome. Venice had managers long before the word management was in use.

> The Arsenal can be read with this chapter's ideas.

Read the Arsenal with the ideas of this chapter. It had a goal: ships. It was an organisation, with thousands of people working together. Its resources were of all five kinds: workers, money from the state, timber and iron, docks and tools, and knowledge of how to build a galley. Some officials directed the whole yard, others directed sections, and others guided small crews. The names differ from ours, but the three levels are recognisable. The idea of management is older than the word.
