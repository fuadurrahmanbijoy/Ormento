## A Question Before We Begin

> One morning in the bakery, written down entry by entry.

Suppose the owner of a small bakery keeps a diary of one morning. The entries read like this. At five o'clock, checks the flour in the store. At ten past five, decides that the counter worker will help with the dough. At twenty past, explains the change to both workers. At six, notes that the oven has been late for three days. At seven, writes down how many loaves are ready. At eight, thinks about next month's orders and decides to ask a supplier for a lower price.

> The owner never touched the dough, yet every entry was work.

Not one entry says that the owner shaped a loaf or served a customer. Yet nobody would say that the owner did nothing. The morning was full, and each entry was a real piece of work. This raises two questions. Can the many things a manager does be sorted into a few kinds? And, once we know what a manager does, how can we tell whether it was done well?

> The chapter answers both questions in turn.

The first question takes up the first two sections. We name four kinds of managerial work, and then we ask which skills a manager needs for them. The second question takes up the last two sections. We learn a ratio that measures how well resources are used, and we separate it from two ideas that are often confused with it.

> This chapter builds on the definition, resources and levels of Chapter I.

*You will need:* the meaning of management, the five resources, and the three levels of managers, all from Chapter I. If any of these is unclear, read the summary at the end of that chapter first.

## 2.1 The Management Process

> Four words cover everything in the owner's diary.

Return to the owner's diary. Read the entries again and ask what each is *for*. Checking the flour prepares for the day. Moving the counter worker arranges people. Explaining the change guides them. Noting the late oven checks a result. Asking the supplier for a lower price looks ahead. These five purposes, and a few more like them, can be gathered under four headings. Each heading is a function.

@ Function | A main kind of work that managers do. Example: checking results.
> A function is a main kind of work that managers do.

A **function** means a main kind of work that managers do. The word does not mean a party or a duty of the day. It names a group of activities that belong together because they have the same purpose. Management has four functions. We take them one at a time, and we go slowly, because they are the frame for the rest of the book.

@! Go slowly here.
@ Planning | Choosing goals and deciding how to reach them.
> Planning means choosing goals and the way to reach them.

The first function is **planning**. Planning means choosing goals and deciding how to reach them. It looks ahead. Before the bakery can bake, someone must decide how many loaves are wanted, when, and how they will be made. The owner who asks a supplier for a lower price next month is planning. A plan is the result: a chosen route from where the group is to where it wishes to be.

@ Organising | Arranging people, tasks and resources so that the work can be done.
> Organising means arranging people and resources for the work.

The second function is **organising**. Organising means arranging people, tasks and resources so that the work can be done. A goal and a plan are not enough if nobody knows who does what. The owner who moves the counter worker to the dough is organising. So is the owner who decides that bakers start at four and the rider leaves at six. The result is a pattern of tasks, people and tools that fits the plan.

@ Leading | Guiding and influencing people so that they work toward the goals.
> Leading means guiding people toward the goals.

The third function is **leading**. Leading means guiding and influencing people so that they work toward the goals. People are not machines that start at a signal. They need to understand what is asked, to be encouraged and sometimes to be corrected. The owner who explains the change to two workers is leading. Chapter XII studies this function in depth. For now, notice that it is the function most closely tied to people.

@ Controlling | Checking results against the goals and correcting what is wrong.
> Controlling means checking results and correcting them.

The fourth function is **controlling**. Controlling means checking results against the goals and correcting what is wrong. Without it, a manager would not know whether the plan was working. The owner who notes that the oven has been late three days running is controlling. So is the owner who counts the loaves at seven. Controlling asks the same question every time: are we where we meant to be?

> The four functions are the same wherever management is found.

These four functions are not special to bakeries. A hospital head plans next year's beds, organises the wards, leads the nurses and controls the cost of medicines. A school head does the same for classrooms and teachers. The four words describe what managers do in every organisation, at every level. Only the details change.

@ Management process | The continuing cycle of the four functions.
> The functions form a continuing cycle called the management process.

The functions do not stand apart. Together they form a cycle. The **management process** means the continuing cycle of the four functions. Planning leads to organising, organising leads to leading, leading leads to controlling, and controlling leads back to planning, because what the manager learns from checking changes the next plan. Figure 4 shows this.

> Figure 4 shows each function leading to the next, and the last leading back.

Look at Figure 4 and follow the arrows. They run clockwise from planning. The arrow from controlling back to planning is the most important one. It shows that management does not finish. A manager who checks results and finds a gap does not stop. The finding becomes the starting point of the next plan, and the cycle turns again.

> The order is a teaching order, because later functions depend on earlier ones.

Why do we place planning first? Because the other functions depend on it. You cannot arrange people for work until you know what the work is. You cannot guide people toward a goal until the goal is chosen. You cannot check results without something to check them against. In this sense the order is logical. Each function needs the one before it.

> In real life, a manager moves between the functions many times a day.

In real life, the order is not strict. The owner does not plan for an hour, then organise for an hour, and so on. In one morning the owner may plan, organise, lead and control within ten minutes, and then repeat the sequence on a different subject. The four functions overlap. A single conversation, such as the owner's talk with the two workers, may contain organising, leading and controlling together.

> Controlling appears most often here, but this is only one morning.

The table shows controlling three times and planning once. That does not mean that controlling is more important. It shows that one early morning, with the shop already open, is mostly about checking. A different day, such as the first of the month, would show more planning. The sorting works because every entry has a home, and only four homes are needed.

> Do not confuse the work of managing with the work of the bakery.

One caution. Baking, selling and delivering are not functions of management. They are the operating work of the bakery. A manager's functions are about arranging, guiding and checking that work. When the owner personally bakes a loaf, the owner is working as a baker. The same person may switch between the two kinds of work many times a day, and the switch is easy to miss.

> Levels differ in how much time they give to each function.

Do all managers spend the same time on each function? No. The mix changes with level. Top managers give more of their time to planning, because they look furthest ahead. First-line managers give more to leading and controlling, because they stand beside the daily work. Middle managers divide their time more evenly, and organising is the heart of their role. In every case, however, all four functions appear at every level.

> A small organisation may have all four functions done by one person.

In the small bakery, one person performs all four functions at all three levels. As the shop grows, the owner will pass some of the work to others, and the pattern of who does what will change. Later chapters study each function in turn: planning in Chapter VI, organising in Chapters IX and X, leading in Chapters XI and XII, and controlling in Chapter XIII.

> Before moving on: four functions make a continuing cycle.

*Before moving on.* A function is a main kind of work that managers do. The four functions are planning, organising, leading and controlling. Together they form the management process, a continuing cycle in which controlling leads back to planning. All four appear at every level, though in different amounts.

> You should now be able to name and define the four functions.

*You should now be able to say:* "Managers plan, organise, lead and control, and the four functions form a cycle that never finishes."
