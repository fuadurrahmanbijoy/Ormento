:chapter IV | The Outside World
:mood Where every organisation meets forces it did not choose
:desc An organisation does not work alone. Customers, prices, laws, new machines and rival firms act on it every day, whether the manager watches them or not. This chapter names these outside forces, shows how they reach the work inside, and describes the two ways a manager can answer them: to adapt, or to influence.

> A stall owner loses half her customers in one day and cannot find any mistake of her own.

Hasina sells tea and biscuits from a small stall beside a bus stop. On Sunday she sells about 120 cups. On Monday she sells about 60. Her tea is the same as before. Her price is the same. Her helper arrives on time, and the kettle works well. Nothing in her stall has changed, yet half of her customers have gone.

> She checks her stall, finds nothing wrong, and then looks outside it.

She checks her cups, her stock and her cash box. All are in order. Then she looks up the road. The road authority has moved the bus stop to the next street, 200 metres away. The people who waited beside her stall every morning now wait somewhere else. Her work was good. The cause of her loss lay outside the stall.

> Every organisation has such a Monday, because results depend on what happens outside as well as inside.

Every organisation, small or large, meets a day like this one. A manager who watches only the work inside the organisation will be surprised again and again. Good tea does not protect a stall from a moved bus stop. Good bread does not protect a bakery from a rise in the price of flour. Results come from two sources: the decisions made inside, and the forces that act from outside.

> The chapter asks three questions: what is outside, how does it reach us, and what can a manager do?

This chapter asks three questions. What lies outside an organisation, and how can we sort it? How does a change outside reach the work inside? And what can a manager do about it? The answers prepare the ground for planning in Chapter 6, because a plan is only as good as the manager's view of the future outside.

## 4.1 Inside and Outside

> An organisation has a boundary: managers direct what lies inside it and cannot direct what lies outside it.
@ Boundary | The line between what managers can direct and what they cannot.

In Chapter 1 we defined an organisation as a group of people working together for shared goals. Every organisation has a **boundary**, which is the line between what its managers can direct and what they cannot. Inside the line are the people, money, materials and equipment the manager controls. Outside the line is everything else. For Hasina, the kettle and the helper are inside. The bus stop is outside.

> The word environment has a wider meaning here than in daily speech.

@! Go slowly here.
@ Environment ⚑ | Everything, outside and inside the organisation, that affects its work and results. In daily speech: nature.
Managers use the word **environment** in a special way. In daily speech, the word often means nature: air, trees and rivers. In this book it has a wider meaning. The environment of an organisation is everything, outside it and inside it, that affects its work and its results.^1 The moved bus stop belongs to Hasina's environment. So does the skill of her helper. Both affect how many cups she sells.

^1: The two meanings are related. In both, the environment is what surrounds a living thing or a business and shapes how it fares. A manager uses the word for the whole surrounding set of forces, not only for nature.

> A second picture from the market shows how a seller reads the surroundings every day.

Think of a vegetable seller in a street market. Each morning she looks at the sky, the number of people in the street and the prices that other sellers write on their boards. She cannot change the rain. She can bring a cover for her goods, which is a change inside her stall. She can also stand near the busiest corner, or agree with her neighbours to sell at the same price. In this way she already reads the outside, tests what lies in her power, and chooses an answer. A manager does the same work with more care and with more at stake.

> The environment has two parts: the outside part, which the manager cannot control, and the inside part, which the manager can.

@ External environment | The forces outside the organisation's control.
We divide the environment into two parts. The **external environment** is the set of forces outside the organisation's control. These are the forces that begin beyond the boundary: customers, prices in the market, laws, rival firms and new technology. The manager did not choose them and cannot direct them. For a bakery, the price of flour in the market belongs here.

> The internal environment holds the parts the manager can direct: owners, staff and culture.
@ Internal environment | The owners, the staff and the culture inside the organisation.
The **internal environment** is the set of owners, staff and culture inside the organisation. The owners decide how much money to risk and what to aim for. The staff bring skill and effort. The culture is the set of habits and shared beliefs, such as the habit of starting work at five in the morning. A manager can change these parts, though not always quickly. A habit that has lasted ten years does not change in a week.

> Culture is the least visible inside part, yet it shapes how the staff meet every outside force.

Of the three inside parts, culture is the hardest to see. It is the set of habits and beliefs that the staff share, such as whether they report a mistake at once or hide it. Culture decides how the staff respond when an outside force arrives. In one bakery, a worker who sees that the flour is of poor quality tells the owner immediately. In another, the worker says nothing. The same outside force, a poor delivery, then produces two different results. The internal environment therefore shapes the effect of the external one.

> The boundary is not a wall: forces cross it all the time, and some inside parts are hard to change.

The boundary is not a wall. Forces cross it in both directions, as the arrows in the figure show. A flour mill raises its price, and the change enters the bakery. The bakery sells a loaf, and a customer carries an opinion away. Notice also that the dividing line is one of control, not of place. The bakery's rented shop is a place outside the owner's mind, yet the owner controls its use. A customer may stand inside the shop and still belong to the external environment, because the owner cannot direct the customer's choice.

> A stakeholder is any person or group affected by the organisation, and some stakeholders also affect it.

@ Stakeholder | Anyone who is affected by the organisation, and may also affect it.
Managers also need a word for the people and groups who stand around the organisation. A **stakeholder** is anyone who is affected by what the organisation does, and who may also affect it in return. The bakery has many stakeholders: its customers, its staff, its owner, its flour supplier, its neighbours and the tax office. Some stakeholders lie inside the boundary, such as staff and owners. Others lie outside it, such as customers and suppliers.

> A stakeholder is a party, while a force is an influence; each stakeholder carries one or more forces.

Do not confuse a stakeholder with a force. A stakeholder is a person or a group. A force is a pressure that acts on the organisation. The two are linked. The flour supplier is a stakeholder, and the supplier carries a force: the price of flour. The customers are stakeholders, and they carry another force: their choice to buy or not to buy. In Chapter 5 we shall ask what an organisation owes to its stakeholders. Here we only use them to find the forces.

> The line matters because it shows where effort can repair a problem and where only a response is possible.

Why does the line matter? It tells a manager where effort can change a result. If the cause of a problem lies inside, the manager can repair it: train the staff, mend the oven, correct the schedule. If the cause lies outside, no repair is possible, and the manager must choose a response. A manager who mistakes one for the other wastes effort. Hasina could have blamed her helper for the fall in sales. She might have replaced the helper, and nothing would have improved, because the cause was the bus stop.

> The bakery of the case file already has both environments in its first month.

The bakery of our case file shows both environments from its first month. Its owner opened it as a sole proprietor, with four staff: two bakers, one counter assistant and one rider. The owner's savings of Tk 600,000, the staff and the habit of early baking are inside. The price of flour, the customers who pay Tk 70 for a loaf, the owner of the building who receives Tk 40,000 in rent each month, and the rules for food shops are outside. Notice that rent is a cost inside the books but a force outside the boundary: the owner cannot direct how the rent is set.

The environment is everything that affects an organisation. The part outside its control is the external environment. The part inside it is the internal environment. Stakeholders are the people and groups who carry the forces.

The skill, the habit and the savings are internal. The tax and the price of sugar are external.

## 4.2 The External Environment

> Outside forces are many, so managers sort them into six kinds to see them more easily.

Outside forces are many and mixed. A manager who tries to watch them all at once will see none of them well. So managers sort them into kinds. This book uses six: economic, social, political-legal, technological, competitive and global. The sorting is a tool. It does not describe six separate boxes in the real world. Real forces overlap, and one event can belong to two kinds. The figure below shows the six kinds around one organisation.

:fig The six kinds of outside force acting on one organisation | figures/fig4_forces.svg

> A force can be sorted by asking three short questions: what changed, who carries it, and which kind it is.

How does a manager sort a new force? Three questions are enough. What has changed? Who or what carries the change? And to which of the six kinds does it belong? Apply them to Hasina's Monday. What changed? The place of the bus stop. Who carries it? The road authority, which is a public body that makes decisions for everyone. Which kind is it? A political-legal force, because it is a decision of government. The force then reached the stall through its customers, as the next section explains.

### Economic forces

> Economic forces are the conditions of money and trade that change what things cost and what people can buy.

@ Economic forces | Outside conditions of money and trade, such as prices, incomes and interest rates.
**Economic forces** are the outside conditions of money and trade. They include the general level of prices, the incomes of households, the cost of borrowing money, and the number of people who have work. They affect what the organisation pays and what its customers can pay. Think of a family whose income falls. It buys fewer treats and chooses cheaper food. A shop in that street feels the change within a week.

> Economic forces reach a firm through its costs and through its customers' spending.

The same forces act in two directions. They act on cost: when the price of flour rises, the bakery pays more for each loaf. They act on demand: when household incomes rise, more families can afford fresh bread, and fewer bake at home. A general rise in prices is called inflation. When inflation is high, the bakery's costs rise, and its customers also feel poorer. The two effects can arrive together.

> The cost of borrowing is another economic force, and it reaches firms that need money for machines.

The cost of borrowing is also an economic force. When banks charge more interest on loans, a firm that wishes to buy an oven on credit must pay more for the same oven. Some firms then delay their purchases. When banks charge less, more firms buy. The bakery of our case file used its owner's savings to start, so it feels this force less than a firm that borrowed. A manager must remember that the same force can be large for one firm and small for another.

> An economic force is neither good nor bad in itself; the same change can help one firm and harm another.

An economic force is not good or bad in itself. A rise in the price of flour harms the bakery. The same rise helps the farmer who grows the wheat and the mill that holds a large stock. When managers classify a force, they ask a second question: for whom is this a threat, and for whom is it an opportunity? For many forces, the answer is different for different firms.

### Social forces

> Social forces are the changing numbers, tastes, habits and values of the people around the organisation.

@ Social forces | Outside changes in people's numbers, ages, tastes, habits and values.
**Social forces** are the outside changes in how people live and what they value. They include the number of people in an area, their ages, their education, their tastes and their customs. A bakery near a new housing estate gains customers because the number of people nearby has grown. A bakery in a district where young people leave for work in other cities may lose them.

> Tastes and customs change what people buy, and they also change who is available to work.

Customs change demand in regular ways. Festival seasons, such as Eid, change what families buy and when. Tastes change more slowly. If more families begin to prefer brown bread because they think it is healthier, a bakery that bakes only white bread loses part of its market. Social forces also reach the labour market. The education and the skills of the young people in a town decide who can be hired as a baker or a counter assistant.

> Social forces usually change slowly, so a manager who watches them early has time to respond.

Social forces usually move slowly. A change in age or taste takes years. This is useful to the manager, because early observation leaves time to respond. There are exceptions. A fashion or a rumour can spread in days. A report that a certain food is unsafe can empty a shop in a week, even if the report is false. Managers therefore watch both the slow movements and the sudden ones.

### Political-legal forces

> Political-legal forces are the laws, rules, taxes and policies of governments that apply to the organisation.

@ Political-legal forces | Outside laws, rules, taxes and government policies that apply to the organisation.
**Political-legal forces** are the laws, rules, taxes and government policies that apply to the organisation. They also include the stability of the government itself. A law may forbid an action, such as selling unsafe food. It may require an action, such as keeping written records. It may charge the organisation money, as a tax does. Laws differ from one country to another. This book takes Bangladesh as its default and says so when another system differs.

> A new law reaches an organisation as a new duty, a new cost, or a new limit.

Consider a clinic, a new setting for the idea. Suppose a new law requires every clinic to keep a written record of each patient visit. The law is a political-legal force. The clinic did not choose it and cannot ignore it. Each nurse must now spend minutes on every visit writing the record. The clinic may need a clerk. Its costs rise, and the time for care falls. In the bakery, the corresponding force is a labelling rule for packaged goods, which the case file records in Month 4.

> Political-legal forces are not only limits: governments also support firms, and stable rules help planning.

Political-legal forces are not only limits. A government may support a type of business with training schemes or lower charges, and clear and stable rules help a manager to plan. A government that changes its rules often makes planning hard, even when each rule is fair. A manager therefore watches two things: what the rules are, and how often they change.

### Technological forces

> Technological forces are the new machines, methods and knowledge available outside the organisation.

@ Technological forces | Outside advances in machines, methods and knowledge that an organisation can use or must face.
**Technological forces** are the new machines, methods and knowledge that exist outside the organisation. The organisation can use them, and its rivals can use them too. Technology reaches a firm through purchase. The bakery's new mixer in Month 2 is an example. The mixer was designed and built elsewhere. The bakery bought it, and its output rose from 200 to 250 loaves a day with the same 32 staff hours.

> The same new technology can be an opportunity for the firm that adopts it and a threat for the firm that does not.

The same technology can be an opportunity or a threat. A bakery that adopts a mixer gains speed. A bakery that does not adopt it may fall behind its rivals. Technology also changes how customers behave. Mobile payment applications let a customer pay without cash. Ordering by telephone or by message lets a customer buy without visiting the shop. Each change creates a possible gain for the firm that responds early.

> New technology can destroy an old business, as the history at the end of this chapter shows.

At its strongest, new technology can make an old product unnecessary. A firm that makes a product by an old method may find that customers no longer want the product at all. The history at the end of this chapter tells of one famous firm that faced such a change. For now, remember that technology is a force that managers must watch even when their own product seems safe.

### Competitive forces

> Competitive forces are the pressures from rivals, and also from customers, suppliers and others who shape the market.

@ Competitive forces | Outside pressures from rivals, customers, suppliers and others who shape the market.
**Competitive forces** are the outside pressures from the firms and groups that share the market with the organisation. The most plain pressure comes from rivals, who sell the same product to the same customers. A second pressure comes from customers, who can compare prices and move to another seller. A third comes from suppliers, who may raise their prices if the buyer has no other source. A fourth comes from new firms that enter the market, and a fifth from substitutes.^2

^2: A substitute is a different product that meets the same need, such as rice or biscuits for bread. The idea that these five pressures together shape an industry comes from Michael Porter (1979).

> A rival opening nearby, such as the competitor 300 metres from the bakery, makes it easier for customers to leave.

The bakery in the case file meets such a force in Month 4: a competitor opens 300 metres away. The competitor does not enter the bakery. Its effect arrives through the customers, who now have a second choice at a short walk. If the competitor sells a similar loaf at a lower price, the bakery may lose customers. If the competitor sells a different product, the effect may be small. The manager must find out which case applies.

> Competition is not always a fight: firms may also share costs or buy together, and a rival may increase the number of customers in the area.

Competition has exceptions. Rivals sometimes gain from one another. Several bakeries in one street may attract more buyers than a single bakery would, because customers know where to look for bread. Rivals may also buy flour together to obtain a lower price. A manager who sees every rival only as an enemy may miss these gains.

### Global forces

> Global forces are the influences that come from other countries and from trade between countries.

@ Global forces | Outside influences from other countries and from trade between countries.
**Global forces** are the outside influences that come from other countries. They include the prices of goods traded across borders, the exchange rate between currencies, the rules of trade, and events in other lands. A firm need not sell abroad to feel them. Suppose the flour used by the bakery is made from wheat bought from other countries. Then a change in the world price of wheat will reach the bakery's flour price after some time.

> A change in the exchange rate changes the taka price of every imported item.

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. If the taka falls in value against the dollar, then an item priced in dollars costs more taka. Imported machines, spare parts and ingredients all become dearer. A factory that sells clothes to buyers in other countries depends on global forces more directly still, since its customers live abroad. Even a one-shop bakery, however, is not free from them.

> Global forces overlap with the other five, so one event often belongs to two kinds.

Global forces overlap with the others. A foreign firm that opens in Dhaka is a global force and also a competitive one. A foreign government that changes a tax is a political-legal force that acts as a global one. The sorting into six kinds is a help to thought, not a rule of nature. When an event fits two kinds, the manager writes both names and moves on to the more useful question: what will it do to our work?

The table below shows the six kinds side by side. Read each row from left to right. The first column names the force. The second states what it covers. The third and fourth give an example for the bakery and for a clinic. The clinic column shows that the same kinds of force act on very different organisations.

> Forces often act together and affect one another, so managers must watch the whole set.

Forces seldom act alone. A drought in another country (global) raises the price of wheat (economic). A government may respond with a new tax or a limit on exports (political-legal). Families may then buy less bread (social). The manager sees only the final change in the flour price, but the cause was a chain of forces. For this reason, a manager who studies one kind of force and ignores the others will often misread the situation.

### Opportunity or threat?

> A manager gives each force a second label: opportunity, threat, or both.
@ Opportunity | An outside change that could help the organisation if it acts.
@ Threat | An outside change that could harm the organisation if it does not act.

After sorting a force into its kind, the manager gives it a second label. An **opportunity** here means an outside change that could help the organisation, if it acts. A **threat** means an outside change that could harm it, if it does not act. Many forces are both. The label depends on the firm, not on the force alone.

> The same force can be an opportunity for one firm and a threat for another, as three examples show.

Three examples show this. A rise in the price of flour is a threat to a bakery and an opportunity to the farmers who grow wheat. A new mixer on the market is an opportunity to the bakery that buys it and a threat to a bakery that stays with hand kneading. A new bus stop near a shop is an opportunity for the shop that gains customers and a threat for the shop that loses them. When managers speak of forces, they should therefore always name the firm they have in mind.

> A force is also an opportunity only if the organisation can use it, so capacity matters.

An opportunity is real only if the organisation can use it. A bakery that hears of a large festival order has an opportunity only if it has the ovens, the staff and the flour to fill the order. Otherwise the order is a risk: it may be taken, failed and lost. The manager therefore compares each force with the organisation's strengths and weaknesses inside. This comparison joins the two environments in one picture, which is why managers study both in the same chapter.

> A short study case applies the three sorting questions and the two labels to a clinic.

A clinic gives a new setting for the method. Suppose three things happen in one year. A new law requires written records of each visit. A larger hospital opens a branch two streets away. And the town's population grows older. For each change we ask what changed, who carries it, and which kind it is. The law is political-legal, and it carries a threat to the clinic's time and cost. The branch is competitive, and it carries a threat to the clinic's patients. The older population is social. It carries both a threat, because waiting times may grow, and an opportunity, because more people need care. A manager who writes these three lines on a page has begun to understand the clinic's year.

> Environments differ in how fast they change, and a manager scans more often where change is fast.

Environments also differ in speed. A tea stall in a quiet village meets few changes in a year. A shop that sells telephones in a city meets new models, new rivals and new prices every month. The first manager can scan seldom. The second must scan often. We cannot say that one environment is better than the other. We can say that the speed of change should decide how often the manager looks outside and how much stock, money and time the organisation keeps ready for surprises.

> You should now be able to name the six forces and give one example of each.

You should now be able to say: the external environment contains six kinds of force; each kind reaches the organisation in a different way; and the same change may be a threat to one firm and an opportunity to another. Try this on a new case. A private school learns that a new school has opened nearby, that a new rule limits school fees, and that parents now prefer lessons that include computer skills. Name the kind of force in each part. The answers are competitive, political-legal and social.

## 4.3 How Outside Forces Reach Inside

> A force outside the boundary is harmless to the work until it reaches an inside part.

@! Go slowly here.
An outside force does no harm to the work of an organisation until it reaches something inside. The moved bus stop did not touch Hasina's kettle. It reached her through her customers. This is the idea of this section: every outside force travels along a route, arrives at an inside part, and changes a result. We shall follow the idea in four steps, with small numbers first.

> There are four common routes: inputs, outputs, rules, and people with ideas.

An organisation has four common routes through its boundary. The first route is its **inputs**, the resources it buys or receives from outside, such as materials, money, people and information. The second is its **outputs**, the goods or services it sends out to customers. The third is the **rules** that the law and the market place on how the work is done. The fourth is the **people and ideas** that cross the boundary every day, such as staff who bring new habits from home and information that brings news of new methods.

> Inputs carry changes in price and supply: a rise in the price of flour enters the bakery through its purchases.

Take the first route. The bakery buys flour, sugar, fuel and labour. When any of these changes in price or supply, the change enters through the purchase. The mill raises the price of flour, and the bakery's cost of each loaf rises on the day it buys. Inputs carry the economic forces and many of the global ones.

> Outputs carry changes in demand: a customer's choice to leave enters the organisation through its sales.

The second route is the output. The bakery sells loaves, and the customers' choices come back to it as sales. A rival opens, and the customers change their choice. The bakery sees the effect as fewer sales at the counter. Outputs carry the social forces and the competitive forces, because both act through what customers decide.

> Rules carry the law into the daily work; people and ideas carry tastes and technology inside.

The third route is the rules. A new law does not need a customer or a supplier to carry it. It applies to the work directly, at the moment it begins to hold. The fourth route is people and ideas. A new baker arrives and knows a faster method. A helper reads a message about a new product and proposes it. Technological and social forces often arrive in this way.

> Inside the organisation, different parts feel different forces, so the same route may reach several parts.

Inside the organisation the force meets different parts. In a bakery we may name four: the buying of materials, the baking, the selling and the keeping of accounts. A rise in the price of flour first reaches the buying and the accounts. A rival's new shop reaches the selling. A new labelling rule reaches the baking and the packing. A manager who knows which part a force meets can send the right person to deal with it, and can warn that person early.

> A force follows a chain: outside force, route, inside part, result, and then the manager's choice.

We can now state the idea as a chain. An outside force acts on a route. The route carries it to an inside part. The inside part changes its result. The manager, seeing the result, must choose a response. The figure below shows the chain for the bakery's flour. Notice that the boundary lies between the first box and the second. Only the first box is outside.

:fig One outside change followed through the organisation | figures/fig4_chain.svg

> The first example uses small numbers: a stall's flour price rises by Tk 10 a kilogram.

Now we follow the chain with numbers. We begin with small, clean figures so that the method is the only new thing.

> The first worked example sets out the data, the method, the arithmetic and the check.
*Given.* A bread stall uses 0.2 kg of flour for each loaf and sells 100 loaves a day. The price of flour rises from Tk 60 to Tk 70 per kg.
*Find.* The extra cost of flour for each loaf, and for each day.
*Formula.* Extra cost per loaf = flour used per loaf × rise in price per kg. Extra cost per day = extra cost per loaf × loaves per day.
*Steps.* The rise in price is Tk 70 − Tk 60 = Tk 10 per kg. Extra cost per loaf = 0.2 kg × Tk 10 per kg = Tk 2. Extra cost per day = Tk 2 × 100 loaves = Tk 200.
*Answer.* The rise adds Tk 2 to the cost of each loaf and Tk 200 to the cost of each day.
*Check.* The stall uses 0.2 kg × 100 = 20 kg of flour each day. At Tk 10 more for each kilogram, 20 × Tk 10 = Tk 200. The two methods agree.

> Three common slips: the wrong base for a percentage, mixed periods, and missing units.

Three slips are common. First, a percentage rise is measured on the old price. Here the rise is Tk 10 on Tk 60, which is 10 ÷ 60 = 0.167, or about 16.7 per cent. Dividing by the new price gives 14.3 per cent, which is wrong. Second, a cost per loaf and a cost per day must not be mixed. Third, always write the unit, because Tk 2 and Tk 200 mean very different things.

> The second example is a new setting: a clinic, where a law reaches the work through the rules.

Our second example comes from a clinic, a new setting for the same idea. The route here is the rules, and the result involves time rather than money. We use the productivity ratio from Chapter 2: output divided by input.

> The second worked example applies the same method to time and to productivity.
*Given.* A nurse works 8 hours, or 480 minutes, a day. Each patient visit takes 20 minutes. A new law adds 5 minutes of writing to each visit.
*Find.* The change in patients seen each day, and in productivity.
*Formula.* Patients seen = minutes available ÷ minutes per visit. Productivity = patients seen ÷ hours worked.
*Steps.* Before the law: 480 ÷ 20 = 24 patients, so productivity is 24 ÷ 8 = 3.0 patients per hour. After the law each visit takes 20 + 5 = 25 minutes, so 480 ÷ 25 = 19.2 patients. Productivity is 19.2 ÷ 8 = 2.4 patients per hour.
*Answer.* The nurse sees about 19 patients instead of 24. Productivity falls from 3.0 to 2.4 patients per hour, a fall of 0.6 ÷ 3.0 = 20 per cent.
*Check.* Five extra minutes on a 20-minute visit is one quarter more time. One quarter more time gives one fifth fewer patients: 24 × 0.8 = 19.2. The answers agree.

> Two more traces show that the method works for competitors and for social change.

Two short traces show that the method is general. For the bakery, a competitor opens 300 metres away. The force is competitive, and the route is outputs. The inside part is the selling. The effect is fewer sales at the counter, and perhaps lower prices later. For the clinic, the town's population grows older. The force is social, and the route is outputs, since the patients are the clinic's customers. The inside part is the clinic's rooms and staff. The effect is more visits and longer waiting, unless the clinic adds staff.

> How strongly a force strikes depends on how much the firm depends on the thing that changed.

Two firms facing the same force may feel it very differently. The strength of the effect depends on how much the firm depends on the thing that changed. A bakery that spends a large part of its costs on flour feels a flour price rise sharply. A firm that uses little flour barely notices it. Speed matters too. A change in the price of flour arrives within days. A change in the age of the town's people may take ten years. Managers therefore ask of each force: how soon will it arrive, and how much do we depend on it?

> Real chains are often longer and slower than the simple chain, and the effects may be indirect.

The chain in the figure is short on purpose. Real chains are longer. A change in the price of flour may first reduce the owner's profit, then delay a plan to buy a new oven, then slow the rise of output, and at last change the staff's chance of a bonus. The effects may also arrive late. A manager who traces only the first step sees only a small part of the result. For this reason it is useful to ask a second question after each step: and what does that change next?

> You should now be able to follow any outside change along its route to a result.

You should now be able to say: an outside force reaches the organisation through inputs, outputs, rules, or people and ideas; the force changes an inside part and then a result; and the strength of the effect depends on dependence and on speed. Try it on a new case. A bank raises the interest rate on loans. Which route does the change use to reach a bakery that has borrowed money for an oven? It uses the inputs route, because borrowed money is an input.

## 4.4 Managing the Environment

> A manager who sees an outside force can answer it in two ways: change the inside, or try to change the outside.

A manager who sees an outside force has two kinds of answer. The manager can change something inside the organisation so that the force does less harm or brings more gain. Or the manager can try to change the force itself. Both answers have names, and both are needed. Before we describe them, we must ask how the manager sees the force at all.

> Environmental scanning is the habit of watching outside forces for early signs of change.

@ Environmental scanning | Watching outside forces for early signs of change.
The first task is to see. **Environmental scanning** is the habit of watching the outside forces for early signs of change. A baker who scans talks to customers at the counter, asks the flour supplier about coming prices, reads the news about wheat and tax, and notes when a new shop opens. Each small signal is useful. Together the signals warn the manager early. A warning that arrives late leaves no choice. A warning that arrives early leaves many.

> Scanning uses many cheap sources, and the manager chooses a few to watch every week.

The sources of scanning are many and cheap. A manager may listen to customers and staff, who see changes first. The supplier knows about coming prices. Newspapers report new laws and new rivals. Trade groups circulate information among their members. A manager cannot watch everything, so it is wise to choose a few sources and to review them every week. A short written list is better than a memory, because a list shows which forces have gone unwatched.

> Scanning feeds the planning premises of Chapter 6: assumptions about the future that a plan rests on.

Scanning has a further use. In Chapter 6 we shall meet the planning premise, which is an assumption about the future on which a plan rests. A plan that assumes flour will cost Tk 60 for a year is a poor plan if flour is already rising. The premises come from the environment, so good scanning supports good planning. A manager who scans seldom writes premises that are far from the facts.

> To adapt means to change the inside of the organisation so that it fits the outside force.

@ Adapt | To change the organisation so that it fits an outside force.
The first answer is to **adapt**, which means to change the organisation so that it fits the outside force. The force stays as it is. The organisation moves. When flour becomes dearer, the bakery may raise its price, change its recipe, find a cheaper supplier or reduce waste. When a new law requires records, the clinic may hire a clerk. All of these changes are inside the boundary, so they lie within the manager's power.

> Adaptation can change four things: the price, the product, the process and the people.

An organisation can adapt in four main ways. It can change its price, as the bakery may do when flour rises. It can change its product, for example by offering a smaller loaf at the old price. It can change its process, by using less flour with a better recipe or by wasting less. And it can change its people, by training the staff for a new rule or by hiring a clerk. Each of the four lies inside the boundary. A manager who lists all four before choosing is less likely to choose only the first one that comes to mind.

> Waiting and watching is also a choice, and it is right when a force is small or uncertain.

There is a fifth possibility that deserves its place: to wait and watch. If a force is small, or if its path is uncertain, an early answer may cost more than the force itself. A manager who waits should still scan, and should set a point, such as a price of flour or a loss of sales, at which waiting will end. Waiting with such a point is a decision. Waiting without one is only delay.

> Adapting early costs less than adapting late, because early action leaves time to choose.

Timing changes the cost of adapting. A bakery that learns in advance that flour will rise can buy extra stock, negotiate with its supplier or change the recipe in an orderly way. A bakery that learns on the day of the rise must pay at once. The same answer, applied early, may cost less and cause fewer errors. This is the practical value of scanning: it moves the manager's answer from the day of the shock to the days before it.

> Adapting is fast and within the manager's power, but it leaves the cause in place and may not be enough.

Adapting has strengths. It is within the manager's power, and it can begin at once. It also has limits. The cause remains, so the pressure may return. An adaptation may cost money, as a new oven does. A rival may adapt faster. And some adaptations are not possible: a bakery cannot lower its price to match a rival if its costs are higher.

> To influence means to try to change the outside force itself, by actions that stay within the law.

@ Influence | To try to change an outside force.
The second answer is to **influence**, which means to try to change the outside force. Here the organisation acts across the boundary. It cannot control the force, so it can only try to change it. A bakery may advertise, so that more customers prefer its bread. It may sign a long contract with a mill, to fix the price of flour for a year. Bakeries may form a trade group and speak together to officials about a tax. A clinic may explain to the authority how a rule affects patients.

> Influence takes several forms, such as advertising, agreements and joint action with other firms.

Influence takes several forms. Advertising tries to change the tastes of customers. A long contract tries to fix a price or a supply. Joint action, through a trade group, tries to change a rule or a price by speaking with many voices. Good relations with officials, suppliers and neighbours also count, because a firm that is trusted is more likely to be heard. Each form has a cost in time or money, and none can promise the result.

:fig The two answers to an outside force | figures/fig4_options.svg

> Influence can remove the cause, but it needs power or numbers, it is slow, and it must stay honest and lawful.

Influence also has strengths and limits. It can remove the cause instead of only the effect. Its limits are real. A single small firm has little power. Results arrive slowly, and they are uncertain. Honesty is a firm rule: to influence an outside force by lies or by gifts to officials is wrong and may be against the law. Chapter 5 discusses such limits under the name of ethics.

> The table compares the two answers in four ways.

The table below compares the two answers. Read down each column. Notice that the two columns describe different places of action. Adapting acts inside the boundary. Influence acts across it.

:table Adapting and influencing compared
| Question | Adapt | Influence |
| What changes? | The inside of the organisation | The outside force |
| Bakery example | Raise the price; change the recipe | Join a bakers' group to speak with flour mills |
| Strength | Within the manager's power; can be quick | May remove the cause itself |
| Limit | The cause stays; may cost money | Slow, uncertain, needs power; must stay lawful |

> Most firms use both answers, and four questions help a manager to choose the mix.

Most organisations use both answers. A manager chooses the mix by asking four questions. Can the force be changed at all? A law may be changed by many voices, but the weather cannot. Does the firm have power alone, or can it join others? A single stall cannot move a bus stop, but fifty stalls may be heard. How soon is the answer needed? Influence is slow, so an urgent problem needs adaptation first. And what does each answer cost, in money and in right conduct? The cheapest answer is not always acceptable.

> Timing and cooperation often decide which answer is better, and both answers can be used together.

Adaptation and influence do not exclude one another. A bakery may adapt this month by raising its price a little, and may also join a group of bakeries to seek a better price from the mills next year. The first answer protects the profit today. The second tries to reduce the problem for the future. A manager who uses only one of the answers, always, is likely to be surprised sooner or later.

> The word control must be used with care: the manager can influence the outside, not control it.

Here we meet an exception to the earlier definition. We said that the external environment lies outside the organisation's control. Influence does not contradict this. To influence is to try, and to try is not to control. A large firm may succeed often, and a small firm rarely. Even a large firm cannot control the weather, the exchange rate or the tastes of a whole country. The accurate statement is that the manager controls the inside, influences a small part of the outside, and must adapt to the rest.

> You should now be able to name the two answers and to say when each is suitable.

You should now be able to say: scanning lets the manager see forces early; to adapt is to change the inside, and to influence is to try to change the outside; and most firms use both, choosing by power, speed and cost. Try this on a new case. Rain in the week before a festival reduces the number of people in the market. The firm cannot change the rain. Which answer remains? Only adapting, for example by selling through telephone orders for that week.

## Worked Case: Three Forces at Once

> The case file for Month 4 records three outside changes that reached the bakery together.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Morning Loaf Bakery, Month 4. Flour rises from Tk 60 to Tk 69 per kg, a rise of 15%. Output is 250 loaves a day at 0.24 kg of flour per loaf, so the bakery uses 60 kg of flour a day. It trades 26 days a month. A competitor opens 300 metres away. A new labelling rule applies to packaged goods. The loaf price is Tk 70 and the ingredient cost is about Tk 24 per loaf. Rent is Tk 40,000 a month.

### Situation

> Three separate changes arrive in one month, and the owner must understand each before choosing.

Three outside changes arrive in the same month. The price of flour has risen. A competitor has opened close by. A rule has changed the way packaged bread must be labelled. We shall use the chapter's tools in order: sort the forces, trace each to the inside, measure what can be measured, and list the answers.

### Question

> The question asks which forces act, how they arrive, and how large the cost of the flour change is.

Which kind of force is each change? By which route does each reach the bakery? What does the flour change cost in a day and a month? Which answers are open to the owner?

### Reasoning

> The first step sorts the three changes into kinds and routes.

First, sort. The rise in the price of flour is an economic force, and it arrives through the inputs route. The competitor is a competitive force, and it arrives through the outputs route, because the effect shows in the choices of customers. The labelling rule is a political-legal force, and it arrives through the rules route, because it changes how packaged bread is prepared.

> The second step measures the flour change, one line at a time.

Second, measure the flour change.

> The bakery's flour change is worked in the same order as the earlier examples.
*Given.* Flour rises from Tk 60 to Tk 69 per kg. The bakery uses 0.24 kg per loaf, makes 250 loaves a day and trades 26 days a month.
*Find.* The extra cost per loaf, per day and per month, and the percentage rise.
*Formula.* Extra cost per loaf = flour per loaf × rise in price per kg. Extra cost per day = extra cost per loaf × loaves a day. Extra cost per month = extra cost per day × trading days.
*Steps.* The rise is Tk 69 − Tk 60 = Tk 9 per kg, which is 9 ÷ 60 = 0.15, or 15 per cent. Extra cost per loaf = 0.24 kg × Tk 9 = Tk 2.16. The ingredient cost of a loaf moves from about Tk 24 to about Tk 26.16. Extra cost per day = Tk 2.16 × 250 = Tk 540. Extra cost per month = Tk 540 × 26 = Tk 14,040.
*Answer.* The flour rise adds Tk 2.16 to each loaf, Tk 540 to each day and Tk 14,040 to each month.
*Check.* The bakery uses 60 kg a day. At Tk 9 more per kilogram, 60 × Tk 9 = Tk 540. The two methods agree. The monthly cost is also about 35 per cent of the monthly rent (14,040 ÷ 40,000 = 0.351), which shows that the change is large for a shop of this size.

> A fourth step tests the price answer against the competitor, using stated suppositions.

Fourth, test the price answer. The next figures are suppositions made for study. They are not facts of the case file. Before the flour rise, the money left from each loaf after ingredients was Tk 70 − Tk 24 = Tk 46, and the bakery made 250 loaves, so the daily total was 250 × Tk 46 = Tk 11,500. After the rise it is 250 × Tk 43.84 = Tk 10,960, a fall of Tk 540, as before. If the price rises to Tk 72.16, each loaf again leaves Tk 72.16 − Tk 26.16 = Tk 46. The price answer pays only if enough customers stay. The break-even number of loaves is Tk 10,960 ÷ Tk 46 = 238.3. So the bakery can lose up to 250 − 238.3 = 11.7 loaves a day, or about 4.7 per cent of its sales, before the price rise makes things worse than doing nothing. With a rival 300 metres away, the owner must judge whether a loss of that size is likely.

> The third step lists the answers open to the owner, with the limits that the competitor creates.

Third, list the answers. The owner may adapt. A price rise that recovers the whole extra cost would be Tk 2.16 on each loaf, which is 2.16 ÷ 70 = 0.031, or about 3.1 per cent of the price. Other answers are a change of recipe, a search for a cheaper source of flour, and less waste. But the competitor 300 metres away limits the first of these. If the rival keeps its price, a higher price may send customers across the road. The owner may also try to influence. A single bakery has little power over a flour mill, but several bakeries buying together may obtain a better price. For the labelling rule, adaptation is the only answer, because the owner must follow the law. These are possible answers only. The case file records no decision for this month.

### Conclusion

> The three forces differ in route and in the answers they allow, and they act together on one set of results.

The three forces arrive by three different routes. Each allows a different answer. They also act together: the flour rise cuts the profit of every loaf, and the competitor makes it harder to raise the price. The bakery must therefore read the three forces as a set. This habit, to see forces together, prepares the planning of Chapter 6.

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas are listed in order.

The environment is everything, outside and inside, that affects an organisation. The external environment lies outside the manager's control and has six kinds of force: economic, social, political-legal, technological, competitive and global. The internal environment consists of owners, staff and culture. Stakeholders are the people and groups who carry the forces. A force reaches the work inside by four routes: inputs, outputs, rules, and people and ideas. A manager sees forces early by scanning. The manager then adapts, by changing the inside, or tries to influence, by changing the outside. Most firms do both. The manager should also remember three further points. The same force may be an opportunity for one firm and a threat for another. Forces seldom act alone, and real chains of effect are longer than the simple chain. And the choice between adapting and influencing depends on power, speed, cost and right conduct.

## Where people go wrong

> Five common errors are stated and corrected.

*The environment means nature.* In this book it means everything, outside and inside, that affects the organisation. Nature is only one small part.

> A force can be an opportunity for one firm and a threat for another.
*Outside forces are always threats.* A force is a threat to one firm and an opportunity to another. The manager asks for whom it is which.

> A force acts whether or not the manager watches it.
*A firm can ignore what it cannot control.* A force acts whether the manager watches it or not. What the manager cannot control, the manager can still see and answer.

> Customers are only one kind of stakeholder.
*A stakeholder is the same as a customer.* Customers are one kind of stakeholder. Staff, owners, suppliers, neighbours and officials are others.

> Influence uses honest and lawful means.
*To influence the environment means to pay for favours.* Influence uses honest and lawful means such as advertising, contracts and speaking together. Bribes and lies are wrong and may be against the law.

> Adapting does not mean surrender, since it is often the quickest way to protect the goal.

*To adapt means to give in.* Adapting is a choice made to protect the goal. A bakery that changes its recipe to meet a flour price has not given in. It has kept the goal and changed the method.

## In history

> Kodak shows how a technological force can reach a firm that has itself created the technology.

In 1975 an engineer at Eastman Kodak, Steven Sasson, built a camera that recorded pictures as digital data. The prototype was about the size of a toaster, and it stored black-and-white pictures on a cassette tape. Sasson later recalled that managers doubted that people would wish to view photographs on a television screen. Printed photographs were selling well, and the firm had a large film business to protect.

> The firm later sold digital cameras, yet people's habits and the arrival of phone cameras reduced the value of its older business.

Over the following decades, the habits of people changed. Digital pictures spread, and cameras appeared inside telephones. Kodak made digital cameras itself, but its older business of film and prints declined. In January 2012 Kodak filed for Chapter 11 protection, a legal process in the United States that lets a firm reorganise its debts. It left the process in 2013 as a smaller company.

> The case also shows that a firm may hold the knowledge of a change long before it holds the will to answer it.

The case also shows a difference between knowing and answering. A firm may hold the knowledge of a change long before it decides to respond. In Kodak's story the knowledge existed in 1975. The difficulty lay in the response, which would have changed a large and profitable business. Managers who read the environment well must therefore ask two questions: what is changing, and are we willing to change in answer?

> The lesson is about seeing and answering outside change early; the causes of the firm's decline are still debated.

The case does not give one simple cause, and observers still disagree about the reasons. It does show three of the ideas in this chapter. A technological force can reach even the firm that created the technology. Social change, in how people took and kept pictures, travelled with it. And an early warning is useful only if the organisation answers it. Scanning shows the change, and adapting or influencing is the answer that follows.
