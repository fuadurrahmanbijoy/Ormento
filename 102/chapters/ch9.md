> Three cousins run a stall, and every task is shared without effort.

Three cousins open a tea-and-snack stall beside a bus stop in Dhaka. On the first day, each cousin does everything in turn. One buys the tea leaves, then boils the water, then serves the customers, then washes the cups. The cousins talk all day, so each one knows what the others have done. Nobody needs a rule, because the whole business can be seen from the place where any one of them stands.

> Growth brings five new workers, and the order disappears.

After a year the stall is busy, and the cousins hire five more people. Within a week the trouble begins. Two workers buy sugar on the same morning, and nobody buys tea leaves. One cousin tells a new worker to serve, and another cousin tells her to fry samosas; she does neither well. Two people argue over whose turn it is to wash the cups while customers wait. The stall sells the same tea as before, to the same people, at the same price.

> The work is the same; only the number of people has changed, and that raises four questions.

Nothing about the tea has changed. What has changed is the number of people, and with it the distance between one person and the whole business. With three people, each could see everything. With eight, nobody can. Four questions now need an answer. What work must be done? Who does which part? Who gives orders to whom? How do the parts fit together?

> The answers to the four questions form the structure of the organisation.

@ Structure | The way an organisation divides its work among people and links those people together.
@ Design | Choosing a structure on purpose, so that it fits the organisation's needs.

The answers to these four questions together give the stall its **structure**. Structure means the way an organisation divides its work among people and links those people to one another. The stall's first structure was three cousins sharing everything. Its second structure must be chosen with care, and choosing a structure on purpose is called **design**. A good design lets eight people work as smoothly as three. A poor design makes eight people work worse than three.

> The chapter answers the four questions in order.

This chapter answers the four questions in turn. It begins with what organising is and the steps it follows. It then shows how work is divided and grouped, how formal and informal arrangements differ, and which forms an organisation can take. Next it explains how to choose a design that fits the situation. It ends with the means of keeping the parts working together.

## What Organising Is

> A family preparing a wedding feast must divide the work before anyone cooks.

Begin with something familiar. A family is preparing a wedding feast for two hundred guests. The plan is clear: the food must be ready by seven in the evening. A plan alone does not cook the rice. Someone must decide who buys the food, who cooks it, who prepares the hall, who welcomes the guests, and who answers if the fish runs short. Until those choices are made, willing people will do some tasks twice and leave other tasks undone.

> Arranging people and work so that plans become action is called organising.

@! Go slowly here.
@ Organising | Arranging people and work so that plans become action.

That arranging is what management calls **organising**. Organising means arranging people and work so that plans become action. The family's plan said what must happen; the organising said who would do each part. Chapter 2 named organising as the second of the four functions, after planning. Here we study it fully, one idea at a time.

> Organising follows planning, continues over time, concerns people, and serves goals.

Four features describe the nature of organising. First, it follows planning: without a plan, nothing tells us what to arrange. Second, it is continuing: when plans, people or conditions change, the arrangement must change too. Third, it deals with people as well as tasks, so it must take account of their skills, habits and feelings. Fourth, it is a means and not an end: nobody organises for its own sake, but only to reach the goals in the plan.

> Organising has five purposes, and each removes one kind of waste.

Organising serves five purposes. It makes plans become daily work. It gives every task an owner, so that no task is left undone and none is done twice. It allows resources to be used well, because people and equipment are placed where they help most; this raises productivity, the measure taught in Chapter 2. It makes each person's duties known, so that anyone can say who is answerable for a result. And it makes growth possible, because a business that can add people without confusion can add output without confusion.

> Six steps lead from a plan to a working arrangement.

The work of organising follows six steps, in this order. Step one is to study the plan and the objectives, because the work to be done comes from them. Step two is to list all the work, in small parts: buying, mixing, baking, selling, delivering, and so on. Step three is to divide that work into jobs that one person can do. Step four is to group the jobs under managers; the next section calls these groups departments.

> The last two steps give people the means to act and join the parts.

Step five is to give each person and each manager the authority and the resources needed to do the job. Authority means the right to give orders and use resources; Chapter 10 teaches it in full. Step six is to link the parts, so that they work together as one. The figure below shows the six steps in order. Steps two to four are the subject of the next section, and step six is the subject of the last section of this chapter.

:fig The six steps of organising, from the plan to the working arrangement. | ch9_fig1_steps.svg

> The steps match the four questions raised by the stall.

The steps match the four questions from the stall. Steps two and three answer *what work must be done* and *who does which part*. Steps four and five answer *who gives orders to whom*. Step six answers *how the parts fit together*. When a business is in trouble, it helps to ask which of the four questions has been left unanswered, because the missing answer usually points to the step that was skipped.

> Owner, employee and customer each see organising from a different seat.

Organising looks different from each seat. The owner sees a way to reach the plan with fewer mistakes. The employee sees an answer to two daily questions: what exactly is my job today, and whom do I ask when I am unsure? The customer notices organising only when it fails: a long wait, a wrong order, or two people giving different answers. A good arrangement is mostly invisible, which is why its value is easy to forget.

> No arrangement is final, and a very small firm needs little of it.

There are limits. A one-person shop needs almost no organising, because one mind holds the whole plan. Arrangements also grow old: a structure that suits ten people may block fifty. And organising can be overdone. Too many jobs, too many levels and too many rules can slow the very work they were meant to help. The right amount depends on the situation, and the section on designing a structure returns to this point.

> A short recap fixes the idea before moving on.

*Before moving on.* Organising means arranging people and work so that plans become action. It follows planning, continues as conditions change, concerns people, and serves goals. Its six steps run from studying the plan to linking the parts.
*You should now be able to say:* "Organising is arranging people and work so that plans become action, and it follows six steps."

## Dividing the Work and Grouping the Jobs

> In 1776 Adam Smith described ten workers who made far more pins together than apart.

In 1776 a Scottish thinker named Adam Smith (1723 to 1790) published a famous book on the wealth of nations. Early in it he describes a small pin workshop.^1 Making a pin, he says, takes about eighteen separate operations. One worker draws out the wire, another straightens it, a third cuts it, a fourth points one end, and a fifth grinds the other end to receive the head. Making the head takes several more operations, and so do whitening the pins and putting them in paper. In the workshop he describes, ten workers shared the operations among them.

> A short calculation shows how large the gain was.

Smith reports that the ten workers made more than 48,000 pins in a day. We can find the share of each worker with the measure from Chapter 2. *Given:* total output 48,000 pins a day; 10 workers. *Find:* pins per worker per day. *Formula:* output per worker equals total output divided by the number of workers. *Steps:* 48,000 ÷ 10 = 4,800 pins. *Answer:* each worker's share was 4,800 pins a day. *Check:* 4,800 × 10 = 48,000, which matches the total.

> Working alone, one person could make perhaps one pin a day, not thousands.

Smith then asks what would happen if each worker made pins alone, doing every operation. He answers that one such worker could hardly make twenty pins in a day, and perhaps not even one. Take the generous figure of twenty. The shared work gave 4,800 pins per worker, and 4,800 ÷ 20 = 240. So the divided work produced at least 240 times as much per worker. Check: 20 × 240 = 4,800. The same ten people, the same wire, and a far larger output: the gain came from the way the work was arranged.

> The idea behind the pin workshop is called division of labour.

@ Division of labour | Splitting a job into parts so that each person does one part.

The idea behind Smith's workshop has a name: **division of labour**. Division of labour means splitting a job into parts, so that each person does one part. Each worker then becomes a specialist, a person who does one narrow kind of work and does it well. The cousins' stall had no division of labour in its first year, because each cousin did every task. After hiring, the stall must decide how to divide its work.

> A task is one piece of work; a job is the set of tasks one person holds.

@ Task | One piece of work, such as cutting wire or serving a customer.
@ Job | The set of tasks that one person is asked to do.

Two small terms keep the idea exact. A **task** means one piece of work, such as drawing out wire or serving a customer. A **job** means the set of tasks that one person is asked to do. In Smith's workshop, drawing out wire was a task, and the worker who did it all day held a job. Dividing labour changes the jobs: it cuts big jobs into smaller ones.

> Smith gave three reasons why dividing the work raises output.

Smith gave three reasons for the gain. First, a worker who repeats one task becomes quick and skilled at it. Second, no time is lost in changing from one task to another, such as changing tools or moving to another place. Third, a worker who studies one task often finds a better tool or method, and some tasks can in time be given to machines. The third reason links to Chapter 3, where we saw that studying work can find the best method.

> At the bakery, even four people divide their labour into three kinds of job.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Month 1. Morning Loaf Bakery opens with four employees: two bakers, one counter employee and one rider. Output is 200 loaves a day from 32 working hours a day, which is 6.25 loaves per hour. Labour is already divided into three kinds of job: making bread, selling bread and delivering bread. The owner is the only manager. No employee is asked to bake in the morning and ride in the afternoon.

> Division of labour has costs as well as gains.

Division also has costs. Work cut into very small parts can bore the person who repeats one part all day, and boredom can lower quality and raise absence. A worker may learn only one narrow skill, and so find it hard to cover for a colleague who is ill. Workers who see only their small part may not care about the finished product. Finally, every division creates a need to join the parts together again. When ten workers make pins in separate steps, someone must make sure that the straightened wire reaches the cutter. This last cost is called coordination, and the final section of the chapter teaches it.

> Job rotation and wider jobs reduce the boredom of narrow work.

Managers have found ways to reduce the costs of narrow jobs without giving up the gains of skill. In job rotation, employees move between jobs at fixed times, so that each learns more than one task and none repeats one task all day. In job enlargement, a job is made wider by adding tasks of a similar kind. Both ideas keep most of the benefit of division and lower the boredom. Chapter 11 returns to them when it studies what drives people to work well. The point to hold here is that division of labour is a matter of degree, and the right degree can be changed.

> Division of labour has two natural limits: the market and the cost of joining the parts.

Division of labour has natural limits. Smith himself saw one: it cannot go further than the size of the market allows. A workshop that sells only two thousand pins a week cannot employ ten specialists, because most of them would have nothing to do. A second limit lies in the costs named above. At some point another division costs more in waiting, boredom and coordination than it gains in skill. The rule is to divide the work while the gain from skill is larger than the cost of joining the parts, and to stop there.

> A short recap before the next idea.

*Before moving on.* Division of labour means splitting a job into parts so that each person does one part. It raises output through skill, saved time and better methods. It costs boredom, narrow skills and a need for coordination, and it is limited by the market and by those costs.

### Grouping Jobs into Departments

> Once many jobs exist, they must be gathered under managers.

@ Department | A group of jobs placed under one manager.
@ Departmentalisation | Grouping jobs into departments.

Dividing the work creates many jobs. One manager cannot direct fifty jobs one by one, so the jobs are gathered into groups. A **department** is a group of jobs placed under one manager. **Departmentalisation** means grouping jobs into departments. The choice is important, because a department decides who works beside whom, who shares a manager, and which skills sit together.

> The first choice is what the jobs in each group should have in common.

@ Function | In Chapter 2, one of the four management activities. Here, one specialist area of work, such as selling.

Four bases for grouping are common. Grouping by **function** puts together jobs that need the same kind of skill, such as production, selling or finance. Grouping by product puts together all the jobs needed to make and sell one product. Grouping by territory puts together all the jobs in one place. Grouping by customer puts together all the jobs that serve one kind of buyer. The table below compares the four. Read each row from left to right: the basis, what it gathers, when it works well, and where it is weak.

:table Four common ways to group jobs
| Basis | Gathers together | Works well when | Weakness |
| Function | jobs needing the same skill | the firm has one main product | departments may put their own goals before the whole firm's goals |
| Product | all jobs for one product | the firm sells several different products | the same skills are repeated in each group, which costs more |
| Territory | all jobs in one place | customers are spread over a wide area | it is harder to keep one standard everywhere |
| Customer | all jobs for one kind of buyer | buyers differ greatly in their needs | groups overlap in skill, and costs rise |

> Function is the usual first choice for a firm with one main product.

Grouping by function is the usual first choice for a firm that makes one main product. Skills sit together, so people learn from one another and equipment is shared. Its weakness is that each department may care more for its own work than for the whole. A clinic with twelve employees could group by function: a medical department, a pharmacy, and a front desk. A company with clinics in four cities might group by territory instead, so that each city has its own manager who knows the local patients.

> Factories also group by process or by time.

Two further bases appear in factories. Grouping by process puts together all jobs that use the same machine or method, such as painting. Grouping by time creates shifts, such as a day shift and a night shift, when the work must continue around the clock.

> Large firms use more than one basis, at different levels.

Large firms often use more than one basis. A firm may group by product at the top, giving each product its own division, and then group by function inside each division. The choice is not made once for all time. When a firm adds a new product, enters a new city, or doubles in size, the best basis may change.

> Grouping by function helps specialists but can hide the customer from view.

Each basis has a hidden risk, and the risk of function is worth a closer look. When all the bakers sit in one department and all the sellers in another, each group learns its own craft well. Yet the customer who wants fresh bread at eight o'clock deals with both groups, and neither group sees the whole request. If the bread is late, production blames the counter for a wrong order, and the counter blames production for slow baking. The remedy is not a different chart alone. It is a link between the departments, which is the subject of the last section of this chapter.

> A short recap of grouping.

*Before moving on.* A department is a group of jobs under one manager. Departmentalisation groups jobs by function, product, territory, customer, process or time. Function is the usual first choice for a small firm with one main product.
*You should now be able to say:* "Division of labour splits the work into jobs; departmentalisation gathers the jobs into departments."

## Formal and Informal Organisation

> A chart shows who should report to whom, but not how people really work together.

@ Organisation chart | A diagram of the jobs in an organisation and the reporting lines between them.
@ Chain of command | The path along which orders pass from the top of an organisation to the bottom.

Most organisations draw their structure as a diagram of boxes and lines, called an **organisation chart**. Each box is a job, and each line joins a manager to the people who report to that manager. The path of orders from the top box down to the bottom box is the **chain of command**. A new nurse in a ward looks at the chart and learns that she reports to the ward sister. In her first days she does exactly that.

> The chart, the written rules and the job descriptions together form the formal organisation.

@ Formal organisation | The structure that is officially described, with its jobs, departments and reporting lines.

The structure that the chart shows, together with the written rules and job descriptions that go with it, is the **formal organisation**. Formal organisation means the structure that is officially described, with its jobs, departments and reporting lines. It is planned in advance. It is written down. It belongs to the jobs, not to the people who hold them: when one nurse leaves, the box remains and another nurse fills it.

> People form ties that no chart shows.

@ Informal organisation | The friendships, habits and unwritten rules among people, outside the chart.

But people are not boxes. In her first week, the same nurse learns that the quickest way to find a missing medicine trolley is to ask an older nurse who is not her manager. She learns who eats lunch with whom and which doctor likes to be asked early. These ties make up the **informal organisation**. Informal organisation means the friendships, habits and unwritten rules among people that the chart does not show. No manager ordered them, and no document records them.

> The table sets the two kinds of organisation side by side.

The table below sets the two side by side. Read each row as one question and its two answers.

:table Formal and informal organisation compared
| Question | Formal organisation | Informal organisation |
| How does it begin? | It is planned by managers | It grows among people |
| What is it built on? | Jobs and positions | Persons and friendships |
| Is it written down? | Yes, in charts and rules | No |
| How fast does it change? | Slowly, by decision | Quickly, with the people |

> Informal ties can speed up work or spread resistance.

Informal organisation has good and bad effects. It can speed work, because people share knowledge across departments without waiting for permission. It can give people a sense of belonging, which helps them stay. It can also spread rumours, protect poor work from criticism, or unite people against a change. Whether the effect is good or bad depends on whether the informal groups share the goals of the organisation.

> A ward with a friendly informal network shows both the benefit and the danger.

Consider a hospital ward again. The ward sister has told her nurses to record each medicine in a new written book. An older nurse, whom the others trust, says the book wastes time. Within a week most nurses have stopped using it. The formal organisation gave the order, and the informal organisation decided whether it was followed. Had the sister first asked the trusted nurse to try the book and report its faults, the same group might have carried the change instead of blocking it.

> A manager should know the informal organisation, use it, and not try to remove it.

Managers cannot abolish informal organisation, because it comes from people's need for company. A sensible manager learns who the trusted people are, listens to what they say, and explains changes before announcing them. Managers who ignore the informal organisation are often surprised when a plan that looks perfect on the chart fails in daily practice. Chapter 11 returns to informal groups and explains why they form.

> Four checks help a manager read the informal organisation.

A manager can learn a great deal by asking four plain questions. Who do people ask when they are unsure? Who eats and rests together? Whose opinion changes the opinions of others? Which unwritten habits help the work, and which hinder it? The answers do not appear on any chart, but they explain why some orders are carried out quickly and others are quietly ignored.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* The formal organisation is the structure shown on the chart and in the rules. The informal organisation is the web of friendships and habits outside it. Both exist in every organisation, and a manager works with both.

## Forms of Organisation

> Six forms show the main ways to arrange jobs and authority.

Structures can be arranged in several recognisable forms. Six are described here: line, line-and-staff, functional, committee, project and matrix. Few real organisations use one form alone. Most mix several, as a house may be built with brick, wood and glass. Learning the pure forms helps us to see what each part of a mixed structure is for.

### Line Organisation

> The simplest form is a single chain of command from top to bottom.

@ Line | In daily life, a row of people or things. In an organisation, the positions that do its core work, joined in one chain of command.

The simplest form uses only **line** positions. Line positions are the jobs that do the organisation's core work directly, joined in one chain of command. In a bakery, the line jobs are baking, selling and delivering. In a line organisation every person has one manager, and orders pass straight down the chain. This gives clear duties, fast decisions and unity of command, the idea from Chapter 3. Its weakness is that each manager must know everything about the work below. As the firm grows, the managers become overloaded, and no specialist is there to help them. Line organisation therefore suits small firms.

### Line-and-Staff Organisation

> Advisers who support the line, but do not command it, are added as a firm grows.

@ Staff | In daily life, all the employees of a firm. In organisation design, advisers who support the line with special knowledge but do not direct it.

As a firm grows, line managers need expert help with matters such as law, accounts or hiring. The firm adds **staff** positions. Staff means advisers who support the line with special knowledge but do not direct line work. Take care with the word. In daily speech, staff means all the employees of a firm. In this chapter it means advisers only, and we use the word employees when we mean everyone. A legal adviser who tells a manager whether a contract is safe holds a staff position. The adviser gives advice; the manager decides.

> Line-and-staff organisation gains expertise but can cause friction.

A **line-and-staff organisation** keeps the chain of command and adds advisers beside it. Its strength is expertise without overloading the line managers. Its weaknesses are cost and friction. Line managers may ignore advice they did not ask for, and advisers may feel that their good ideas are wasted. Chapter 10 explains the kinds of authority that line and staff positions hold. ### Functional Organisation

> Taylor proposed that a worker take orders from several specialist foremen.

Frederick W. Taylor, whose scientific management we met in Chapter 3, proposed a different arrangement for the workshop (Taylor, 1903). In a **functional organisation**, each worker takes orders from several specialist bosses, and each boss is an expert in one part of the work. One boss might direct the speed of work, another the quality checks, and another the repair of machines.^2 The strength is expert guidance at every point. The weakness is serious: the worker has more than one boss, so unity of command is broken, orders may conflict, and nobody can say who is answerable for the result. Few firms use the pure form today. A mild version remains where specialists have the right to instruct others on their own subject, and Chapter 10 returns to it.

### Committee

> A committee gives a task to a group and not to one person.

A **committee** is a group of people formally chosen to study a matter or to decide it together. Some committees are standing, meaning they continue; others are temporary, meaning they end when the task ends. Committees bring together different knowledge and different views, and they share responsibility for a decision. Their weaknesses are slowness, compromise and cost, since many people spend time on one matter. They can also leave nobody answerable. Chapter 8 listed the strengths and weaknesses of group decisions, and the same points apply here. A committee is best used for matters that need many views, and worst used for matters that need speed.

### Project Organisation

> A project team brings people from several departments for one task, then ends.

A **project organisation** is a temporary team formed to complete one task that has a fixed end, such as opening a new shop. The team is led by a project manager, and its members come from the departments. When the task is finished, the team ends and the members return to their departments. Its strengths are focus and speed on the one task. Its weaknesses are disruption to the departments that lend their people, and uncertainty for members who do not know where they will go next. Keep this form apart from the matrix below: the project team is temporary, while a matrix is permanent.

### Matrix Organisation

> A student in a school play has a class teacher and a play director at once.

@! Go slowly here.

Begin with something familiar. In a school, a student acts in the school play. During lessons the student answers to the class teacher. During rehearsals the same student answers to the play director. Both give orders, and both are right to do so, each in their own area.

> The matrix crosses a line of skill with a line of tasks.

@ Matrix | Two lines of command crossed, so that one person reports to two managers.

A **matrix** is the organisation form that does this on purpose. A matrix means two lines of command crossed, so that one person reports to two managers. One line follows the specialist area, such as production, sales or finance. The other follows the task, such as one project or one product. The figure below shows the idea as a grid. Each employee sits where a column meets a row: the head of the column controls the skill, and the manager of the row controls the task.

:fig A matrix organisation: each employee answers to a head of function and to a project manager. | ch9_fig3_matrix.svg

> Matrix organisation shares scarce experts but breaks unity of command.

The matrix has real strengths. Scarce experts can be shared among many projects instead of being locked into one. The firm can respond quickly when a new project begins. Technical standards and project needs both have a voice. It also has real weaknesses. Recall unity of command from Chapter 3, which gives each person one boss. The matrix breaks that rule, so an employee may receive two conflicting orders on the same morning. Decisions can be slow, power struggles are common, and the structure works only where people communicate openly. It suits firms with many projects and scarce experts, such as building and engineering firms. It rarely suits a small firm with one product.

> A recap of the matrix before the comparison of forms.

*Before moving on.* A matrix gives each person two bosses, one for the skill and one for the task. It shares experts and speeds new projects, but it breaks unity of command and needs open communication.
*You should now be able to say:* "A matrix crosses a line of skill with a line of tasks, so one person has two managers."

> The same form looks different from the owner's, the employee's and the customer's seat.

Each form also looks different from each seat. The owner of a small firm sees a line organisation as control and speed. An employee in a matrix sees it as interesting work and also as two sets of demands on one day. A customer of a firm that relies on committees sees it as delay, because every decision waits for a meeting. The same structure can therefore be a strength to one person and a burden to another, and a wise manager asks all three how the design feels in practice.

> No form is best; real firms mix them to suit different parts of the work.

No form is best in every case. A bakery of four employees may be a line organisation. When it grows, it may add a part-time adviser and become line-and-staff. It may form a temporary project team to open a second shop, and a committee to review hygiene. The skill lies in matching each form to the part of the work it suits, and in knowing the weakness that comes with each choice.

## Designing a Structure

> A tax office and a design studio need very different structures, and neither is wrong.

Consider two organisations. A tax office handles thousands of similar forms. Mistakes are costly, and every citizen must be treated by the same rules. A small web design studio makes a different website each month, and its work changes every week. Give the tax office the studio's loose arrangement, and each clerk will handle the same form in a different way. Give the studio the tax office's rules, and every new idea will wait for three signatures. The two structures differ because the two situations differ. Design means choosing the structure that fits.

### The Bureaucratic Model

> Max Weber described a design built on rules, ranks and written records.

Max Weber (1864 to 1920) was a German thinker who studied how large organisations, such as state offices and armies, did their work.^3 He described a form that he called bureaucracy. In everyday speech the word now suggests delay, but Weber used it for a particular design, and we use it in his sense.

> The bureaucratic model rests on written rules, fixed ranks and records.

@ Bureaucratic model | A design built on written rules, fixed ranks and records.

The **bureaucratic model** is a design built on written rules, fixed ranks and records. Weber named its main features. Duties are fixed, through a clear division of labour. Positions are arranged in ranks, and each rank is controlled by the one above. Written rules say how tasks are done, and written records keep the results. Everyone is treated by the same rules, whoever they are. And people are chosen and promoted for their skill, not for their family or friends.

> The bureaucratic model gives fairness and accuracy, but it responds slowly to change.

The model has strong points. It is fair, since the same rule applies to all. It is predictable, since people know what to expect. It is accurate, because records and procedures reduce errors. It can run very large organisations. Its weaknesses appear when conditions change. Rules written for yesterday may not fit today. Employees may follow a rule even when it plainly harms the customer. Decisions climb through many ranks and arrive late. People who are not asked for ideas stop offering them. The model suits stable conditions, repeated work and large size.

### The Behavioural Model

> The behavioural model builds the structure around the needs and abilities of people.

@ Behavioural model | A design that stresses people, teamwork and flexibility more than rules and ranks.

The **behavioural model** is a design that stresses people, teamwork and flexibility more than rules and ranks. It draws on the human relations approach from Chapter 3, which stressed people's feelings and groups. In this design, jobs are broad and can change. Work is done in teams. There are few written rules. Decisions are taken by the people who have the knowledge, whatever their rank. Talk moves freely across levels, and influence comes from expertise as well as from position.^4

> The behavioural model gives speed and creativity, but weaker order and fairness.

This model also has strong points. It responds quickly to change, it encourages new ideas, and it often raises commitment, since people feel trusted. Its weaknesses are the reverse of the first model's strengths. Duties can be unclear, and conflicts can grow without clear rules to settle them. Routine decisions may take longer because more people join the talk. The design is hard to manage when the organisation is large, and it depends on skilled and willing people. It suits changing conditions, creative work and expert employees.

### Situational Factors

> No single design suits every firm, so the design must fit the situation.

@! Go slowly here.

If no model is best, what decides the choice? Chapter 3 introduced the contingency approach, which says that the best action depends on the situation. Applied to structure, it names factors that push a design one way or the other. Five factors are taught here: size, technology, environment, strategy and people. We take them one at a time.

> Size changes how many rules and levels a firm needs.

*Size.* A firm of four can work by talk alone, because everyone sees everything. A firm of four hundred cannot. As size grows, the firm needs more departments, more levels of managers, and more written rules, because no one can speak with everyone. Growth therefore pushes a design toward the bureaucratic end of the scale, and a firm that grows must review its structure.

> Technology means the way the work is done, from routine to one-of-a-kind.

*Technology.* Technology here means the methods and equipment used to do the work. Routine work, such as making thousands of identical items, fits fixed jobs and clear rules. One-of-a-kind work, such as designing a special machine, needs flexible teams. Joan Woodward studied manufacturing firms in England in the 1950s and found that structures differed with the production method (Woodward, 1965).

> A stable environment fits rules; a changing one needs flexibility.

*Environment.* Chapter 4 explained the forces outside an organisation. When they change slowly, rules written today will still serve tomorrow. When they change fast, through new technology, new competitors or new laws, rules age quickly and people must adapt on their own. Tom Burns and G. M. Stalker studied firms in Britain in the 1950s and found that firms in stable conditions did well with rule-bound structures, while firms in fast-changing conditions did better with flexible ones (Burns and Stalker, 1961).

> Structure should follow strategy, the firm's chosen way to reach its goals.

*Strategy.* Strategy means the firm's chosen way to reach its goals, the long-range kind of plan from Chapter 6. Alfred Chandler studied large American firms and found that when a firm changed its strategy, for example by adding many products, its old structure began to fail, and a new one followed (Chandler, 1962). His conclusion is often stated in four words: structure follows strategy.

> People matter, because skilled and willing employees need less supervision.

*People.* The skills and attitudes of employees also matter. Skilled professionals who value freedom usually work well under the behavioural model. New employees who need clear instruction may do better with defined duties and rules. A structure that ignores the people who must work in it will be resisted, however logical it looks on the chart.

### Applying the Factors

> A small clinic shows the five factors working together.

Take a new setting. A clinic has twelve employees and is told by a new law to keep written records of every patient. How should its structure respond? We apply the five factors in turn. *Size:* twelve is small, so little formality is needed in general. *Technology:* patient care differs from one patient to the next, but billing is routine. *Environment:* the new law adds a fixed outside demand for records. *Strategy:* the clinic aims at good care, which depends on the doctors' judgement. *People:* doctors and nurses are skilled and value freedom to decide.

> The same factors also tell a manager when to change the design.

The factors also tell a manager when to change a design. Suppose the clinic doubles to twenty-four employees. Size has changed, so more people need clear reporting lines, and a single manager can no longer hear every request. Suppose instead that a new technology lets patients book by telephone application. Technology has changed, so the front desk must learn new routines and the rules for records must change. A manager who reviews the five factors once a year will notice such changes before they cause trouble, and will adjust the structure in small steps and not in a crisis.

> The factors point to a mixed design.

The factors point in different directions, and the answer is a mixed design. For records and billing, the clinic should adopt bureaucratic features: written rules, forms and a person answerable for accuracy. For patient care, it should keep behavioural features: teamwork and decisions by those who know the patient. Mixed designs are common because most organisations contain both routine and changing work.

:box IN SHORT
A good design divides the work into jobs, groups the jobs under managers, links the groups, and fits the firm's size, technology, environment, strategy and people. When one of these changes, the design should be reviewed.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* The bureaucratic model is built on written rules, fixed ranks and records. The behavioural model stresses people and flexibility. Five factors, namely size, technology, environment, strategy and people, decide which mix fits.
*You should now be able to say:* "The best structure depends on the situation, so a manager studies five factors before choosing."

## Coordination: Fitting the Parts Together

> Division creates parts, and the parts must then be fitted together.

@ Coordination | Fitting the parts of an organisation together so that they work toward one goal.

Return to the stall. The cousins have divided the work: one makes tea, one fries snacks, one washes cups. If the cups are not ready when the tea is ready, the tea grows cold and customers wait. Each worker has done the assigned task, and the stall still fails. The missing element is **coordination**. Coordination means fitting the parts of an organisation together so that they work toward one goal. Division makes the parts, and coordination makes them one.

> The need for coordination grows faster than the number of people.

Coordination becomes harder as the organisation grows, and the growth is faster than it first appears. Any two people may need to speak to each other, so the number of possible pairs matters. *Given:* the stall has 3 people, then 8. *Find:* the number of possible pairs. *Formula:* pairs equal people × (people − 1) ÷ 2. *Steps:* for 3 people, 3 × 2 ÷ 2 = 3 pairs; for 8 people, 8 × 7 ÷ 2 = 28 pairs. *Answer:* the stall has 3 possible pairs at first and 28 after hiring. *Check:* for 3 people called A, B and C, the pairs are AB, AC and BC, which is 3. More than doubling the people (3 to 8) multiplied the possible links by nearly ten.

> Two other conditions raise the need: dependence between parts, and uncertainty.

Two other conditions raise the need for coordination. The first is dependence between the parts. Dependence can be of three kinds. In sequence, the output of one part becomes the input of the next, as dough becomes bread and bread reaches the counter. In sharing, the parts work separately but together make the whole, as two shops of one firm add their sales. In two-way dependence, each part keeps needing something from the other, as a kitchen and a counter keep exchanging changes to orders (Thompson, 1967). The more the dependence, the more the coordination. The second condition is uncertainty. When plans change often, fixed rules fail, and people must talk.

> Coordination uses six means, from the chain of command to shared records.

Managers have six main means of coordination. *The chain of command:* a manager above resolves disputes between two parts; Chapter 10 teaches it. *Plans, schedules and rules:* these fix in advance who does what and when, as Chapter 6 showed. *Shared objectives:* when every part aims at one measurable target, as in Chapter 7, the parts adjust themselves to it. *Direct contact:* meetings, briefings and conversation let people settle matters as they arise. *Linking roles and teams:* a person or team whose task is to connect departments. *Shared records and information systems:* a written order sheet, or a computer system, that all can read.

> The wedding feast shows how a briefing and a written list work together.

Return to the wedding feast from the start of the chapter. The family can coordinate in two ways. It can hold a short meeting at noon, where each helper reports what is ready and what is late; that is direct contact. It can also keep a written list on the kitchen wall, where each task is ticked when finished; that is a shared record. The meeting settles surprises, and the list prevents forgotten tasks. Most organisations, like the family, use more than one means at once, because each covers a weakness in the other.

> Each means suits some conditions and costs something.

No means is free. Rules and plans cost little to use but suit stable, repeated work. Direct contact suits changing work but takes time, and a firm that holds too many meetings may coordinate its parts and exhaust its people. A manager chooses by asking how much dependence and how much uncertainty there is, and then selects the cheapest means that works. Coordination also differs from control. Coordination fits the parts together as work proceeds, while control, taught in Chapter 13, checks results against standards afterward.

> Coordination is the main cost of a design, and it is easy to underpay.

Many firms count the saving from dividing work and forget the price of joining it again. A firm that splits one job into four should ask what it will spend on meetings, forms and managers to fit the four parts together. If the answer is larger than the gain in skill, the division has gone too far. This is why the question at the end of the chapter asks whether more division always needs more coordination. The answer lies in the balance between the two, and a good manager keeps both in view.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Coordination means fitting the parts of an organisation together so that they work toward one goal. The need grows with the number of people, the dependence between the parts, and uncertainty. Six means serve it: chain of command, plans and rules, shared objectives, direct contact, linking roles, and shared records.

## Worked Case: The Bakery at Month Nine

> The case applies the whole chapter to one growing business.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Month 9. *Situation.* Morning Loaf Bakery has grown from four employees to eight: production has four, sales has three, and delivery has one. The plan for months 7 to 12 is 300 loaves a day, with objectives agreed in Month 7. Buying and money stay with the owner for now. Until now the owner told each employee what to do each day. With eight employees, that has become too much.

> The question asks for a division, a grouping and a link between the parts.

:case *Question.* How should the work be divided and grouped, and how should the parts be fitted together?

> The reasoning starts with the work and the plan.

:case *Reasoning.* First, we list the work from the plan: mixing and baking, selling at the counter, delivering, and buying and money. Second, we divide the work into jobs. Baker, helper, counter employee and rider are jobs, and the buying and money tasks stay with the owner. Third, we choose a basis for grouping. The bakery has one main product, one shop and one kind of customer, so grouping by function suits it. Fourth, we form three departments: production, sales and delivery. Production is headed by the head baker and sales by the counter lead. The rider reports directly to the owner.

:fig Morning Loaf Bakery, Month 9: three departments grouped by function. Case file, illustrative. | ch9_fig5_bakery_chart.svg

> The parts are linked by a daily briefing and an order sheet.

:case *Reasoning, continued.* Fifth, we ask how the parts fit together. Production must know today's orders, sales must know when the bread is ready, and delivery must know which orders to carry. These are dependencies of the sequential and two-way kinds. The bakery coordinates them with two means: a briefing at 7 am each day, which is direct contact, and an order sheet, which is a shared record. The owner also notices an informal organisation: a lunch group has formed among four of the eight employees. It appears on no chart. The owner decides to listen to it, since it may carry news early, and not to forbid it.

> The conclusion states the design in one sentence.

:case *Conclusion.* The bakery is grouped by function into three departments under the owner, coordinated by a daily briefing and an order sheet, and it has an informal lunch group beside the formal structure.

> The check counts people and tests the design against the rules of the chapter.

:case *Check.* Count the employees: production 4 plus sales 3 plus delivery 1 equals 8, which matches. Each employee has one manager: the head baker directs three, the counter lead directs two, and the owner directs the head baker, the counter lead and the rider. Every task on the work list has an owner. Chapter 10 returns to these counts. Since the plan rises to 300 loaves a day, the owner should review the design when the bakery next grows.

## Summary

> A summary of the whole chapter.

Organising means arranging people and work so that plans become action, in six steps. Division of labour splits the work into jobs, which raises output but needs coordination. Departmentalisation gathers jobs into departments by function, product, territory, customer, process or time. Every organisation has a formal structure on its chart and an informal organisation among its people. The main forms are line, line-and-staff, functional, committee, project and matrix. The bureaucratic model suits stable, routine work; the behavioural model suits changing, creative work; and size, technology, environment, strategy and people decide the mix. Coordination fits the parts together by the chain of command, plans, shared objectives, direct contact, linking roles and shared records.

## Think It Through

> A tailoring workshop tests whether more division always needs more coordination.

A tailor working alone makes three shirts a day. A workshop of six tailors, each doing one step, makes twenty-four shirts a day. Find the output per worker in the workshop, and compare it with the lone tailor. Then notice that the cutter finishes early on some days and the stitchers wait for one another on others. Does more division of labour always need more coordination? What new work appears when the workshop grows to twelve? Think about the number of possible pairs, the kinds of dependence, and the means of coordination, and form your own answer.

## Where People Go Wrong

> A last mistake: a design chosen once stays right for ever.

*"A good design lasts for ever."* A design is a decision made for a situation, and situations change. Size, technology, environment, strategy and people all move over time, so the design must be reviewed from time to time.

> Mistake one: the chart is the whole organisation.

*"The chart shows the organisation."* The chart shows only the formal organisation. Friendships, habits and unwritten rules also shape how work is done, and a plan that ignores them may fail.

> Mistake two: more departments always mean better order.

*"More departments mean better order."* Each department adds a manager and a boundary that the work must cross. Departments should be added when the gain from specialist skill exceeds the cost of coordination.

> Mistake three: staff means employees.

*"Staff means all employees."* In daily speech it does. In organisation design it means advisers who support the line but do not command it. The same word has two meanings, so check which one is in use.

> Mistake four: informal organisation is a problem to be removed.

*"Informal groups are a problem."* They are a fact. They can spread rumours, and they can carry knowledge and loyalty. A manager studies and uses them and does not try to remove them.

> Mistake five: one design is best for all firms.

*"There is one best structure."* The tax office and the design studio show that the best design depends on the situation. A structure that was right at ten employees may be wrong at a hundred.

> Mistake six: coordination and control are the same.

*"Coordination and control are the same."* Coordination fits parts together while the work goes on. Control checks results against standards afterward and corrects them.

## In History: Order at General Motors

> General Motors was rescued by a better structure and not by a better car.

General Motors was founded in 1908 by William Durant, who built it by buying many car companies. By 1920 the company was in crisis. Its units were poorly joined. Durant resigned as president in November 1920.

> Sloan proposed independent divisions under a central office.

Alfred P. Sloan (1875 to 1966), an executive of the company, had written a plan for a new structure. He proposed that each car division should run as a separate business under its own general manager, grouped by product. A central office would set broad policy, gather figures, and judge each division by the return it earned. Sloan became president in 1923. Committees, such as a technical committee formed in that year, helped the divisions to share knowledge without losing their independence.

> The structure became a model for large firms.

The design worked. It gave divisions the freedom to respond to their own markets, and it gave the centre the information to judge them. The historian Alfred Chandler later described it as a pattern copied by many large firms. Sloan's own account appeared in *My Years with General Motors* (1963). The case shows this chapter's main lessons in one story: group by product when products multiply, coordinate by information and committees, and review the structure when the firm grows.

Weber, Max. *Economy and Society*, published in German after his death in 1920, with an English translation in 1947. It gave the first careful account of bureaucracy as a form of organisation.

Woodward, Joan (1965), *Industrial Organization: Theory and Practice*; Burns, Tom and Stalker, G. M. (1961), *The Management of Innovation*; Chandler, Alfred D. (1962), *Strategy and Structure*.
