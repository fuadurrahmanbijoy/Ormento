:chapter XVI | Leasing
:mood Is it wiser to own the machine or to rent it?
:desc A firm often needs an asset but does not need to own it. This chapter explains what a lease is, the two main kinds, why firms choose to lease, and how to compare leasing with buying in today's taka and after tax.

> A rent that adds up to more than the price looks like a bad bargain.

The bakery needs a display freezer. It costs Tk 1,50,000 to buy. A leasing company offers the same freezer for Tk 42,000 at the end of each year for five years. Add the five payments and the total is Tk 2,10,000. That is Tk 60,000 more than the price. Many people would stop here and say that buying must be cheaper.

> The simple sum leaves out time, borrowing and tax.

The simple sum is not wrong, but it is not complete. Buying the freezer needs Tk 1,50,000 today, and the bakery does not have it. It must borrow the money, and the loan has interest. Buying also gives tax benefits that renting does not give. And a taka paid in five years is worth less than a taka paid today. A fair comparison must put all these things on one scale.

> This chapter gives a method that does so.

This chapter builds that scale. We first learn what a lease is and the two kinds of lease. We then examine why firms lease at all. After this we compare leasing with buying by following four steps. The freezer will show whether the extra Tk 60,000 is real.

## What Leasing Is

> Using an asset and owning it are two different things.

Think of a student who rents a room instead of buying a house. She has the use of the room every day, but the owner of the house is another person. She pays a rent for the right to use it, and when the agreement ends she gives the room back. Many firms treat machines in the same way.

@ Lease | An agreement in which the owner of an asset lets another party use it in return for regular payments.

> The agreement has a name, and so do its two parties.

A **lease** means an agreement in which the owner of an asset lets another party use it, in return for regular payments, for a stated time. A bakery that agrees to use a freezer for five years and to pay each year has made a lease. The agreement is written in a contract that states the asset, the time and the payments.

@ Lessor | The owner of the asset, who lets another party use it.

@ Lessee | The party that uses the asset and pays for the right to do so.

> The owner and the user need separate names.

The owner of the asset is the **lessor**. The user is the **lessee**. A simple way to remember the two: the lessor lets the asset go to another, and the lessee is the party that has the lease. In our case, the leasing company is the lessor and the bakery is the lessee.

@ Lease rental | The regular payment that the lessee makes to the lessor for the use of the asset.

> The regular payment is the price of using the asset.

The payment that the lessee makes at fixed times is the **lease rental**. It may be paid each month, each quarter or each year. In this chapter we assume that it is paid at the end of each year, the same timing as in the annuities of Chapter 5.

> The next figure shows who gives what.

The next figure shows how the parties are linked. The lessor buys the asset from a supplier. It then allows the lessee to use the asset, and the lessee pays the rental.

:fig The parties to a lease. The lessor buys the asset from a supplier and lets the lessee use it; the lessee pays the rental. | fig16_1.svg

> How to read the figure.

Read the figure from the top. The supplier makes or sells the freezer and receives its price from the lessor. The arrow from the lessor to the lessee shows the freezer in use. The arrow below it shows the rental going back. Note that the lessee never pays the supplier. The lessor carries the large payment, and recovers it through the rentals.

> Ownership and use are separated, and this has consequences.

The key idea is that the lease separates ownership from use. This has two results. First, the lessee does not need a large sum on the first day. Second, the lessee does not receive what the asset is worth when the lease ends, unless the contract says so. We shall see in the next section that the contracts differ on this point.

> Leasing exists in Bangladesh, in several forms.

In Bangladesh, leases are offered by some finance companies and banks.^1 The ideas in this chapter apply wherever a lease is used, but the tax rules differ between countries, and a student should check the rules for the place and year concerned.

^1: Leasing by financial institutions in Bangladesh is understood to be regulated by Bangladesh Bank under the Financial Institutions Act, 1993. This statement and the tax treatment of lease rentals and depreciation must be verified against current law before printing.

> Recap: a lease divides ownership from use.

*Before moving on.* A lease lets one party use an asset owned by another, for payments called lease rentals. The owner is the lessor and the user is the lessee. The lessee gains use without paying the full price at the start.

## Operating and Financial Leases

@ Operating lease | A short lease in which the lessor keeps most of the risks of owning the asset.

@ Financial lease | A long lease that covers most of the life of the asset, and in which the lessee carries most of the risks.

> Not all leases are alike.

A lease for one weekend and a lease for the whole life of a machine are very different agreements. So leases are divided into two main kinds, according to how long they last and who carries the risk. The names are **operating lease** and **financial lease**.

> The first kind is short and flexible.

An operating lease is a short rental in which the owner keeps most of the risk of owning the asset. It lasts for much less than the working life of the asset, and the lessee can often cancel it with notice. The lessor usually pays for repair and insurance, and the rental includes these costs. Renting a car for a month is an example. The lessor expects to lease the asset again to another user, or to sell it.

> The second kind is long and firm.

A financial lease is a long rental that covers most of the life of the asset. The lessee usually cannot cancel it without a heavy penalty. The lessee pays for repair and insurance. In effect, the lessor has lent the price of the asset, and the rentals repay it with interest. This kind of lease is much like a loan, and the decision method that we shall learn is built for it.

@ Residual value | What the asset is worth at the end of the lease or at the end of its useful life.

> The end of the lease raises a question about value.

At the end of a lease, the asset still has some worth. This is its **residual value**, which means the value of the asset when the lease ends. If a freezer that cost Tk 1,50,000 can be sold for Tk 10,000 after five years, its residual value is Tk 10,000. Who receives that Tk 10,000? In an operating lease, the lessor does. In a financial lease, the contract may let the lessee buy the asset for a small sum, or return it.

> The table lists the main differences.

The table below puts the two kinds side by side. Read each row to compare them on one point.

:table Operating lease and financial lease compared
| Point | Operating lease | Financial lease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Length | Short, much less than the asset's life | Long, most of the asset's life |
| Cancelling | Often allowed with notice | Usually not allowed, or heavy penalty |
| Repair and insurance | Usually paid by the lessor | Usually paid by the lessee |
| Residual value | Belongs to the lessor | May go to the lessee by contract |
| Nature | Rental for use | Like a loan to buy the asset |

@ Sale and leaseback | A firm sells an asset it owns to a lessor and leases the same asset back at once.

> A third arrangement links to the second.

One more arrangement is common. In a **sale and leaseback**, a firm that already owns an asset sells it to a lessor and at once leases it back. The firm receives cash and keeps the use of the asset. A firm that needs money for other purposes can use this method, but it then pays rentals for something it once owned.

> The name of the lease does not settle the matter.

There is an exception. A contract may be called an operating lease and yet behave like a financial lease, if it lasts for most of the asset's life and cannot be cancelled. The substance counts and not the name. Accounting rules also treat leases in a particular way. Under the international standard that many countries now use, a lessee records most leases as an asset and a liability in its accounts, so the old difference matters less for the lessee's records, but it is still useful for understanding the risks.^2

^2: The standard referred to is IFRS 16, which took effect for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2019. Whether and from when it applies in Bangladesh, and the exact rules for lessees and lessors, must be verified with the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Bangladesh before printing.

> Recap: two kinds, one test.

*Before moving on.* An operating lease is short and the lessor keeps most of the risk. A financial lease is long and works like a loan. The residual value is the worth of the asset at the end. The substance of the contract decides which kind it is.

## Why Firms Lease

> A good decision begins with the reasons.

If a firm can borrow and buy, why does it lease? There are several reasons, and each is true in some situations and false in others. We list them with care.

> The first reason is to save cash at the start.

*Little cash at the start.* A lease needs no large payment on the first day. The bakery can use the freezer immediately and pay from the sales that the freezer helps to earn. A firm that is short of cash, or a young firm that finds borrowing hard, may value this very much. A loan, however, can also pay for the whole price, so this advantage is smaller than it seems when a loan is available.

> The second reason is to keep choices open.

*Flexibility and change.* Some assets become old-fashioned quickly, such as computers and phones. A short lease lets a firm return the asset and take a newer one. The firm avoids being stuck with something it no longer wants. The cost of this flexibility is usually a higher rental.

> The third reason is skill and service.

*Service and skill.* Some lessors specialise in an asset. They know how to maintain it and may include repair in the rental. A bakery does not need to learn to repair freezers.

> The fourth reason is tax.

*Tax.* An owner of an asset claims depreciation, and this saves tax. A lessor that pays much tax can use the depreciation well, and may pass part of the benefit to the lessee through a lower rental. A firm with little taxable profit cannot use depreciation, so leasing may suit it. The rental is also an expense that reduces taxable profit.

> The fifth reason is fewer conditions.

*Fewer conditions.* A lender may place conditions, called covenants in Chapter 13, on a loan. A lease may carry fewer such conditions. A lessor also holds the asset as its own security, so it may agree when a bank has refused.

> The drawbacks are as real as the reasons.

There are drawbacks too, and a careful student states them. The total of the rentals can exceed the cost of buying and borrowing. The rentals are fixed obligations, like loan payments, and they add to financial risk as described in Chapter 9. The lessee loses the residual value. And the lessee usually cannot change the asset. A firm should lease for a clear reason, not from habit.

> Two questions to test the section.

*Pause and check.* (1) A new café needs an oven for six months to test a recipe. Which kind of lease suits it, and why? (2) Give one reason why a firm with little taxable profit may prefer to lease. A short answer is given at the back of the book.

> Recap: reasons and drawbacks.

*Before moving on.* Firms lease to save cash at the start, to keep choices open, to receive service and skill, to use tax benefits that they cannot use themselves, and to avoid conditions. The drawbacks are higher total cost, fixed obligations and the loss of residual value.

## The Lease-or-Buy Decision: Four Steps

@! Go slowly here. Several parts combine.

> Two ways of getting the same asset must be compared fairly.

The bakery can obtain the freezer in two ways. It can lease, or it can borrow and buy. We want to know which costs less. A fair comparison needs three things. The cash flows must be in the same units, in taka that are paid out. They must be after tax, because tax differs between the two ways. And they must be brought to today, because they fall at different times.

> We assume that buying is paid for with a loan.

We assume that if the bakery buys, it pays by borrowing the whole price. This keeps the comparison clean. Both choices then involve fixed yearly payments, and the real question is which set of payments costs less after tax. If the owner could pay with cash, the same method would apply, with the cash outlay in place of the loan.

> The decision follows four steps.

The decision is made in four steps. First, find the after-tax cash outflows of the lease. Second, find the after-tax cash outflows of buying. Third, find the present value of each set of outflows. Fourth, choose the choice with the smaller present value, because it costs less in today's taka. The next figure shows the steps.

:fig The four steps of the lease-or-buy decision. | fig16_2.svg

> The first step is the easier of the two.

*Step 1: the lease.* The rental is an expense, so it reduces taxable profit and saves tax. If the rental is Tk 42,000 and the tax rate is 25%, the tax saved is Tk 10,500. The after-tax outflow is Tk 42,000 less Tk 10,500, which is Tk 31,500. In symbols, the after-tax rental equals the rental × (1 − T). This is the same rule as for interest in Chapter 15.

> The second step has three parts.

*Step 2: buying.* The outflows of buying have three parts. The firm pays the loan instalments, which are interest plus part of the loan. It saves tax on the interest, and it saves tax on the depreciation of the asset. Only interest and depreciation save tax. The repayment of the loan itself is not an expense, so it saves none. At the end, the firm may sell the asset, and the sale brings a cash inflow that reduces the outflow of that year.

> Each year's net outflow is built from these parts.

For each year, the after-tax outflow of buying equals the loan instalment, less the tax saved on interest, less the tax saved on depreciation, and less any cash received from selling the asset. It may help to write the interest and the depreciation together: tax saved = tax rate × (interest + depreciation).

> The third step needs a discount rate, and the choice matters.

*Step 3: present values.* We must choose a rate to bring each outflow to today. We use the after-tax cost of debt from Chapter 15, and not the weighted average cost of capital. The reason is that lease rentals are fixed, and the lessor has a claim like that of a lender. They carry the same kind of risk as loan payments, so they should be discounted at the price of loan money. In the bakery's case this is 9%.

> The fourth step is the decision.

*Step 4: choose.* Add the discounted outflows of each choice. The choice with the smaller total is cheaper. If the totals are close, the owner should also weigh matters that the numbers leave out, such as the need for flexibility or service.

> Recap: four steps, one discount rate.

*Before moving on.* Compare the after-tax outflows of the lease and of buying, bring both to today at the after-tax cost of debt, and choose the smaller present value. Interest and depreciation save tax; loan repayments do not.

> A check the reader can test.

*You should now be able to say:* the lease-or-buy decision compares the present values of the after-tax cash outflows of two ways of obtaining the same asset.

## After-Tax Outflows and the Present-Value Comparison

> We begin with a small example to learn the layout.

We now carry out the four steps with numbers. The first example uses small, round figures so that the method is the only new thing. The loan in this first example is repaid in equal parts of principal, which makes the interest easy to calculate. The second example, in the case that follows, uses equal instalments.

> The data of the first example.

*Given.* A machine costs Tk 1,00,000 and lasts 4 years. It will have no value at the end. The firm can borrow the whole price at 12%, repaying Tk 25,000 of the loan at the end of each year, together with the interest on the balance. Depreciation is Tk 25,000 a year. The firm can instead lease the machine for Tk 30,000 at the end of each year. The tax rate is 25%, and the after-tax cost of debt is 9%. *Find.* Whether to lease or buy.

> The lease side is a single annuity.

*Step 1.* The after-tax rental is 30,000 × (1 − 0.25) = Tk 22,500 in each of the 4 years.

> The purchase side builds up year by year.

*Step 2.* The interest is 12% of the balance owed at the start of each year: Tk 12,000, Tk 9,000, Tk 6,000 and Tk 3,000. The table sets out the work.

:table Buying with a loan: after-tax outflows (Tk)
| Year | Loan paid | Interest | Interest + depreciation | Tax saved (25%) | After-tax outflow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 37,000 | 12,000 | 37,000 | 9,250 | 27,750 |
| 2 | 34,000 | 9,000 | 34,000 | 8,500 | 25,500 |
| 3 | 31,000 | 6,000 | 31,000 | 7,750 | 23,250 |
| 4 | 28,000 | 3,000 | 28,000 | 7,000 | 21,000 |

> How to read the table.

Read the first row. The firm pays Tk 25,000 of the loan and Tk 12,000 of interest, which is Tk 37,000. The interest plus the depreciation of Tk 25,000 is Tk 37,000, and 25% of this, Tk 9,250, is saved in tax. The outflow after tax is Tk 37,000 − Tk 9,250 = Tk 27,750. The other rows follow the same rule.

> Step three finds the present values.

*Step 3.* The discount factors at 9% from the table are 0.9174, 0.8417, 0.7722 and 0.7084 for years 1 to 4, and the annuity factor for 4 years is 3.2397. For the lease: Tk 22,500 × 3.2397 = Tk 72,893. For buying: 27,750 × 0.9174 = 25,458; 25,500 × 0.8417 = 21,463; 23,250 × 0.7722 = 17,954; 21,000 × 0.7084 = 14,876. The total is Tk 79,751.

> Step four is the answer and its check.

*Answer.* The present value of the lease is Tk 72,893 and that of buying is Tk 79,751. Leasing costs about Tk 6,858 less, so the firm should lease. *Check.* Before tax, the lessee pays Tk 1,20,000 in rentals, while buying costs Tk 1,00,000 plus Tk 30,000 of interest, which is Tk 1,30,000. Leasing is cheaper before tax as well, so the direction of the answer is sensible.

> Slips that students make.

*Common slips.* First, the whole loan instalment is treated as an expense, but only the interest saves tax. Second, depreciation is forgotten, although it saves tax in every year. Third, the weighted average cost of capital is used as the discount rate, when the after-tax cost of debt is correct. Fourth, the rental is not reduced by tax. Fifth, the two choices are discounted at different rates or over different numbers of years.

> Timing can change the figures.

There are exceptions. Some leases require the rental at the start of each year, and not at the end. Then the payments form an annuity due as in Chapter 5, and each rental is one year closer to today. If the lessee pays for repair and the lessor does not, those costs must be added to the lease side, with their tax effect. If the residual value is uncertain, the owner may wish to test the decision with a lower value to see whether it changes.

> Recap: build both sets of outflows, then compare.

*Before moving on.* After-tax rental is the rental times one less the tax rate. For buying, subtract the tax saved on interest and depreciation from the loan payment, and subtract any after-tax sale value. Discount both at the after-tax cost of debt. The lower present value wins.

> A test with new figures.

*Pause and check.* A lessor asks Tk 20,000 a year for four years, and the after-tax cost of debt is 9%. What is the present value of the after-tax rentals if the tax rate is 25%? A short answer is given at the back of the book.

## Worked Case: The Freezer

> The case file now contains the second example, with equal instalments and a residual value.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* The bakery needs a display freezer that costs Tk 1,50,000 and is expected to last five years. The lessor offers it for Tk 42,000 at the end of each year for five years. The alternative is to borrow Tk 1,50,000 at 12%, repaid in five equal yearly instalments, and to buy. The owner expects to sell the freezer for Tk 10,000 at the end of year 5. The tax rate is 25%, and the after-tax cost of debt is 9%, as found in Chapter 15.^3

^3: Depreciation is taken as a straight line to nil over five years, Tk 30,000 a year. The instalment is calculated as Tk 1,50,000 divided by the annuity factor for 5 years at 12%, 3.6048, which is about Tk 41,611. The schedule is rounded to the nearest taka, so a small balance of Tk 3 remains after the last instalment. Repair and insurance are assumed to be the same under both choices, and so are left out.

> The situation, the question and the layout of the reasoning.

*Situation.* The owner wishes to obtain the freezer at the lowest cost in today's taka. *Question.* Should the owner lease or buy with a loan? *Reasoning.* Follow the four steps. We first find the loan schedule, because the interest in each year is needed for the tax saved.

:table Loan schedule: Tk 1,50,000 at 12%, instalment Tk 41,611 (Tk)
| Year | Interest | Principal repaid | Balance at year end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 18,000 | 23,611 | 1,26,389 |
| 2 | 15,167 | 26,444 | 99,945 |
| 3 | 11,993 | 29,618 | 70,327 |
| 4 | 8,439 | 33,172 | 37,155 |
| 5 | 4,459 | 37,152 | 3 |

> How the schedule is built.

Interest in year 1 is 12% of Tk 1,50,000, which is Tk 18,000. The principal repaid is the instalment less the interest, Tk 41,611 − Tk 18,000 = Tk 23,611. The balance falls to Tk 1,26,389, and interest in year 2 is 12% of that. Each row repeats the rule.

> The tax saved on interest and depreciation is the next step.

Now find the tax saved. Depreciation is Tk 30,000 a year. In year 1, interest plus depreciation is Tk 18,000 + Tk 30,000 = Tk 48,000, and 25% of this is Tk 12,000. The after-tax outflow is the instalment less this saving, Tk 41,611 − Tk 12,000 = Tk 29,611. In year 5 the sale also brings Tk 10,000. This is a taxable gain, because the book value is nil, so after 25% tax the firm keeps Tk 7,500.

:table Buying with a loan: present value of after-tax outflows (Tk)
| Year | Instalment | Tax saved | Sale after tax | Net outflow | Factor at 9% | Present value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 41,611 | 12,000 | 0 | 29,611 | 0.9174 | 27,165 |
| 2 | 41,611 | 11,292 | 0 | 30,319 | 0.8417 | 25,520 |
| 3 | 41,611 | 10,498 | 0 | 31,113 | 0.7722 | 24,025 |
| 4 | 41,611 | 9,610 | 0 | 32,001 | 0.7084 | 22,670 |
| 5 | 41,611 | 8,615 | 7,500 | 25,496 | 0.6499 | 16,570 |
| Total |  |  |  |  |  | 1,15,950 |

> The lease side is a repeated payment.

For the lease, the after-tax rental is 42,000 × (1 − 0.25) = Tk 31,500 each year. Its present value is 31,500 × 3.8897 = Tk 1,22,526, using the annuity factor for 5 years at 9%. If each year is discounted separately with the four-place factors, the total is Tk 1,22,523, and we use this figure.

> Conclusion and check.

*Conclusion.* The present value of buying is Tk 1,15,950 and that of leasing is Tk 1,22,523. Buying with a loan costs Tk 6,573 less in today's taka, so the owner should buy. *Check.* The lease pays slightly more in total, Tk 2,10,000 against Tk 2,08,055 for the instalments, and it gives up the Tk 7,500 residual value. So buying should cost less, as found. The figure below shows the yearly outflows of both choices side by side.

:fig After-tax cash outflow each year, for leasing and for buying with a loan. Buying is lower in year 5 because of the sale of the freezer. | fig16_3.svg

> How to read the figure.

Each pair of bars shows one year. The shaded bar is the lease, which is the same each year at Tk 31,500. The plain bar is buying. Buying costs less than leasing in years 1 to 3, a little more in year 4, and much less in year 5. The lower outflows of the early years are worth more than later ones, because of discounting.

> The rental that would make the choice equal.

How high may the rental be before leasing is the better choice? The total present value of buying is Tk 1,15,950. Leasing costs the present value of 0.75 × rental × 3.8897, which is 2.9173 × rental. Setting these equal gives rental = 1,15,950 ÷ 2.9173, which is about Tk 39,750. A lessor who offers less than this would make leasing the cheaper choice.

> The owner's decision and what else matters.

The lessor asks Tk 42,000, which is above Tk 39,750, so the decision is to buy with the loan. The difference in present value, Tk 6,573, is small against the Tk 1,50,000 price. If the owner values the repair service that a lessor might include, or fears that the freezer may soon be out of date, these matters could outweigh the saving. Numbers guide the choice, but they do not make it alone.

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

A lease lets a lessee use an asset that a lessor owns, in return for lease rentals. An operating lease is short, and the lessor keeps most of the risk. A financial lease is long, and works like a loan. The residual value is the worth of the asset at the end. Firms lease to save cash, to keep choices open, to receive service and to use tax benefits, but the total cost may be higher. The lease-or-buy decision follows four steps: after-tax outflows of the lease, after-tax outflows of buying, present values at the after-tax cost of debt, and a choice of the smaller one.

:box IN SHORT
To compare leasing with buying, find the present value of the after-tax cash outflows of each choice at the after-tax cost of debt. Choose the choice with the smaller present value.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Treating the whole instalment as an expense.

*Treating the whole loan instalment as a tax-saving expense.* Only the interest and the depreciation save tax. The part of the instalment that repays the loan does not.

> Comparing totals without time.

*Comparing the sum of the rentals with the price.* The sum of rentals is paid over several years, and part of the price would be borrowed. Compare present values after tax.

> Using the wrong rate.

*Discounting at the weighted average cost of capital.* Lease rentals and loan payments are fixed claims, so they are discounted at the after-tax cost of debt.

> Believing a lease is free of debt.

*Believing that a lease is not a form of borrowing.* A financial lease is a fixed obligation for years. It adds to financial risk in the same way as a loan, even when the records show it differently.

> Forgetting what is lost.

*Forgetting the residual value.* The lessee gives up the worth of the asset at the end. It belongs in the purchase side of the comparison.

## In History

> A new kind of company began in California.

Ancient records show that rented tools and land are very old. But leasing as the main business of a company is new. In 1952 a company called the United States Leasing Corporation was founded in San Francisco, and it is described as the first company in the United States to treat leasing as its main business. Its founder is named in the records of the equipment finance industry as Henry Schoenfeld. Within a few years other leasing companies appeared, and the idea spread to Europe in the early 1960s.^4
