:chapter XIII | Long-Term Debt Financing
:mood A loan is a promise about the future, and the future keeps its own accounts.
:desc This chapter explains how a firm borrows money for years and not for weeks. It shows why a new firm needs long-term funds, what every source of money asks in return, how term loans, debentures and mortgages differ, and how a repayment schedule and the cost of debt are worked out.

> A longer loan can cost more interest and still be the better loan.

Consider a puzzle. A bank offers the baker Tk 3,00,000 at 12 per cent a year. The baker may repay all of it after one year, or repay it in five equal yearly instalments. The rate is the same in both cases. The five-year loan costs far more interest in total. Why would a sensible baker choose it?

> The answer depends on when the money arrives, not only on how much interest is paid.

The answer depends on a question that the interest figure does not ask: when does the money to repay arrive? A loan is a promise about the future. The borrower promises to hold, on certain days, the money to repay. If the business earns that money later than the lender demands it, the promise cannot be kept, however low the interest. This chapter studies loans of long length, and the way a borrower and a lender fit the promise to the business.

> The chapter relies on annuities, financial leverage and bond features.

*You will need* the annuity tables of Chapter 5, the idea of financial leverage from Chapter 9, and the bond words of Chapter 11: face value, coupon rate and maturity. We apply them to the oven loan of Sundial Bakery.

## 13.1 Why a New Firm Needs Long-Term Funds

> A firm should hold long-lasting assets with long-lasting money.

In Chapter 12 we learned the matching principle: fund long needs with long money. An oven lasts for years, so the money that buys it should be available for years. This is the first reason a firm needs long-term funds. It buys assets that last, and it should not have to repay their cost before they have earned it.

> A new firm earns slowly, so it needs time before money returns.

The second reason is speed. A new firm earns slowly. Customers must learn the shop exists, a baker must learn how much to bake, and the first months often bring losses. A loan that is due in one year may fall due before the firm has learned to earn. A loan of five years gives the business time.

> A new firm has fewer options than an old one.

The third reason concerns a new firm in particular. It has no record of paying debts, and its profit is uncertain, so lenders see it as risky. Short loans must be renewed again and again, and each renewal is a new decision for the lender. If one lender refuses, the firm may be forced to repay at once. A long loan, agreed once, protects the firm from that risk.

> Long-term funds also keep short-term money free for daily work.

The fourth reason follows from Chapter 12. Working capital is needed every day. If a firm uses its cash and overdraft to pay for ovens, it has less money for flour and wages. Long-term funds pay for the long-term assets, and short-term money stays free for daily work.

> A test with numbers compares a one-year loan with five yearly instalments.

We now test the puzzle with numbers. The oven costs Tk 3,00,000 and brings in Tk 1,00,000 a year, as in Chapter 7. For this test, we ignore tax and treat the oven's yearly inflow as cash available to repay. The next table compares what the bakery owes at the end of the first year under each plan. Read each row from left to right. The last column is the difference between what comes in and what must be paid out.

:table Cash due at the end of year 1 against the oven's inflow
| Plan | Due (Tk) | Oven inflow (Tk) | Difference (Tk) |
| Repay all after one year | 3,36,000 | 1,00,000 | minus 2,36,000 |
| Five equal yearly instalments | 83,223 | 1,00,000 | plus 16,777 |

> The one-year loan leaves a gap of Tk 2,36,000; the five-year loan leaves a surplus.

The one-year loan falls due as Tk 3,00,000 plus Tk 36,000 of interest, which is Tk 3,36,000. The oven has brought in only Tk 1,00,000, so the bakery is Tk 2,36,000 short. The five-year plan asks for Tk 83,223 in the first year, which is less than the oven's inflow, so the bakery keeps Tk 16,777. Section 13.5 shows how the instalment of Tk 83,223 is found.

> The longer loan pays more interest in total, which is the price of more time.

The five-year plan costs more interest, Tk 1,16,114 against Tk 36,000. This is the price of time: the borrower keeps the lender's money for longer and pays for each year of keeping it. The baker accepts the larger sum because the smaller sum would have to be paid before the oven could earn it.

> Recap: long-term funds fit long-lived assets and give a new firm time and safety.

*Before moving on.* A firm needs long-term funds to match long-lived assets, to give a new business time to earn, to avoid repeated renewal risk, and to keep short-term money free. You should now be able to say why a larger total of interest can still be the better choice.

## 13.2 What Every Source of Money Asks in Return

@! Go slowly here.

> Every source of money asks for four things in return; four questions reveal them.

Whoever gives money to a firm expects something in return. Before we study individual loans, we need four questions that can be asked of any source of money. We call them claim, cost, control and risk. They apply to a bank, to a supplier and to an owner. Each question has a simple everyday form.

> A claim is the right to be paid, or to share what is left.

@ Claim | A right to be paid a stated sum, or to share in what is left.

The first question is about the **claim**, which means a right to be paid a stated sum, or to share in what is left. A friend who lends Tk 1,000 has a claim for exactly Tk 1,000, plus any agreed interest. A friend who becomes a partner in a business has a claim to a share of the profit, whatever it turns out to be. The first claim is fixed. The second is not.

> Cost is the price the firm pays for using the money.

The second question is about cost: what does the firm pay for using the money? For a loan, the cost is interest and any fees. The third question is about control: what say does the giver of money have in running the business? A bank that lends does not vote on what to bake. An owner usually does. The fourth question is about risk, and it needs more care.

> A fixed charge must be paid whether or not the firm earns a profit.

@ Fixed charge | A payment the firm must make on a set date, whatever its profit.

Risk to the firm arises from the **fixed charge**, which means a payment the firm must make on a set date, whatever its profit. In Chapter 9 the bakery's interest of Tk 7,000 a month was a fixed charge. It was due in a quiet month as well as a busy one, and it made profit swing more widely when sales moved. If the firm fails to pay, the lender may take legal action and may take the collateral, which section 13.4 explains.

> A table compares borrowed money with owners' money on the four questions.

The next table compares the two main kinds of money on the four questions. The first kind is debt, which is money borrowed from lenders. The second is owners' money, which Chapter 14 studies fully. Read down each column to see the character of each kind.

:table Debt and owners' money on four questions
| Question | Debt (lender's money) | Owners' money (see Chapter 14) |
| Claim | A fixed sum, repaid on agreed dates | A share of what is left after all debts |
| Cost | Interest, and fees | A share of profit, which is not fixed |
| Control | None over daily decisions, but promises may limit the firm | Votes and a say in decisions |
| Risk to the firm | High: fixed charges must be paid | Lower: no fixed payment |

> Debt is cheaper and fixed; owners' money is flexible and carries a share of control.

The table shows a balance. Debt gives the firm money without sharing control, and the interest it costs is fixed in advance. But the firm must pay it. Owners' money asks for no fixed payment, but it gives the owners a say and a share. Neither is better for every firm. A wise firm uses both and watches the balance, as Chapters 9 and 15 explain.

> Recap: four questions for any source: claim, cost, control and risk.

*Before moving on.* Ask four questions of any source of money: what is the claim, what is the cost, who has control, and what is the risk? Debt has a fixed claim and a fixed charge. You should now be able to say why debt adds risk even when its interest is low.

## 13.3 Term Loans

> A term loan is a bank loan repaid over several years.

@ Term loan | A bank loan that is repaid over several years.

A **term loan** means a bank loan that is repaid over several years. The word term here means the period of the loan. Term loans are the most common way for a small firm to borrow for an oven, a van or a shop fitting. Chapter 12 named the medium-term and long-term periods; term loans usually run from one year to ten years or more, so they cover both.

> Every term loan has a short list of features.

A term loan has features that are agreed before the money is paid. The first is the amount. The second is the term, which is the number of years until the loan is fully repaid, and the date of full repayment is its maturity, a word we met in Chapter 11. The third is the interest rate, which may stay fixed or may change with market rates. The fourth is the pattern of repayment: regular instalments, or one payment at the end. The fifth is any collateral, and the sixth is any promise the borrower must keep. We study these in section 13.4.

> The lender asks three questions before lending.

Why does the bank lend, and on what conditions? A bank wants three things. It wants a clear purpose, such as an oven that will earn income. It wants a clear source of repayment, which is usually the cash the business earns. And it wants a second source in case the first fails, which is usually collateral. A firm that shows all three has a better chance of a loan on good terms.

> The loan moves through a sequence from request to final payment.

A term loan moves through a sequence. The firm asks for the loan and shows its plans. The bank examines the plans and the firm's accounts. If the bank agrees, both sign a written agreement that states the terms. The bank then pays out the money, and the firm uses it for the stated purpose. The firm repays on the agreed dates until the last payment is made. The details differ between banks and countries, and the borrower should read each document before signing.

> Lenders in Bangladesh include commercial banks and development institutions.

In Bangladesh, term loans are given by commercial banks and by institutions set up to finance long-term projects. Chapter 14 describes these institutions. The loan terms, rates and rules change over time, so any figure in this book is illustrative and should be checked against a lender's current offer.

> Recap: a term loan has an amount, term, rate, repayment pattern, collateral and promises.

*Before moving on.* A term loan is a bank loan repaid over several years, with an agreed amount, term, rate, repayment pattern, collateral and promises. A bank lends when it sees a purpose, a source of repayment and a second source. You should now be able to list the features of a term loan.

## 13.4 Methods of Debt Financing

> A firm may borrow from one bank, from many lenders, or against property.

A firm has more than one way to borrow for the long term. It may borrow from one bank, which is the term loan. It may borrow from many lenders at once, by selling written promises to repay. Or it may borrow against land or a building. This section studies the second and third ways, and then the terms on which any loan is made.

> A debenture is a written promise to repay, backed by the firm's general credit.

@ Debenture | A firm's written promise to repay a loan with interest, backed by the firm's general credit.

A **debenture** means a firm's written promise to repay a loan with interest, backed by the firm's general credit and not by one named asset. A debenture is a kind of bond, so it has the features of Chapter 11: a face value, a coupon rate and a maturity. A firm sells debentures to many lenders, each of whom lends a small part of the total. Debentures suit large, well-known firms. A small bakery would not issue them.^1

> A mortgage is a loan secured on land or a building.

@ Mortgage | A loan secured on property, which the lender may sell if the loan is not repaid.

A **mortgage** means a loan secured on land or a building, which the lender may sell if the loan is not repaid. A baker who wishes to buy the shop in which the bakery works might take a mortgage from a bank. The bank holds a claim on the shop until the last instalment is paid. When the loan is repaid, the claim ends.

> Collateral is an asset pledged to back a loan.

@ Collateral | An asset pledged to the lender, to be sold if the loan is not repaid.

@ Secured loan | A loan backed by collateral.

@ Unsecured loan | A loan not backed by collateral.

The mortgage is one case of a wider idea. **Collateral** means an asset pledged to the lender, to be sold if the loan is not repaid. A **secured loan** is a loan backed by collateral. An **unsecured loan** is a loan not backed by collateral, and its lender relies on the borrower's general credit. Lenders charge less for a secured loan, because their risk is smaller. Some writers say security for collateral, but this book keeps collateral, because Chapter 11 gave security another meaning.^2

> A covenant is a promise by the borrower that limits what the firm may do.

@ Covenant | A promise in a loan agreement that limits what the borrower may do or requires it to do.

Lenders also protect themselves by promises. A **covenant** means a promise in a loan agreement that limits what the borrower may do, or requires it to do. A covenant may require the firm to send its accounts to the bank each year, or to insure the oven. It may forbid the firm to borrow more without the bank's agreement. The firm gives up some freedom, and in return the lender lends on better terms.

> Default is failure to pay as agreed, and the lender's rights then begin.

@ Default | Failure to pay a loan as agreed.

The last word in this group is the one every borrower wishes to avoid. **Default** means failure to pay a loan as agreed. A default allows the lender to use the rights in the agreement: to demand repayment of the whole balance, and to sell the collateral. Covenants are often written so that breaking one counts as a default, even if all payments are on time.

The next table sets the three methods side by side. Read each row for one method. The last column applies the method to Sundial Bakery.

| Debenture | Many lenders | General credit | Large sums for large firms | Not suitable: too small |

*Before moving on.* Methods of long-term debt are the term loan, the debenture and the mortgage. Collateral backs a secured loan, covenants limit the borrower, and default lets the lender act. You should now be able to say which method suits a small firm that buys an oven.

## 13.5 Repayment Schedules and the Cost of Debt

@! Go slowly here.

> Most term loans are repaid in regular instalments, each with two parts.

A student who borrows money for fees might repay a little each month. Each payment does two jobs. It pays the interest for the month, and it reduces the amount still owed. Most term loans work in the same way.

> An instalment loan is repaid by regular payments of interest and principal.

@ Instalment loan | A loan repaid by regular payments, each including interest and part of the amount borrowed.

@ Principal (of a loan) | The amount borrowed that is still owed. In Chapter 2, principal meant the one who hires an agent.

An **instalment loan** means a loan repaid by regular payments, each including interest and part of the amount borrowed. The amount borrowed that is still owed is called the **principal**. Interest is charged on the principal that remains, so as the principal falls, the interest in each instalment falls too.

> A repayment schedule lists every instalment and splits it.

@ Repayment schedule | A table listing each instalment, how much of it is interest, and how much repays the principal.

A **repayment schedule** means a table that lists each instalment, how much of it is interest, and how much repays the principal. A borrower who understands a schedule can see exactly what is owed on any date.

> The instalment is the loan divided by the annuity factor.

How is the instalment found? The instalments form an ordinary annuity, which we studied in Chapter 5. The loan is the present value of those instalments, at the loan's interest rate. In words: instalment equals the loan divided by the present value annuity factor. In symbols: P = L / A, where P is the instalment, L is the loan, and A is the factor for the rate and the number of years.

> A first worked example finds the oven loan's instalment.

*Given.* The loan L is Tk 3,00,000. The rate is 12 per cent a year, and the term is 5 years, with one instalment at the end of each year. The factor table of Chapter 5 gives A as 3.6048. *Find.* The yearly instalment.

> One division gives the instalment.

*Steps.* P equals 3,00,000 divided by 3.6048, which is Tk 83,222.4. A bank rounds an instalment up to the next whole taka, so that the loan is surely repaid, and the instalment is Tk 83,223. *Answer.* The bakery pays Tk 83,223 at the end of each of the five years. *Check.* The schedule below must end at zero.

> The schedule shows interest, principal and the balance, year by year.

The next table is the repayment schedule. Read each row from left to right. The interest is 12 per cent of the opening balance. The principal repaid is the instalment less the interest. The closing balance is the opening balance less the principal repaid, and it becomes the next year's opening balance. In the last year, the instalment is Tk 1 smaller, because of rounding.

The check passes. The closing balance in year 5 is zero, and the principal repaid adds to Tk 3,00,000. Total interest is Tk 1,16,114, which is Tk 4,16,114 paid less Tk 3,00,000 borrowed. Not every loan has equal instalments. Some banks ask the borrower to repay equal parts of the principal each year, and to add the interest on the balance. The total interest is Tk 1,08,000, which is Tk 8,114 less than under equal instalments, because the balance falls faster. But the first payment is Tk 96,000, against Tk 83,223. > The cost of a loan is the interest rate on the balance, before tax.

Now the cost of the debt. In a simple loan with no fees, the cost before tax is the interest rate on the balance, which is 12 per cent a year. A fee paid at the start raises the cost, because the borrower receives less than the amount on which interest is charged. A borrower should always ask for the total of interest and fees, and not the rate alone.

> Interest reduces taxable profit, and so it reduces tax.

@ Tax shield on interest | The tax saved because interest is subtracted from profit before tax is calculated.

A second effect lowers the cost. The firm subtracts interest from its profit before it calculates tax. The tax saved is called the **tax shield on interest**, which means the tax saved because interest is subtracted from profit before tax is calculated. Chapter 3 introduced interest and tax in the profit layout. We use the illustrative tax rate of 25 per cent.^3

> A table compares tax with and without the loan's interest in year 1.

*Given.* In year 1 the firm's profit before interest and tax is Tk 1,00,000, an illustrative figure. The loan's interest for the year is Tk 36,000. *Find.* The tax saved, and the real cost of the interest.

:table Year 1: tax with and without the loan's interest
| Line | Without the interest | With the interest |
| Profit before interest and tax | 1,00,000 | 1,00,000 |
| Interest | 0 | 36,000 |
| Profit before tax | 1,00,000 | 64,000 |
| Tax at 25 per cent | 25,000 | 16,000 |
| Profit after tax | 75,000 | 48,000 |

> The firm saves Tk 9,000 of tax, so Tk 36,000 of interest costs Tk 27,000.

*Steps.* Tax falls from Tk 25,000 to Tk 16,000, a saving of Tk 9,000, which equals 25 per cent of Tk 36,000. Profit after tax falls only from Tk 75,000 to Tk 48,000, a fall of Tk 27,000. *Answer.* Tk 36,000 of interest costs the firm Tk 27,000 after tax. As a rate on Tk 3,00,000, that is 9 per cent. *Check.* In words: after-tax cost equals the rate times one less the tax rate. In symbols: 12 per cent × (1 - 0.25) = 9 per cent. Chapter 15 uses this figure.

> The tax shield works only when the firm has profit to tax.

The tax shield has limits. It works only when the firm has taxable profit. A firm that makes a loss pays no tax, so interest saves it nothing now. It also does not make debt free. The firm still pays Tk 27,000 for Tk 3,00,000 in this year, a cost of 9 per cent. And tax rules change, so the rate and the rules should be verified for the firm's own type.

> Common slips in schedules and cost.

*Common slips.* Students charge interest on the original loan each year instead of on the balance. They forget that the last instalment may differ by rounding. They treat the instalment as all interest or all principal. They apply the tax saving to the whole instalment, when it applies only to the interest. A schedule that does not end at zero has an error.

> Recap: a schedule splits each instalment; after-tax cost is lower than the rate.

*Before moving on.* An instalment loan is repaid by equal instalments, found by dividing the loan by the annuity factor. A schedule shows interest falling and principal rising. The tax shield lowers the cost of 12 per cent to 9 per cent. You should now be able to say why year 5's instalment holds less interest than year 1's.

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas in one paragraph.

A new firm needs long-term funds to match long-lived assets, to give the business time to earn, and to avoid repeated renewal risk. Any source of money can be examined by four questions: claim, cost, control and risk. Debt gives a fixed claim and a fixed charge. A term loan is a bank loan repaid over several years. Debentures are sold to many lenders, and a mortgage is secured on property. Collateral backs a secured loan, covenants limit the borrower, and default lets the lender act. An instalment is found by dividing the loan by the annuity factor, and a repayment schedule splits each instalment between interest and principal. Interest reduces tax, so the cost of debt after tax is the rate times one less the tax rate.

## Where People Go Wrong

> A longer loan is not necessarily a worse loan.

*The loan with the most interest is the worst loan.* Not so. A longer loan pays more interest because it uses the money for longer. If the shorter loan falls due before the asset earns, the shorter loan is the worse choice.

> Instalments are equal, but their parts are not.

*Equal instalments mean equal interest each year.* They do not. Interest is charged on the balance, which falls each year, so the interest part falls and the principal part rises.

> The tax shield does not make debt free.

*The tax shield makes borrowing free.* It does not. The firm saves tax only on the interest, and it still pays the rest. At 25 per cent tax, 12 per cent debt costs 9 per cent.

> A debenture is a loan, not a share.

*The holder of a debenture is an owner.* No. A debenture holder is a lender. The holder has a fixed claim and no vote, unlike an owner.

> Collateral is not a payment.

*Collateral is paid to the lender.* No. Collateral remains the borrower's, and the lender takes it only if the borrower defaults.

## In History

> Railway companies showed how long-term debt can finance very large projects.

In the nineteenth century, railway companies in Britain and elsewhere needed sums far larger than their founders could supply. Alongside shares, many raised money by issuing debentures, which promised fixed interest and were often backed by the company's property and earnings. The method allowed thousands of lenders each to lend a small sum to one firm. It made large, long-lived assets possible. It also showed the risk of fixed charges: companies that did not earn enough from their traffic could not pay the interest, and some fell into financial difficulty. The lesson for this chapter is that long-term debt can build what short-term money cannot, but its promise must be kept.
