:chapter X | Risk Premium, Required Return and the Market
:mood What is the fair price of a chance?
:desc This chapter links risk to the return that investors ask for. It shows how a safe return and an extra reward for risk make up the return a firm must earn, how one number called beta measures the part of risk that is rewarded, and how markets treat news. The ideas prepare the way for valuing securities and for pricing a firm's funds.

> Two offers of money are placed side by side: one is sure, and one is a gamble.

Imagine that you have Tk 1,00,000 to invest for one year. A government offers you a certificate that pays 6 per cent, and the payment is certain. A friend offers you a share in a new business. The friend says the share could pay nothing, or it could pay 24 per cent, and on average it will pay 12 per cent. You must choose. Which would you take?

> Most people ask for more than the sure return before they accept a risk.

Most people would not accept the share if it offered only 6 per cent on average. The certificate gives the same average, and it gives it without worry. Most people would accept the share only if it promised a good deal more. But how much more? Would 7 per cent be enough? Would 12 per cent? The answer is a number, and it is not chosen by feeling. It is built from two parts, each of which this chapter teaches you to find.

> The firm faces the same question as the investor, because investors supply its money.

The firm faces this same question, from the other side. The investors who supply a firm's money are people like you, with a certificate always available as an alternative. If a firm wants their money, it must promise a return that makes the risk worth taking. A project that cannot earn that return should not be started. The return that investors ask for is therefore the standard against which a firm judges its projects.

> The chapter has four parts: the two ingredients, the rule, the number called beta, and how markets treat news.

First we meet the two ingredients of the return that investors ask for. Next we see how risk changes it. Then we meet one number, called beta, and a rule that uses it. Last, we ask a question about markets themselves: do prices already know the news?

> Before this chapter, you should know risk, expected value and the standard deviation.

*You will need:* from Chapter 8, the idea of risk as the spread of possible results, the expected value, the standard deviation, and the two kinds of risk named there, market risk (which means risk that touches all firms) and firm-specific risk (which means risk that touches one firm).

## 10.1 The Risk-Free Return and the Risk Premium

> Lending to a trusted borrower earns little, lending to a stranger needs a bigger reward.

Think about lending money to two people. The first is your bank, which will return your money with a small, known payment. The second is a shop owner you hardly know, who says he will pay you back next year. You would not lend to the shop owner for the bank's small payment. You would ask for more, to pay you for the chance that he may not repay. The extra payment is the heart of this section.

> The risk-free return is what you earn when there is no real chance of loss.

@ Risk-free return | The return on an investment where there is no real chance of loss.

The first part of any required return is the **risk-free return**. It means the return on an investment where there is no real chance of loss. No investment is free of every risk, so in practice people take as the risk-free return the rate on short-term loans to a government that borrows in its own money, such as treasury bills. The government can always create the money it owes. In this book, the risk-free return is the base on which every other return is built.

> The risk-free return is risk-free in taka, but inflation can still reduce what the taka will buy.

There is an exception. The government will repay the taka, but prices may rise in the meantime, so each taka may buy less. A return that is certain in taka is not certain in what it buys. For the purposes of this chapter we set that problem aside and treat the risk-free return as the starting point.

> The risk premium is the extra return asked for carrying extra risk.

@ Risk premium | The extra return that investors ask for carrying extra risk.

The second part is the **risk premium**. It means the extra return that investors ask for carrying extra risk. A safe investment needs no premium. A risky one needs a large premium. Putting the two parts together gives the rule of this section.

> The rule in words and in symbols.

required return = risk-free return + risk premium.

> The symbols used in the rule.

In symbols, let R be the return that investors ask for, Rf the risk-free return, and RP the risk premium. Then R = Rf + RP. We give the name *required return* to the left-hand side in section 10.2.

> First example: small numbers, a clean rule.

*Worked example 1.* *Given.* The risk-free return is 5 per cent a year. Investors ask a risk premium of 3 per cent to hold shares in a shop. *Find.* The return they ask. *Formula.* R = Rf + RP.

> Steps, answer and check.

*Steps.* R = 5% + 3% = 8%. *Answer.* Investors ask 8 per cent a year. *Check.* The answer is more than the risk-free return of 5 per cent, which it must be if the premium is positive, and the extra part is exactly the 3 per cent premium.

> The market return gives a premium for the market as a whole.

@ Market return | The average return earned by all the shares in a market taken together.

Where does a premium come from in practice? Look at the market as a whole. The **market return** means the average return earned by all the shares in a market taken together, which is often measured by an index of many shares. The market is risky, so its return is higher than the risk-free return. The difference between them is the premium for holding the market as a whole: market premium = market return − risk-free return.

> The case file gives illustrative figures for the risk-free return and the market return.

:case Case file, illustrative. In the bakery's story we take, for teaching, a risk-free return of 6 per cent a year and a market return of 12 per cent a year. These are not real market figures. They keep the arithmetic clean.

> Second example: the market premium and a larger premium for a riskier use of money.

*Worked example 2.* *Given.* Rf = 6%; market return = 12%. A new product for the bakery needs a return of 13.2%. *Find.* The market premium, and the premium that the new product must offer. *Formula.* Premium = required return − risk-free return.

> Steps, answer and check.

*Steps.* The market premium is 12% − 6% = 6%. The premium for the new product is 13.2% − 6% = 7.2%. *Answer.* The market premium is 6 per cent, and the new product must offer a premium of 7.2 per cent. *Check.* The new product's premium is larger than the market's, which is reasonable only if the new product is riskier than the market. Section 10.3 shows that it is, by a factor of 1.2.

> The investor, the manager and the lender each use the premium in their own way.

The three seats give three views of the premium. To an investor, the premium is the reward for waiting while the result is uncertain. To a manager, it is a cost that the firm must be able to pay, because it is the price of attracting the investor's money. To a lender, it is the reason that a loan to a risky firm carries a higher rate of interest than a loan to a safe firm. In every case the premium is the price of risk, and the price grows with the amount of risk.

> A change in the safe return moves every required return by the same amount.

The rule also shows what happens when the safe return changes. Suppose the risk-free return rises from 6 per cent to 8 per cent, and the new product's premium stays at 7.2 per cent. Its required return rises from 13.2 per cent to 15.2 per cent. If the safe return falls to 4 per cent, the required return falls to 11.2 per cent. Because the safe return is the base of every required return, a change in it moves the whole ladder up or down together.

> The figure shows the required return as a stack: the safe return, and then the premium.

The figure below shows the idea as a stack. Read it from left to right. The first column is a safe government loan: it has only the risk-free part. The second column is the market as a whole. The third is the new product. In each column, the plain block is the risk-free return and the hatched block is the risk premium. The riskier the use of money, the taller the hatched block.

:fig Required return as a stack: the risk-free return, and a premium that grows with risk. | fig10_stack.svg

> Figures for the risk-free return and the premium change with time and place.

Both parts of the rule change. The risk-free return rises and falls with the economy, with the government's needs, and with the country. The premium changes with how worried investors are. A figure for either one is an estimate for a certain time and place, and must be dated when it is used. The figures in this book are chosen for teaching.

> Before moving on: required return = risk-free return + risk premium.

*Before moving on.* The return that investors ask has two parts: a safe return for waiting, and a premium for carrying risk. You should now be able to say: "If the safe return is 5 per cent and the premium is 4 per cent, investors ask for 9 per cent."

## 10.2 Risk and the Required Return

@! Go slowly here.
> The return that investors ask for has a name, and it is a name for an idea you already know.

@ Required return | The least return an investor will accept to hold an asset, given its risk.

We now give a name to the sum of the two parts. The **required return** means the least return an investor will accept to hold an asset, given its risk. If an asset's expected return is above its required return, investors want it. If it is below, they do not.

> The required return is the same idea as the discount rate of Chapters 4 to 7.

You have met this idea before under another name. In Chapters 4 to 7 we used a *discount rate* to bring later money back to today. That rate is the return that an investor could earn on another use of money of equal risk. It is therefore the required return. From this chapter on, this book uses the name required return, and the two names stand for one idea. In Chapter 7, the least return that a project must earn was called the *hurdle rate*. The hurdle rate is the required return, applied to a project.

> A project passes the test if its expected return is at least the required return.

The rule for decisions follows. Compare the return a project is expected to earn with the required return. If the expected return is at least as large, the project is acceptable. If it is smaller, the project would leave investors worse off than their alternative, and it should be rejected. For the bakery's new product, with a required return of 13.2 per cent, an expected return of 15 per cent passes the test and an expected return of 11 per cent fails it.

> Two ideas with equal expected returns can still deserve different required returns.

Recall the two product ideas of Chapter 8. The cake line and the café corner both had an expected return of 12 per cent, but the standard deviation of the cake line was 2.83 per cent and that of the café corner was 8.49 per cent. The expected returns are equal and the risks are not, so investors would not ask the same return from each. Suppose, as a thought exercise only, that investors required 11 per cent for the cake line and 14 per cent for the café corner. Then the cake line, which is expected to earn 12 per cent, passes its test by one point. The café corner, which is also expected to earn 12 per cent, fails its test by two points. The lesson is that an expected return is judged against a required return that depends on risk, and not against one number for every project. (Section 10.3 will refine which kind of risk counts.)

> Higher risk means a higher required return, as a ladder shows.

The link between risk and required return can be shown as a ladder. The table below goes from the safest use of money at the top to the riskiest at the bottom. Read each row across: the first column names the use of money, the second its risk, and the third the return that investors ask for.

:table The ladder of risk and required return
| Use of money | Risk | Return asked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short-term loan to a government | Lowest | Lowest: the risk-free return |
| Loan to a large, steady firm | Low | A little above the risk-free return |
| Shares in a large, steady firm | Medium | Risk-free return plus a moderate premium |
| Shares in a young firm with uncertain sales | High | Risk-free return plus a large premium |

> The risk that matters is not all risk; spreading money across many firms removes some of it.

There is a puzzle in this ladder. In Chapter 8, risk was measured by the standard deviation, which counts all the spread in a result. But a large part of that spread can be removed by an investor, and an investor is not paid a premium for risk that can be removed for free. We show how in the next paragraphs.

> A small table shows two shares whose swings cancel.

Take two shares. The first belongs to a firm that sells umbrellas, and the second to a firm that sells ice cream. In a rainy season the umbrella share returns 16 per cent and the ice-cream share returns 4 per cent. In a sunny season the returns are the other way round. Each season has an equal chance, 0.5. The table below gives the returns for each share, and for an investor who puts half of her money in each.

:table Returns on two shares, and on half of each (per cent)
| Season | Umbrella share | Ice-cream share | Half in each |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rainy | 16 | 4 | 10 |
| Sunny | 4 | 16 | 10 |
| Expected value | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| Standard deviation | 6 | 6 | 0 |

> Holding both gives a steady 10 per cent although each share alone swings.

@ Diversification | Spreading money over many investments, so that the ups of some cancel the downs of others.

Each share alone has an expected value of 10 per cent and a standard deviation of 6 per cent. The investor who holds half of each earns 10 per cent in either season, and her standard deviation is zero. This spreading of money over many investments is called **diversification**. Diversification means spreading money over many investments so that the ups of some cancel the downs of others. In the real world the cancelling is never complete, but the effect is large.

> Diversification removes firm-specific risk, but not market risk.

Diversification can remove firm-specific risk, which is the risk that touches one firm, such as a fire in its factory or a lost contract. It cannot remove market risk, which touches all firms together, such as a general fall in demand. An investor who holds many shares can dilute the first kind of risk, and she keeps the second. She is therefore paid a premium for market risk only. This is a deep idea, and it prepares the next section: we need a number that measures only the market risk of a share.

> Before moving on: the required return is the discount rate; the premium pays for the risk that cannot be spread away.

*Before moving on.* The required return is the least return investors accept, given the risk. It is the same idea as the discount rate. Investors are paid for market risk, which diversification cannot remove, and not for firm-specific risk. You should now be able to say: "A project whose expected return is below the required return should be rejected."

## 10.3 Beta and the Capital Asset Pricing Model

@! Go slowly here.
> One number tells how strongly a share moves with the market.

Suppose the market as a whole rises by 10 per cent in a year. Some shares will rise by more than 10 per cent, and some by less. Some may fall. A share that tends to move with the market, and more strongly, carries more market risk than a share that moves weakly. We need a single number that records this tendency.

> Beta measures how strongly a security moves with the market.

@ Beta | A number showing how strongly a security's return moves with the market's return.

That number is **beta**. Beta means a number that shows how strongly the return on a security moves with the return on the market. A beta of 1.0 means that the security moves, on average, as the market does. A beta of 1.5 means that for each 1 per cent move in the market, the security tends to move by 1.5 per cent in the same direction. A beta of 0.5 means that it moves by one-half per cent. A beta of zero means that it does not follow the market at all, as with a safe government loan.

> An example: what a beta of 1.2 means in a falling market.

For example, a share with a beta of 1.2 would be expected, on average, to rise by 12 per cent when the market rises by 10 per cent, and to fall by 12 per cent when the market falls by 10 per cent. The words "on average" and "expected" matter. Beta describes a tendency found in past data, and it does not forecast any single year.

> A small table shows what each beta suggests when the market falls by 10 per cent.

The table below shows what several betas suggest when the market falls by 10 per cent. Read each row across: the beta, then the average move that a share with that beta tends to make. Betas below 1.0 fall by less than the market, and betas above 1.0 fall by more.

:table What each beta suggests when the market falls by 10 per cent
| Beta | Average move of the share (%) |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 |
| 0.5 | −5 |
| 1.0 | −10 |
| 1.5 | −15 |

> Beta is estimated from past data, and its value is usually taken from a data service.

Where does beta come from? It is found by statistics, from past returns of the security and of the market. This book does not ask you to compute it. A share's beta is published by many data services, and a manager usually takes it from there. A small firm such as the bakery has no listed shares, so it has no beta of its own. A manager then uses the beta of similar listed firms as an estimate.

> The capital asset pricing model turns beta into a required return.

@ Capital asset pricing model (CAPM) | A rule that finds the required return from the risk-free return, the market premium and beta.

Beta tells us how much of the market premium a security should earn. The **capital asset pricing model (CAPM)** is a rule that finds the required return from the risk-free return, the market premium and beta. In words:

> The CAPM formula in words and symbols.

required return = risk-free return + beta × (market return − risk-free return).

> The symbols used in the CAPM formula.

In symbols, R = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf), where R is the required return, Rf is the risk-free return, β (the Greek letter beta) is the security's beta, and Rm is the market return. The part in brackets is the market premium of section 10.1. The rule says: take the market premium, multiply by beta, and add the risk-free return.

> First CAPM example, with clean numbers.

*Worked example 3.* *Given.* Rf = 5%; Rm = 10%; β = 1.5. *Find.* The required return. *Formula.* R = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf).

> Steps, answer and check for the first CAPM example.

*Steps.* The market premium is 10% − 5% = 5%. Multiply by beta: 1.5 × 5% = 7.5%. Add the risk-free return: 5% + 7.5% = 12.5%. *Answer.* The required return is 12.5 per cent. *Check.* A beta above 1.0 should give a required return above the market return of 10 per cent, and 12.5 is above 10.

> The bakery's new product, the case file figures.

:case Case file, illustrative. Sundial Bakery considers a new product. For teaching, take a risk-free return of 6 per cent, a market return of 12 per cent, and, for similar listed firms, a beta of 1.2. These figures are illustrative.

> Second CAPM example, with the bakery's figures.

*Worked example 4.* *Given.* Rf = 6%; Rm = 12%; β = 1.2. *Find.* The required return of the new product. *Formula.* R = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf).

> Steps, answer and check for the second CAPM example.

*Steps.* The market premium is 12% − 6% = 6%. Multiply by beta: 1.2 × 6% = 7.2%. Add the risk-free return: 6% + 7.2% = 13.2%. *Answer.* The required return is 13.2 per cent. *Check.* With a beta of 1.0, the answer would be the market return of 12 per cent; the beta of 1.2 adds 1.2 − 1.0 = 0.2 of the 6 per cent premium, which is 1.2 per cent, and 12% + 1.2% = 13.2%. The two methods agree.

> A messier example, with a beta below one.

*Worked example 5.* A second security has a beta of 0.8, with the same figures. The market premium is 6%. 0.8 × 6% = 4.8%. The required return is 6% + 4.8% = 10.8%. *Check.* A beta below 1.0 should give a required return between the risk-free return of 6% and the market return of 12%, and 10.8 lies between them.

> The decision: compare the expected return of the new product with 13.2 per cent.

The decision for the bakery follows from section 10.2. If the new product is expected to earn 15 per cent, it passes the test, because 15 is above 13.2. If it is expected to earn only 11 per cent, it fails, because 11 is below 13.2. The result is the discount rate for the new product, and it is the figure that Chapter 15 will use when the bakery retests its projects.

> If the market return changes, the required return changes by beta times the change.

The required return depends on the market premium, and the market premium changes with the market return. Keep the risk-free return at 6 per cent and the beta at 1.2. If the market return is 10 per cent, the premium is 4 per cent and the required return is 6% + 1.2 × 4% = 10.8%. If the market return is 14 per cent, the premium is 8 per cent and the required return is 6% + 1.2 × 8% = 15.6%. The new product's required return moves by 1.2 times the change in the market premium, which is what a beta of 1.2 says it should do.

> The security market line shows the required return for every beta.

@ Security market line | A line that shows the required return for each value of beta.

If we calculate the required return for several betas, a pattern appears. The table below does this with the case file figures. The **security market line** means a line that shows the required return for each value of beta. It starts at the risk-free return, where beta is zero, and rises in a straight line.

| 0 | 6 + 0 × 6 | 6.0 |
| 0.5 | 6 + 0.5 × 6 | 9.0 |
| 1.0 | 6 + 1.0 × 6 | 12.0 |
| 1.5 | 6 + 1.5 × 6 | 15.0 |
| 2.0 | 6 + 2.0 × 6 | 18.0 |

The figure below draws the line. To read it, find a beta on the bottom edge, go straight up to the line, and read across to the left edge. The first dot is the risk-free return, at beta zero. > Beta is not the same as the standard deviation, and it has limits.

*Limits and common confusions.* Beta measures only market risk. The standard deviation of Chapter 8 measures all risk. A share with a high standard deviation may have a low beta if its swings do not follow the market, and the CAPM would then ask for a low premium. Beta is also an estimate from the past, and different data services give different values. The risk-free return and the market return are estimates too. For these reasons the answer from the CAPM is a guide and not a fact.

> Before moving on: required return = risk-free return + beta × market premium.

*Before moving on.* Beta measures how strongly a security moves with the market. The CAPM turns beta into a required return by multiplying the market premium by beta and adding the risk-free return. You should now be able to say: "With a risk-free return of 6 per cent, a market return of 12 per cent and a beta of 1.2, the required return is 13.2 per cent."

## 10.4 Efficient Markets

> A puzzle about news: why do prices move so fast?

Suppose a large firm announces at nine in the morning that it has won a very big contract. By ten, its share price has risen. By eleven, it has stopped rising. Can you, reading the news at noon, still buy the share at a bargain? Most people who study markets would say you cannot, because the price already knows the news. This idea has a name.

> An efficient market is one where prices already reflect the information available.

@ Efficient market | A market in which prices already reflect the information that is available.

An **efficient market** means a market in which prices already reflect the information that is available. In such a market, a share price is a fair summary of what is known. Nobody can find a bargain by using information that the price has already absorbed. Because information is of different kinds, three forms of efficiency are described.

> There are three forms of efficiency, which differ in the information the price has absorbed.

The *weak form* says that prices already reflect all past prices and trading figures. If it is true, studying charts of old prices cannot earn extra profit. The *semi-strong form* says that prices reflect all public information, which includes company reports, news and government announcements. If it is true, reading public news after it appears cannot earn extra profit. The *strong form* says that prices reflect all information, including private information that only insiders hold. If it is true, even insiders cannot profit.

> The figure shows the three forms as circles inside one another.

The figure below shows the three forms as circles, one inside the other. Read it from the middle. The inner circle is the weak form, which covers the least information. The middle ring adds all public information. The outer ring adds private information. A market that is efficient in a wider form is also efficient in the narrower forms inside it.

:fig The three forms of market efficiency, each containing the one inside it. | fig10_efficiency.svg

> A table sets out what each form says and what it would mean for an investor.

The table below sets out the three forms. Read each row across: the form, the information that the price has absorbed, and what the form means for an investor who hopes to earn an extra profit.

:table The three forms of an efficient market
| Form | Prices reflect | Meaning for an investor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weak | Past prices and trading | Studying old price charts gives no extra profit |
| Semi-strong | All public information | Reading published news, after it appears, gives no extra profit |
| Strong | All information, even private | Not even insiders earn an extra profit |

> Most people doubt the strong form, and the use of private information is controlled by law.

The strong form is the hardest to believe. Insiders often do seem to know things that outsiders do not, and using such secret information to trade is forbidden in many countries. Most writers therefore treat the weak and the semi-strong forms as the useful ones. Even these are tested against evidence, and the results differ between markets and between periods.

> Efficiency matters to the manager because the share price is a fair judge of what the firm does.

Why does a financial manager need this idea? If the market is efficient, the share price reflects what the market knows about the firm. A manager cannot raise the price by hiding bad news, because the market will uncover it, and cannot gain by guessing the best time to sell shares, because the price already allows for what is known. What raises the price is a better firm. This agrees with the goal studied in Chapter 2.

> If markets are efficient, a share's price will stay close to the value that its future payments justify.

The idea of efficiency prepares the next chapter. In Chapter 11 we value a security as the present value of what it will pay, discounted at its required return. If the market is efficient, the price in the market and the value found in that way will be close to each other, because the market has already absorbed what is known about the future payments. A large gap between the two would be a puzzle that needs an explanation, such as new information or an error in the estimates.

> Efficient does not mean perfect: prices can be wrong in hindsight, and some markets are less efficient.

*Common confusions.* An efficient market does not mean that prices never fall, or that the price is always right when looked at later. It means that no easy extra profit can be found by using information the price has already absorbed. A market with few traders and little public information is more likely to fall short of efficiency than a large and closely watched one. The efficient-market idea and the CAPM are separate ideas, and a student may accept one without the other.

> Before moving on: an efficient market's prices already reflect the information at the level of its form.

*Before moving on.* In an efficient market, prices already reflect available information. The weak, semi-strong and strong forms differ in the kind of information. You should now be able to say: "If the semi-strong form is true, I cannot earn an extra profit by reading a company's published report after it appears."

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas in a few lines.

The return that investors ask for has two parts: the risk-free return and a risk premium. This required return is the same idea as the discount rate, and applied to a project it is the hurdle rate. Investors are paid for market risk, because diversification can remove firm-specific risk. Beta measures how strongly a security moves with the market. The CAPM gives the required return as R = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf), and the security market line draws the rule. With Rf = 6%, Rm = 12% and β = 1.2, the bakery's new product has a required return of 13.2%. An efficient market is one where prices already reflect available information, in the weak, semi-strong or strong form.

A firm considers a project with a beta of 0.9. The risk-free return is 7 per cent and the market return is 13 per cent. The project is expected to earn 12.5 per cent. Find the required return and say whether to accept. Then suppose that a different data service says the beta is 1.0, and decide again. ## Where People Go Wrong

> Five common misunderstandings and their corrections.

*"A risk-free return has no risk at all."* It has no real chance of loss in taka, but prices may rise and reduce what the taka buy.

> A premium is asked for, not promised.

*"A risk premium is a guaranteed extra."* It is the extra return investors ask for carrying risk. It is an expected reward, and some years it is not earned.

> Beta is a different measure from the standard deviation.

*"Beta measures all of a share's risk."* Beta measures only the risk that moves with the market. The standard deviation measures the whole spread.

> A high beta means high market sensitivity, not a poor firm.

*"A high beta means a bad firm."* A high beta means that the share moves strongly with the market. Investors ask a higher return for it. The firm may be sound.

> Efficiency does not mean that prices are never wrong.

*"An efficient market means prices are always right."* It means that no easy extra profit can be found from information the prices already reflect.

## In History

> A rule for the price of risk, and a way of sorting the evidence on markets.

Before the 1960s, investors knew that both risk and return mattered, but they had no accepted rule for how much extra return a given risk deserved. In 1964 William Sharpe published a paper in the *Journal of Finance* that supplied such a rule. It is the model we call the CAPM.^1 In 1970 Eugene Fama published a review of the evidence on how prices respond to information, in the same journal, and he sorted the question into the three forms of efficiency that this chapter describes.^2 Both men later received the Nobel memorial prize in economic sciences: Sharpe in 1990, and Fama in 2013.^3

## Sources

> The two landmark papers behind this chapter.

Sharpe, W. F. (1964). *Capital asset prices: a theory of market equilibrium under conditions of risk.* Journal of Finance, 19(3). It proposed the model of required return that rests on beta.

> The second landmark paper.

Fama, E. F. (1970). *Efficient capital markets: a review of theory and empirical work.* Journal of Finance, 25(2). It set out the weak, semi-strong and strong forms.

## Review Questions

> Practice questions, with short answers at the back of the book.

> Practice question 1.
1. Define the risk-free return and the risk premium, and say how they combine into the return that investors ask for.

> Practice question 2.
2. The risk-free return is 5 per cent. Investors ask a premium of 4.5 per cent to hold the shares of a small firm. Find the required return.

> Practice question 3.
3. The risk-free return is 7 per cent and the market return is 13 per cent. Find the market premium. Then find the required return for shares with a beta of 0.5, of 1.4, and of 0.

> Practice question 4.
4. A share has a beta of 0.8. The risk-free return is 6 per cent and the market return is 12 per cent. Find the required return and the risk premium of this share.

> Practice question 5.
5. A firm with a required return of 13.4 per cent is offered a project that is expected to earn 13 per cent. Should it accept? Give your reason.

> Practice question 6.
6. Explain what a beta of 1.5 tells an investor. If the market falls by 8 per cent, what fall would you expect in the share, on average?

> Practice question 7.
7. Explain why an investor who holds many shares asks a premium for market risk and not for firm-specific risk.

> Practice question 8.
8. State the three forms of an efficient market and the information each one covers. Which form would be broken by an investor who earns an extra profit from secret news before it is announced?

> Practice question 9.
9. Explain how the discount rate of Chapters 4 to 7 relates to the required return of this chapter.

> Practice question 10.
10. Discuss why a manager should use a required return that depends on risk, and not one rate for every project.
