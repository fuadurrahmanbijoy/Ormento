## The Opening Puzzle

> A baker has a plan and some savings, but the savings are not enough.

A baker in Dhaka wishes to open a shop that sells bread, buns and cakes. The shop needs ovens, tables, flour, sugar, rent and workers. The baker has saved a large sum over many years, but the sum is smaller than the cost of everything on the list. Two questions follow at once. Where can the rest of the money come from? And what must the baker offer to the people who provide it?

> The third question begins on the first morning: how should the money be used?

A third question appears on the first morning. Once the money is in hand, how should it be used? A shop that buys too many ovens has no money left for flour. A shop that buys too much flour may be unable to pay the rent. Every taka can be used in only one way at a time, so each choice closes the other choices. Cooking skill cannot solve this problem; it is a problem about money.

> These three questions together are the subject of finance, which Section 1.4 defines.

Finding money, using money, and making sure that it lasts: these three tasks are the heart of *finance*. Section 1.4 gives the exact definition. This book explains how a business performs these tasks well. We begin in an ordinary place, with the money in a single person's life, because every business idea in this chapter is a larger copy of a personal one.

> The bakery in this book is invented, so that every idea can have exact numbers.

Throughout the book we follow one illustrative business, *Sundial Bakery* of Dhaka. It is not a real firm. Its dates and figures are invented so that each idea can be shown with exact numbers. Its records appear in paragraphs headed *Case file, illustrative*, and the figures in them are carried forward from chapter to chapter.

> The book has a few regular features, and you should learn to use them now.

A few features of the book will help you from the first page. A short note in the side column repeats the main point of each paragraph. A word in bold type is a new term, and it is bold only once, where it is defined. A small raised number points to a footnote at the foot of the page. At the end of a section you will find *Before moving on*, a recap in two or three lines. In this chapter the pace is slow, and a note in the side column will tell you when to go especially slowly.

## 1.1 Money in Daily Life

> We begin with money as every person meets it: coming in, going out, kept, or borrowed.

@! Go slowly here. The words are everyday words, but each receives a precise meaning.

Think of a student who receives Tk 5,000 from the family each month. The student buys food, pays for travel and buys books. Before we say anything about business, we need exact words for what happens to this money. Each word below will be used in the same way throughout the book.

> Money is whatever people accept in payment.

@ Money | What people accept in payment for goods and services.

We begin with **money**. Money means what people accept in payment for goods and services. *Goods* are things that can be touched, such as bread. *Services* are work done for others, such as cutting hair. In Bangladesh the money is the taka, written Tk. A note, a coin and a balance in a bank account all count as money, because a shop will accept each of them when you pay.

> Income is money coming in, and expense is money going out.

@ Income | Money coming in.
@ Expense | Money going out.

Money moves in two directions. **Income** means money coming in. The student's Tk 5,000 each month is income. **Expense** means money going out. When the student pays Tk 800 for a bus pass, the Tk 800 is an expense. Notice the point of view. The same payment of Tk 800 is an expense for the student and income for the bus company.

> Saving is the income that is not spent and is kept for later.

@ Saving | Keeping money for later use.

Suppose that the student spends Tk 4,000 this month. Then Tk 1,000 of the income remains. If the student keeps it, we say that the student makes a **saving**. Saving means keeping money for later use. To find the saving, subtract the expense from the income. Here the saving is Tk 5,000 less Tk 4,000, which is Tk 1,000.

> Fig. 1 shows the month as bars: the money in, and where it went.

Fig. 1 shows this month. This is the first figure of the book, so we shall read it slowly. The upper bar is the income, Tk 5,000. The lower bar has the same total length, but it is cut into two parts. The long plain part is the expense, Tk 4,000. The short shaded part is the saving, Tk 1,000. The length of each part matches its amount, so a part that looks four times longer is four times larger.

:fig One student's month: the income is divided between expense and saving. | figs/fig1_month.svg

> The table states the same facts as numbers.

The table below states the same facts as numbers. Each row is one fact about the month, and the last column shows how the facts connect.

:table One student's month, in taka
| Item | Amount (Tk) | How it is found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income | 5,000 | Given |
| Expense | 4,000 | Given |
| Saving | 1,000 | Income less expense: 5,000 less 4,000 |

> Saving is not only for students; every household and every firm can do it.

Saving belongs to every household, and later to every firm. A family that earns Tk 30,000 in a month and spends Tk 27,000 saves Tk 3,000. A family that spends Tk 32,000 in the same month has an expense larger than its income. It cannot save. To pay its bills, it must use earlier savings, or it must borrow.

> A person who wants something costly may keep saving, or may borrow.

Now suppose that the student wishes to buy a laptop for Tk 40,000. At Tk 1,000 a month, the saving would take forty months. There is another way. The student can ask someone to provide the Tk 40,000 now and agree to return it later.

> Borrowing is taking money now and agreeing to repay it later.

@ Borrowing | Taking money now and agreeing to repay it.

This way is called **borrowing**. Borrowing means taking money now and agreeing to repay it. The money itself is called a **loan** when we think of it from the lender's side, and a loan means a sum of money lent for repayment. The lender takes a risk, because the borrower may fail to repay. The lender also loses the use of that money during the loan. For both reasons, lenders usually ask for something extra.

> A bank is the most familiar place that lends and keeps money.

A **bank** is an institution that keeps people's money safely and lends money to others. Most people meet money's price at a bank. When a person deposits savings, the bank pays a small reward. When a person borrows, the bank charges a larger fee. The difference pays the bank's work and its risk.

> Interest is the fee for using another person's money.

@ Interest | A fee for using someone's money. In daily life, interest also means curiosity.

That extra payment is called **interest**. Interest means a fee for using someone's money. Suppose that a friend lends you Tk 1,000 and asks for Tk 1,100 after one year. The interest is Tk 100. We state interest as a rate: Tk 100 for each Tk 1,000 is 10 taka for each 100 taka, which we call 10 per cent a year.^1

^1: The words *per cent* mean "for each hundred". Chapter 3 treats percentages fully. Until then, we use only rates for which the sum is easy to follow.

> In everyday speech, interest can also mean curiosity, but not in this book.

In everyday speech, "interest" can also mean curiosity, as in "an interest in music". In this book the word always means the fee for using money. It will appear in every chapter after this one.

> This first worked example finds the interest on a clean sum.

Let us calculate interest in the standard order that the whole book will use. We list what is given, state what we must find, write the rule, perform the steps, state the answer and check it.

> The data given and the question to answer.

*Given.* A sum of Tk 10,000 is borrowed for one year at 10 per cent a year.
*Find.* The interest for the year.

> The rule says that interest is the sum times the yearly rate.

*Rule.* In words: interest equals the sum borrowed multiplied by the rate for the year. In symbols, I = P x r. Here I stands for interest, P stands for the sum borrowed, and r is the rate written as a decimal.
*Steps.* Ten per cent is 10 for each 100, which is 0.10. So I = 10,000 x 0.10 = Tk 1,000.

> The answer, and a check that it is sensible.

*Answer.* The interest for the year is Tk 1,000. The borrower repays Tk 11,000 in all.
*Check.* Tk 10,000 contains 100 hundreds. Each hundred pays Tk 10. So 100 x 10 = Tk 1,000. The two methods agree.

> A second example uses a less round sum.

*A second example.* Given: Tk 25,000 is borrowed for one year at 12 per cent. Rate as a decimal: 0.12. Interest: 25,000 x 0.12 = Tk 3,000. The borrower repays Tk 28,000. Check: Tk 25,000 holds 250 hundreds, and 250 x 12 = 3,000.

> Common slips with interest come from the rate and the time.

*Common slips.* A learner may forget that the rate is for one year, and then use it for six months or five years. Another may write 12 per cent as 12 instead of 0.12, and so arrive at an interest larger than the loan. Always write the rate as a decimal before multiplying, and always check whether the answer is sensible. Interest of Tk 3,00,000 on a loan of Tk 25,000 is not.

> Before moving on: income comes in, expense goes out, saving is kept, and a borrower repays the loan plus interest.

*Before moving on.* Income is money coming in, and expense is money going out. What remains and is kept is saving. A borrower repays the loan and also pays interest. These ideas return in larger form in every business.

> The one sentence for the reader to test is stated here.

*You should now be able to say:* "Interest is what I pay for using another person's money."

> The pause question checks that the reader can separate income from a loan.

*Pause and check.* A student receives Tk 5,000 from home and also borrows Tk 2,000 from a friend. Is the Tk 2,000 income? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

## 1.2 Owners, Lenders and the Firm

> A student's money is personal; a business is a different kind of money user.

A student's money is personal. A business uses money in a larger and more complicated way, and it has special words of its own. We meet them one at a time, using the bakery.

> A business is an activity that sells goods or services to earn money.

@ Business | An activity that sells goods or services to earn money.

A **business** means an activity that sells goods or services to earn money. A tea stall is a business. A large factory is a business. In both cases, someone must pay for the equipment and materials before the first sale.

> When we think of a business as one money-using unit, we call it a firm.

@ Firm | A business seen as one money-using unit.

When we wish to look at a business as one unit that collects and spends money, we call it a **firm**. A firm means a business seen as one money-using unit. The words *business* and *firm* name nearly the same thing. We use *business* when we think about the activity and *firm* when we think about its money.

> The owner is the person whose money and risk the business carries.

@ Owner | The person whose money and risk the business carries.

Every firm has at least one **owner**. An owner means the person whose money and risk the business carries. If the firm earns well, the owner gains. If the firm loses, the owner is the first to lose. The owner of Sundial Bakery is one person who has chosen to use personal savings in the firm.

> Money put in to start or run a business is called capital.

@ Capital | Money put in to start or run a business. In daily life, a capital is also a country's chief city.

The money that the owner invests in the firm is called **capital**. Capital means money put in to start or run a business. In this book it does not mean a city. Capital is the first thing that a firm needs, and it is the thing the owner may not have enough of.

> Large firms have many owners, who hold shares in a company.

@ Company | A business that the law treats as a separate unit, owned through shares.
@ Share | One equal part of the ownership of a company.

Large firms often have many owners. Such a firm is usually organised as a **company**. A company means a business that the law treats as a separate unit, owned through shares. A **share** means one equal part of the ownership of a company. If a company has 1,000 shares and you hold 10 of them, you own one hundredth of it. Chapter 2 returns to this idea.

> An investor puts money in to earn more; a lender is one kind of investor.

@ Investor | A person who puts money in, hoping to earn more.
@ Lender | One who lends money for repayment plus interest.

An **investor** means a person who puts money into a firm, hoping to earn more later. Owners are investors. There is a second kind. A **lender** means a person or an institution that lends money for repayment plus interest. A bank that lends to the bakery is a lender. We now have two ways to bring money into a firm. They differ in an important way, which we meet next.

> Sundial Bakery opens with money from two sources, the owner and a bank.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 2 January 2026 Sundial Bakery opens in Dhaka. The owner contributes Tk 6,00,000 of personal savings.^2 The bank lends Tk 4,00,000 at 12 per cent a year. The firm buys ovens and equipment for Tk 7,00,000. The cash left in the firm is Tk 3,00,000.

^2: Amounts follow the South Asian custom of grouping: Tk 6,00,000 means six lakh, or six hundred thousand. One lakh is 1,00,000.

> What the firm owns is an asset, and what it owes is a liability.

@ Asset | Something the business owns that has value.
@ Liability | An amount the business owes.

Examine what the bakery now has and what it owes. The ovens and the cash are **assets**. An asset means something the business owns that has value. The bank loan is a **liability**. A liability means an amount the business owes. The firm owns Tk 10,00,000 of assets and owes Tk 4,00,000.

> Debt is money owed to a lender; equity is what remains for the owner.

@ Debt | Money owed to a lender.
@ Equity | The owners' share of what the business is worth.

Another word for the bank's loan is **debt**. Debt means money owed to a lender. The owner's part is called **equity**. Equity means the owners' share of what the business is worth. We find it by taking the assets and subtracting the debts. Here it is Tk 10,00,000 less Tk 4,00,000, which is Tk 6,00,000. This equals what the owner contributed.

> A claim is a right to receive money from the firm, and the two kinds of investor hold different claims.

@ Claim | A right to receive money from the firm.

Lenders and owners both hold a **claim** on the firm. A claim means a right to receive money from the firm. The two claims are very different, and the table below shows how. Read each row from left to right.

:table Two kinds of claim on a firm
| Feature | Lender (debt) | Owner (equity) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is promised | Repayment and interest | A share of what remains |
| Is the amount fixed? | Yes, as agreed | No, it rises and falls |
| Who is paid first? | The lender | The owner, after the lender |
| Who bears more risk? | Less | More |
| Who directs the firm? | Usually not | Yes |

> A short example shows how the lender is paid first.

The row "Who is paid first?" needs an example. Suppose that, after a bad year, Sundial Bakery must close, and it sells all its assets. The money received is shared in a fixed order. The bank, as lender, receives its Tk 4,00,000 first. The owner receives what remains. To keep the example simple, we ignore interest.

:table What each investor receives if Sundial closes and sells its assets
| Assets are sold for | Bank receives | Owner receives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tk 10,00,000 | Tk 4,00,000 | Tk 6,00,000 |
| Tk 8,00,000 | Tk 4,00,000 | Tk 4,00,000 |
| Tk 3,00,000 | Tk 3,00,000 | Nothing |

> The bank's amount is steady while the owner's amount changes with the sale.

Read down the table. The bank receives Tk 4,00,000 in the first two rows, whatever the sale brings, while the owner's share falls from Tk 6,00,000 to Tk 4,00,000. In the last row the assets bring only Tk 3,00,000. The bank takes all of it and loses Tk 1,00,000, and the owner receives nothing. The lender is protected first, but not always completely.

> Because the lender is paid first, the lender earns less and the owner has the right to decide.

This order explains the whole table. The lender is repaid first and receives a fixed amount, so the lender's risk is smaller, and the reward is smaller too. The owner receives what remains. That may be much more than the bank receives, or nothing. Because the owner carries more risk, the owner also holds the right to direct the firm.

> Each person looks at a firm's money from a different seat.

*From three seats.* The owner hopes the firm will grow in value. The lender wishes only for repayment and interest and will refuse to lend if the risk seems too large. An employee wants wages paid on time and does not mind whether the owner's equity is large, as long as the firm continues. A good decision about money cannot please all three completely, but it must keep the firm able to meet all three.

> Before moving on: assets are paid for by debt and equity, and the lender is paid first.

*Before moving on.* A business earns money by selling goods or services, and a firm is that business seen as a money-using unit. The money comes from owners (equity) and lenders (debt). What the firm owns are its assets, and what it owes are its liabilities. Assets less debt leaves equity.

> The pause question tests the vocabulary of the section.

*Pause and check.* The firm buys a van with Tk 2,00,000 of the bank's money. Is the van an asset or a liability? Is the new loan an asset or a liability?

## 1.3 Where a Firm's Money Comes From and Goes

> A firm's money moves in a loop, and a picture shows the loop.

@! Go slowly here. This picture is the whole book in miniature.

A firm's money does not rest. It comes in, it is spent, it returns, and it goes out again. Fig. 2 shows this loop. Before reading any word in it, notice the shape: five boxes joined by arrows, and the arrows form a circle. A box is a party that holds or uses money. An arrow shows the direction in which the money moves, and the number on it shows the order.

:fig The loop of a firm's money: from investors, through the firm, to its assets, to sales, and back. | figs/fig2_flow.svg

> Step 1 is money moving from investors toward the firm.

Begin at the top left, with the investors. The owners and lenders have money, and they wish to place it in a firm, hoping to earn more. Arrow 1 shows the money moving toward the firm.

> Between investors and firms stands the financial market.

@ Financial market | A place or system where money is lent, borrowed or invested.

Between the investors and the firm stands a **financial market**. A financial market means a place or system where money is lent, borrowed or invested. A bank is the simplest example, because it brings savers and borrowers together. Larger markets bring many investors and many firms together. Chapters 11 and 14 describe them.

> Step 2 is the firm spending the money on assets.

Arrow 2 moves downward. The firm spends the money it received on assets: ovens, flour, a shop. After this step the firm holds things instead of money. This explains why a firm that has plenty of equipment may have almost no cash. Here *cash* means money that can be spent at once, whether in notes, in coins or in a bank account.

> Step 3 is the sale of goods and services, which brings money back.

@ Sales | Money earned from selling.

Arrow 3 moves to the left. The assets are used to make goods and services, and the firm sells them. The money received from customers is the firm's **sales**. Sales means money earned from selling. Other books say *revenue* or *turnover* for the same thing. This book always says *sales*, so that one idea has one word.

> Making the goods costs money, and the word cost has an everyday meaning too.

@ Cost | What is paid to get or make something. In daily life, a cost can mean a price.

Making goods is not free. Flour, sugar, fuel and wages must be paid. Such a payment is a **cost**. A cost means what is paid to get or make something. In everyday speech a person may say that a laptop "costs" Tk 40,000, using the word for a price. In this book, cost means what the firm pays in order to make or provide what it sells.

> Profit is what remains of sales after the costs are paid.

@ Profit | What is left of sales after costs.

What remains of the sales after the costs are paid is **profit**. Profit means what is left of sales after costs. If the sales are smaller than the costs, the result is negative, and we call it a loss. The rule is: profit equals sales less costs.

> A tea stall gives a worked example of profit.

*Given.* A tea stall sells 100 cups in a day at Tk 10 each. The tea, milk, sugar and gas for the day cost Tk 600.
*Find.* The profit for the day.

> The rule and the arithmetic, step by step.

*Rule.* Profit = sales less costs, and sales = number of cups x price per cup.
*Steps.* Sales = 100 x Tk 10 = Tk 1,000. Profit = Tk 1,000 less Tk 600 = Tk 400.

> The answer, and a check that it is sensible.

*Answer.* The profit for the day is Tk 400.
*Check.* Each cup earns Tk 10 and costs Tk 6, since Tk 600 divided by 100 cups is Tk 6. So each cup leaves Tk 4, and 100 cups leave Tk 400. The answers agree.

> A second example uses a day with a loss.

*A second day.* The stall sells only 50 cups, but the gas, milk and tea still cost Tk 520. Sales are 50 x Tk 10 = Tk 500. Profit is Tk 500 less Tk 520, which is a loss of Tk 20. A day of fewer sales can turn a profit into a loss.

> A cash flow is money actually moving in or out.

@ Cash flow | Money actually moving in or out.

Finally, arrow 4 goes back to the investors. A part of the cash returns to them: interest to the lender and payments to the owner. The rest stays in the firm for the next round. Each movement of money into or out of the firm is a **cash flow**. A cash flow means money actually moving in or out. Cash flow is not the same as profit, as Section 1.4 will show.

> Before moving on: money goes from investors to assets, becomes sales, and part returns to the investors.

*Before moving on.* Money flows from investors through the firm to its assets. Selling goods turns the assets back into cash. A part of that cash returns to the investors, and the rest begins the loop again. Profit is sales less costs.

> The test sentence repeats the idea of the loop.

*You should now be able to say:* "A firm's money moves in a loop, and finance is about every arrow in it."

## 1.4 What Finance Is

> The bakery's first day raises a question that no recipe can answer.

At the end of its first day, Sundial Bakery has Tk 3,00,000 in cash. The owner sees three possible uses. The money may be kept for flour and wages. It may be used to buy a second oven. It may be used to repay a part of the bank loan and so reduce the interest. Each use has a different result, and no recipe can decide among them. A choice about money needs its own way of thinking.

> Finance is the study of how money is raised and used wisely.

@ Finance | How money is raised and used wisely.

We can now state the idea precisely. **Finance** means how money is raised and used wisely. The word *raised* covers every way of bringing money in: the owner's savings, a bank loan, or shares sold to other people. The word *used* covers every way of spending it: ovens, flour, wages, or repaying a loan. The word *wisely* means that each choice is judged against a purpose. Chapter 2 names the purpose.

### Raising Money and Using Money

> Raising money and using money are two sides of finance.

Finance has two sides, and the loop of Fig. 2 shows both. On the *raising* side, arrows 1 and 4 matter: money comes in from investors, and part of it returns to them. On the *using* side, arrows 2 and 3 matter: money buys assets, and the assets earn sales. A firm that raises money with great skill but uses it badly will fail. A firm that uses money well but cannot raise enough will never begin.

### Three Questions

> Finance can be organised around three questions, each answered in later chapters.

Finance can be organised around three questions. First, where should the firm find its money? Second, which long-lasting things should the firm buy with it? Third, how can the firm be sure that it can pay its bills every day? Chapter 2 calls these the financial manager's decisions. Later chapters give each question a detailed answer.

### Price and Risk

> Money has a price, which is interest, and a risk, which is the chance of loss.

Two facts make these questions hard. The first is that money has a price. A firm that borrows pays interest, and a firm that uses the owner's savings gives up what the owner might have earned elsewhere. The second is that money carries risk, which means the chance that results will differ from what was hoped. Almost every idea in this book is a way of handling the price of money, its risk, or both.

### A First Decision, with Numbers

> A small calculation shows how one choice of the three would save interest.

Return to the three uses of the bakery's Tk 3,00,000. We cannot judge them all yet, but one can be measured. Suppose the owner repays Tk 3,00,000 of the Tk 4,00,000 loan on the first day. How much yearly interest would the firm save?

> The data given and the question to answer.

*Given.* Repayment Tk 3,00,000; rate of interest 12 per cent a year.
*Find.* The interest saved in one year.

> The rule and the arithmetic, step by step.

*Steps.* Interest saved = 3,00,000 x 0.12 = Tk 36,000.

> The answer, and a check that it is sensible.

*Answer.* The firm saves Tk 36,000 of interest in a year.
*Check.* Tk 3,00,000 holds 3,000 hundreds, and 3,000 x 12 = 36,000.

> But the saving costs the firm all its cash, so the choice is not yet settled.

The saving is real, but it has a price. After the repayment, the firm has no cash for flour or wages. Finance does not give the answer by arithmetic alone; it also asks whether the firm will survive the next month. Keeping some cash may be worth more than the interest saved.

### Profit Is Not Cash

> Profit and cash are different, and a firm needs both.

@! Go slowly here. Many students find this step confusing, because the two words feel alike.

Here is the most important warning of the chapter: profit and cash are not the same. Suppose a tea stall sells 1,000 cups at Tk 10 each to a nearby office during a month. The office pays at the end of the month. During the month, the stall spends Tk 6,000 on tea, milk, sugar and gas.

> The data given and the question to answer.

*Given.* Sales Tk 10,000, to be paid at month end; costs Tk 6,000, paid during the month.
*Find.* The profit, and the cash in hand before the office pays.

> The profit is Tk 4,000 on paper, yet the stall has spent Tk 6,000 and received nothing.

*Steps.* Profit = Tk 10,000 less Tk 6,000 = Tk 4,000. But the stall has paid out Tk 6,000 and received no money. Before the office pays, the cash of the stall is lower by Tk 6,000.

> The answer, and a check that it is sensible.

*Answer.* The profit is Tk 4,000, but the cash movement so far is a payment of Tk 6,000.
*Check.* After the office pays Tk 10,000, the stall's cash is higher by Tk 10,000 less Tk 6,000, which is Tk 4,000, and now the cash equals the profit.

> A firm can show a profit on paper and still lack the cash to pay its bills.

If the stall cannot pay the milk supplier on the fifth day, the supplier stops delivery, and the stall closes with a profit on paper and no tea to sell. This is why finance watches cash flows and not only profit.

> Before moving on: finance studies how money is raised and used, under price and risk, and profit is not cash.

*Before moving on.* Finance studies how money is raised and used wisely. It asks where money comes from, what it buys, and whether bills can be paid. Money has a price, which is interest, and a risk. Profit is not cash.

> The test sentence states the central warning.

*You should now be able to say:* "A firm can have a profit and still run out of cash."

### What Finance Cannot Do

> Finance has limits: it cannot create money, and it cannot make a poor product sell.

Finance has limits. It cannot create money from nothing, and it cannot make a poor product sell. A firm that makes goods nobody wants will fail even if its finance is excellent. Finance helps a good business to survive and grow; it does not replace the work of making and selling things that people want.

> The pause question asks for the difference between profit and cash.

*Pause and check.* In the tea stall example, how does the Tk 4,000 profit differ from Tk 4,000 in the till?

## 1.5 Finance Beside Accounting, Economics and Management

> Several subjects study the firm, and finance works with the others.

A firm is studied by several subjects. Three of them are close neighbours of finance, and we must separate them from the start so that they are not confused.

> Management directs; accounting records; economics explains choice.

@ Management | Directing people and resources to reach a goal.
@ Accounting | Recording and reporting what has happened to a firm's money.
@ Economics | The study of how people and societies choose when resources are limited.

**Management** means directing people and resources to reach a goal. **Accounting** means recording and reporting what has happened to a firm's money. **Economics** means the study of how people and societies choose when resources are limited. Each describes a different job. The table below places finance beside them.

:table Four subjects that study the firm
| Subject | The question it asks | A bakery example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Management | What should we do, and who will do it? | Plan the week's baking and the staff |
| Accounting | What has happened to our money? | Record Tk 3,00,000 in cash and Tk 4,00,000 in debt |
| Economics | How do buyers, sellers and prices behave? | Why customers buy fewer buns when the price rises |
| Finance | How do we raise money and use it wisely? | Whether to buy a second oven, and how to pay for it |

> Accounting records the past; finance uses the record to decide the future.

Accounting and finance are the closest pair. The accountant records what has happened and reports it. The finance worker uses those records to decide what should happen next. Without accounting, finance has no facts. Without finance, accounting records facts that nobody uses.

> Finance is part of management, because every decision about a firm has a result in money.

Finance is also closely linked to management. Every management decision has a result in money. To open a second shop, the manager must find money, buy equipment, hire staff and pay for goods until the first sales arrive. Each of these tasks is a financial question. Finance is therefore part of good management, and the person who answers these questions is a member of the management team.

> One decision, the second shop, shows all four subjects at work together.

Consider one decision seen from all four seats. The manager plans the work of a second shop. The economist asks whether enough customers live nearby and how much they will pay. The accountant records what the shop earns and spends. The finance worker asks how much money the shop needs, where it will come from, and whether the firm can bear the risk. No single subject can answer the whole question.

> Before moving on: management directs, accounting records, economics explains, and finance decides about money.

*Before moving on.* Management directs, accounting records, economics explains how people choose, and finance decides how money is raised and used. The four work together in every firm.

## A Worked Case: The Opening of Sundial Bakery

> This case applies the chapter's words to the bakery's first day.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 2 January 2026 the owner of Sundial Bakery invests Tk 6,00,000. The bank lends Tk 4,00,000 at 12 per cent a year. The firm buys ovens and equipment for Tk 7,00,000 and keeps the rest as cash.

> The question asks for the cash, assets, debt, equity and one year's interest.

*Question.* How much cash does the firm hold? What are its assets, its debt and its equity? How much interest does it owe for one year?

> The cash and assets come first; then the debt and equity follow.

*Reasoning.* The money received is Tk 6,00,000 plus Tk 4,00,000, which is Tk 10,00,000. The ovens and equipment cost Tk 7,00,000, so the cash is Tk 10,00,000 less Tk 7,00,000, which is Tk 3,00,000. The assets are equipment Tk 7,00,000 plus cash Tk 3,00,000, which is Tk 10,00,000. The debt is the bank loan, Tk 4,00,000. The equity is assets less debt: Tk 10,00,000 less Tk 4,00,000, which is Tk 6,00,000.

> The interest for a year is 12 taka for each 100 taka borrowed.

For the interest, Tk 4,00,000 holds 4,000 hundreds. Each hundred earns Tk 12 in a year, so the interest is 4,000 x Tk 12, which is Tk 48,000. Interest for one month is Tk 48,000 divided by 12, which is Tk 4,000.

:fig The opening position of Sundial Bakery: the money that came in equals the money that went out. | figs/fig3_opening.svg

> Fig. 3 shows two sides of the same total.

Fig. 3 shows the result. The left side shows where the money came from, and the right side shows where it went. Both sides total Tk 10,00,000.

> The conclusion states the five answers.

*Conclusion.* The firm holds Tk 3,00,000 in cash and has assets of Tk 10,00,000, debt of Tk 4,00,000 and equity of Tk 6,00,000. It owes Tk 48,000 in interest for the first year.

> The check shows that debt plus equity equals assets.

*Check.* Debt plus equity is Tk 4,00,000 plus Tk 6,00,000, which is Tk 10,00,000, and this equals the assets. If the two sides differ, a figure has been copied wrongly.

## Summary

> The summary restates the chapter in a few lines.

Money comes in as income and goes out as expense; what is kept is saving. Borrowed money must be repaid with interest. A business is an activity that earns money, and a firm is a business seen as one money-using unit. A firm's money comes from owners (equity) and lenders (debt) and is spent on assets. Sales bring cash back, and profit is sales less costs. Finance is the study of how money is raised and used wisely. It works beside accounting, economics and management, and it must watch cash as well as profit.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common mistakes are stated here and corrected.

> The first mistake mixes up profit and cash.

*"Profit is the money in the bank."* Profit is sales less costs, while cash is money actually held. A firm may show a profit and hold no cash, as the tea stall showed.

> The second mistake treats a loan as income.

*"A loan is income."* A loan brings cash in, but the firm must repay it. Only money earned from selling is sales. Borrowed money is debt.

> The third mistake mixes up equity and cash.

*"Equity is the cash the firm holds."* Equity is the owners' share of what the firm is worth, which is assets less debt. Sundial's equity is Tk 6,00,000, while its cash is Tk 3,00,000.

> The fourth mistake treats finance and accounting as one subject.

*"Finance and accounting are the same."* Accounting records what happened. Finance decides what to do about money next.

## In History: A Loan of Twenty-Seven Dollars

> A real case shows that a very small loan can begin a large idea.

In 1976 Muhammad Yunus, a professor at the University of Chittagong, visited the village of Jobra, near the university.^3 He found that women who made bamboo furniture borrowed money at very high interest and gave most of their profit to the lenders. From his own pocket he lent a total of US$27 to 42 villagers. The experience convinced him that very small loans could change the work and the lives of poor people.

^3: The facts in this paragraph follow published accounts of Grameen Bank; the sums are given in US dollars, as those accounts give them.

> The experiment grew into a bank in 1983.

Yunus then arranged a credit line with Janata Bank, an established bank, in December 1976, and he signed as guarantor of the loans. In October 1983 the project became an independent bank, Grameen Bank, whose name means "village bank". In 2006 Yunus and the bank shared the Nobel Peace Prize. Every idea in this chapter appears in the story: borrowing, interest, risk, the lender's claim, and the way that finance can change what a person is able to do.
