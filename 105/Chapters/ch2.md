## The Opening Puzzle

> The bakery has Tk 3,00,000 and a chance to buy a second oven, but who decides, and by what test?

In February 2026 the owner of Sundial Bakery sees a chance to grow. A second oven would cost Tk 3,00,000, which is exactly the cash that the firm holds. With two ovens, the bakery could bake twice as much bread in the same hours. But if the whole sum is spent on the oven, nothing remains for flour and wages. If the firm borrows more money to buy the oven, it must pay more interest. If it waits, a competitor may take the customers.

> Behind the oven lie two harder questions, one about who decides and one about the aim.

Two harder questions stand behind this choice. The first is *who* should decide such things, and what skills the decider needs. The second is *by what test* a decision is called good. A bigger bakery is not always a better one, and a larger profit this year is not always a better result. Before a firm can choose among its options, it must know what it is trying to achieve. This chapter answers both questions.

## 2.1 The Decisions That Finance Makes

> A firm's money problems fall into four kinds of decision.

@! Go slowly here. Four new terms arrive together, and each answers a different question.

In Chapter 1 we followed a loop of money from investors, through the firm and its assets, to sales and back. Every arrow in that loop hides a choice. Experience shows that the choices fall into four groups. Fig. 4 shows them around the firm. Examine the boxes first, then the direction of each arrow.

:fig The four decisions about a firm's money: three about money in motion, and one about profit. | figs/fig4_decisions.svg

> The first decision is what long-lasting things the firm should buy.

@ Investment decision | Choosing what long-lasting things to buy.

The first group is the **investment decision**. An investment decision means choosing what long-lasting things to buy. A second oven is a clear example. The oven will bake for many years, so the choice affects the firm for a long time. A wrong choice is expensive, because a bought oven cannot easily be returned. Chapters 6 and 7 teach how to judge such choices.

> The second decision is where the money comes from.

@ Financing decision | Choosing where the money comes from.

The second group is the **financing decision**. A financing decision means choosing where the money comes from. The oven can be paid for from the owner's further savings, from a bank loan, or by inviting a new owner to invest. Each source has a different price and a different risk, as Chapter 1 showed. Chapters 13 and 14 describe the sources in detail.

> The third decision concerns the money tied up in daily running.

@ Working capital | Money tied up in daily running, such as flour, unpaid bills and cash.

The third group is about the firm's daily life. A bakery must always hold flour, owe money to its supplier, be owed money by customers who buy on credit, and keep some cash for wages. The money tied up in all this is the firm's **working capital**. Working capital means money tied up in daily running, such as goods waiting to be used or sold, bills not yet paid, and cash. Deciding how much of it to hold is the third decision. Chapter 12 treats it fully.

> The fourth decision is how much profit to give to the owners.

@ Dividend | The part of profit paid to the owners.

The fourth group concerns profit. When a firm earns a profit, someone must decide how much to pay to the owners and how much to keep in the firm for later use. The part paid to the owners is called a **dividend**. A dividend means the part of profit paid to the owners. Keeping more profit in the firm leaves more money for growth but gives the owners less now. Chapter 14 returns to this choice.

> The table lists the four decisions in one place, with the bakery's example of each.

The table below gathers the four decisions. Read each row across: the name, the question, the bakery's own case, and the chapter where it is taught.

:table The four decisions of finance
| Decision | The question | The bakery's case | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Investment | What long-lasting things to buy? | The second oven | 6 and 7 |
| Financing | Where does the money come from? | Savings, a loan, or a new owner | 13 and 14 |
| Working capital | How much money for daily running? | Flour, bills, cash in the till | 12 |
| Dividend | How much profit goes to the owners? | Take it out, or keep it in the firm | 14 |

> The four decisions are linked, because a choice in one changes the others.

The four decisions are not separate. Suppose the owner decides to buy the oven using all the cash. That is an investment decision, and it also answers the financing question: the money comes from the firm's own cash. But it damages the third decision, because no cash is left for flour. A good manager therefore never makes one decision alone. Each choice is tested against the other three.

> Before moving on: finance has four decisions, and each one affects the others.

*Before moving on.* Finance makes four kinds of decision: what to buy (investment), where the money comes from (financing), how to run daily money (working capital), and how much profit to pay out (dividend). They are linked, so a manager judges them together.

> The test sentence states the four decisions.

*You should now be able to say:* "Finance decides what to buy, where the money comes from, how much to hold for daily use, and how much profit to pay out."

> The pause question applies the four groups to a new case.

*Pause and check.* A firm decides to borrow Tk 5,00,000 from a bank and use it to buy a delivery van. Which two of the four decisions are involved?

## 2.2 The Financial Manager and the Work

> A person must take these decisions, and that person is the financial manager.

@ Financial manager | The person who decides how a firm raises and uses money.

Someone must make the four decisions, and that person is the **financial manager**. A financial manager means the person who decides how a firm raises and uses money. In a small firm, such as Sundial Bakery, the owner is the financial manager. In a large firm there is a team, led by a senior manager. The title changes with the size of the firm, but the work is the same.

> The financial manager is not the shop manager, and the two should not be confused.

The financial manager is not the same person as the shop manager. The *shop manager* directs the daily work of the shop: baking, serving and staff. The *financial manager* directs the firm's money. In March 2026 the owner of Sundial Bakery hires a shop manager, but the owner remains the financial manager. In a larger firm these are always different people.

> The work has five tasks: plan, raise, allocate, control and report.

The work of a financial manager can be divided into five tasks. The table below states each task in plain words and gives the bakery's example. Read it row by row.

:table The five tasks of the financial manager
| Task | In plain words | A bakery example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan | Look ahead at how much money will be needed and when | Estimate the money needed for flour in the next three months |
| Raise | Find the money from investors and lenders | Ask a bank for a loan |
| Allocate | Choose what to spend the money on | Decide between a second oven and a delivery van |
| Control | Watch cash and costs so that bills can be paid | Check the till and the supplier's bills each week |
| Report | Tell owners and lenders how the firm is doing | Show the bank the year's results |

> Each task serves a different group of people.

Each task serves a different group of people. Planning and control serve the firm's own managers, who need to know that the firm can pay its bills. Raising money serves the firm's future growth. Reporting serves the owners and the lenders, who have lent or invested their money and have a right to know what has happened to it.

> The financial manager works closely with the accountant but has a different task.

The financial manager works closely with the accountant. Chapter 1 showed that the accountant records what has happened and reports it, while finance decides what to do next. The financial manager uses the accountant's records as facts, and the accountant records the effect of the financial manager's decisions.

> From three seats, the financial manager has three different responsibilities.

*From three seats.* To the owner, the financial manager is a guardian of the owner's savings. To the lender, the financial manager is a person who promises that the loan will be repaid and keeps that promise. To the employees, the financial manager is the person who ensures that wages are paid on time. A good financial manager keeps all three promises together.

> Before moving on: the financial manager plans, raises, allocates, controls and reports.

*Before moving on.* The financial manager makes the four decisions. The work has five tasks: plan, raise, allocate, control and report. In a small firm the owner does this work, and the shop manager is a different person.

## 2.3 How Finance Shapes Efficiency and Survival

> Finance decides whether a firm does much with its money or little, and whether it lasts.

Why does the quality of financial decisions matter so much? There are two answers, and both concern the survival of the firm. The first is about how well the firm uses its money. The second is about whether the firm can continue at all.

> Efficiency means getting the most result from the money and effort used.

@ Efficiency | Getting the most result from the money and effort used.

The first answer is **efficiency**. Efficiency means getting the most result from the money and effort used. A firm uses money efficiently when little of it is idle or wasted. Consider cash that lies in the till for a whole year while the firm pays interest on a bank loan. The cash earns nothing, and the loan costs interest. Both are signs of poor use.

> A worked example shows that idle cash has a price.

*Given.* Sundial Bakery holds Tk 1,00,000 of cash that it does not need for the coming year. The bank loan costs 12 per cent a year.
*Find.* The interest that the firm would save in a year if it used this cash to repay part of the loan.

> The rule and the arithmetic, step by step.

*Rule.* Interest saved = sum repaid x rate, as in Chapter 1.
*Steps.* Interest saved = 1,00,000 x 0.12 = Tk 12,000.

> The answer, and a check that it is sensible.

*Answer.* The idle cash costs the firm Tk 12,000 a year in interest that could have been saved.
*Check.* Tk 1,00,000 holds 1,000 hundreds, and 1,000 x 12 = 12,000.

> The result shows that idle money is not free; it has a price in missed saving.

The Tk 12,000 is not a payment that anyone sends to the bank for the idle cash. It is a saving that the firm failed to make. The point for finance is that money which does no work still has a price.

> The second answer is survival: a firm that cannot pay its bills cannot continue.

The second answer is **survival**. A firm may sell good products and still fail, if it cannot pay its bills on the dates they fall due. Wages are due every month, the supplier's invoice is due every week, and the bank's interest is due on fixed days. If cash is not available on those days, the supplier stops delivering, the workers leave, and the bank may demand repayment. Chapter 1 showed that profit is not cash. This is the practical result of that warning.

> Too much debt is a second danger, because interest must be paid in every month, good or bad.

A firm can also fail because it has borrowed too much. Interest is a fixed payment, and it falls due in good months and bad months alike. A firm with large debt must find that payment whatever its sales are. Chapter 9 shows how such fixed payments increase a firm's risk.

> Finance also shapes growth: too little money prevents it, and too much debt makes it dangerous.

Finance therefore shapes growth as well as survival. A firm with too little money cannot grow, because it cannot buy the ovens or the stock. A firm with too much debt may grow quickly but is in danger if sales fall. The financial manager's work is to find the path between the two.

> Before moving on: good finance uses money efficiently and keeps the firm able to pay its bills.

*Before moving on.* Finance matters because it decides how efficiently a firm uses money and whether the firm survives. Idle money has a price, cash must be available on the days bills fall due, and heavy debt adds risk.

## 2.4 The Goal: Profit or Wealth?

> The firm needs a single aim, and the obvious first answer is profit.

@! Go slowly here. This is the central idea of the chapter, and it will guide every decision in the book.

Return to the question left open in the opening puzzle: by what test is a decision called good? A manager who must choose among ovens, loans and cash needs one aim to measure them all. The first answer that most people give is profit. A firm exists to earn money, they say, so the best decision is the one that brings the largest profit.

> Profit maximisation means aiming for the biggest profit.

@ Profit maximisation | Aiming for the biggest profit.

This aim has a name. **Profit maximisation** means aiming for the biggest profit. For a first answer it is reasonable, because profit is what remains for the owners after all costs. But it fails in three ways, and each failure teaches something important about finance.

### First Weakness: Timing

> The aim ignores when the profit arrives.

The first weakness is *timing*. Profit maximisation does not say when the profit arrives. Suppose that a firm may receive Tk 1,00,000 today or Tk 1,00,000 in three years. The two choices have the same profit, so profit maximisation cannot separate them. But nearly everyone prefers the money now. Money received today can be used today, to repay a loan or to buy goods, and it earns interest while the other waits.

:fig Two choices with the same amount of money arriving at different times. | figs/fig5_timing.svg

> Fig. 5 shows the same amount arriving at two different times.

Fig. 5 shows the two choices. Each line is a timeline, with time moving from the left to the right. The tall bar on the first line is the money arriving today. The bar on the second line is the same money arriving in year 3. The sums are equal, but the choices are not. Chapter 4 teaches how to measure the difference with numbers.

### Second Weakness: Risk

> The aim also ignores how sure the profit is.

The second weakness is *risk*. Suppose a firm may choose between two plans. Plan P gives a sure profit of Tk 50,000. Plan Q gives Tk 1,00,000 if sales are good and nothing if they are not, and the two outcomes are equally likely. On average, plan Q gives Tk 50,000 as well. Yet most people prefer the sure sum, because they prefer to avoid the chance of nothing. Profit maximisation does not notice the difference.

### Third Weakness: Cash

> The aim counts profit, but a firm pays its bills with cash.

The third weakness is *cash*. Chapter 1 showed that profit and cash are different. A firm that counts profit when it makes a sale may have no cash until its customers pay. A goal built on profit alone can therefore lead a firm to approve plans that earn on paper but leave the firm unable to pay its bills.

> Wealth is the total value of what the owners own after debts are paid.

@ Wealth | The total value of what a person owns less what the person owes.

The three weaknesses point to a better aim. First we need a word. **Wealth** means the total value of what a person owns less what the person owes. For the owner of a firm, wealth in the firm is the equity of Chapter 1, valued as fairly as possible.

> Wealth maximisation aims to raise the owners' total value over time.

@ Wealth maximisation | Aiming to raise the owners' total value over time.

The better aim is **wealth maximisation**. Wealth maximisation means aiming to raise the owners' total value over time. This aim answers all three weaknesses. It looks at *when* money arrives, because money later is worth less than money now. It looks at *risk*, because a sure sum is worth more than an uncertain sum of the same average size. And it looks at *cash*, because only cash can be spent or paid to the owners.

> The table sets the two aims side by side.

:table Two possible aims of the firm
| Question | Profit maximisation | Wealth maximisation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does it consider timing? | No | Yes |
| Does it consider risk? | No | Yes |
| Does it use cash? | No, it uses profit | Yes |
| Does it look beyond this year? | Not necessarily | Yes, over time |

> Wealth is hard to see inside a firm, so a share price is used as a scoreboard.

@ Shareholder | The owner of a share of a company.
@ Share price | The price at which one share can be bought or sold.

How can anyone see whether wealth is rising? In a large company, a **shareholder** means the owner of a share of the company, and many shares are bought and sold every day. The **share price** means the price at which one share can be bought or sold. When buyers expect the firm to bring in more money, with less risk and sooner, they are willing to pay more. A rising share price is therefore a scoreboard for the owners' wealth.

> The scoreboard has limits, and the manager should not chase the price day by day.

The scoreboard has limits. A share price may rise or fall because of events outside the firm, such as news about the whole country. A manager who tries to push the price up this month, by hiding bad news or by cutting useful spending, may reduce the firm's real value in the years that follow. For this reason wealth is judged over years, not days. A firm whose shares are not traded, such as Sundial Bakery, has no price. Its owner must estimate the firm's value, and the methods of Chapters 7 and 11 are the tools.

> Wealth maximisation does not mean seeking riches by any means.

Finally, a word of caution about the aim itself. Wealth maximisation does not permit a firm to cheat its customers, to deceive its lenders, or to harm its workers. A firm that does these things soon loses its customers, its credit and its good name, and its wealth falls. The aim works best within the law and fair dealing, and lasting wealth usually grows from them.

> Before moving on: profit alone fails on timing, risk and cash, while wealth maximisation meets all three.

*Before moving on.* Profit maximisation ignores timing, risk and cash. Wealth maximisation aims to raise the owners' total value over time, and in a company the share price is its scoreboard. Both aims must be pursued within the law and with fair dealing.

> The test sentence states the goal.

*You should now be able to say:* "A firm aims to raise the owners' wealth over time, because profit alone ignores when, how surely and in what form the money arrives."

> The pause question applies the idea of risk.

*Pause and check.* A firm may choose a sure gain of Tk 60,000 or an equal chance of Tk 1,20,000 and nothing. Why can profit maximisation not tell the two apart?

## 2.5 Agency: When Owners and Managers Differ

> A hired manager may want something different from the owner, and the bakery's bonus rule shows how.

When the owner of a small bakery also runs the shop, the owner's wishes and the manager's wishes are the same, because they are one person. The position changes when the owner hires someone else. On 1 March 2026 Sundial Bakery hires a shop manager. The terms are in the case file.

> The hiring terms are recorded in the case file.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 1 March 2026 Sundial Bakery hires a shop manager at Tk 30,000 a month plus a bonus of 2 per cent of the sales of the month. The bonus depends on sales, not on profit.

> The owner is the principal and the manager is the agent.

@ Principal | The one who hires another to act for them.
@ Agent | The one hired to act for another.

Two words describe the arrangement. The **principal** means the one who hires another to act for them. The **agent** means the one hired to act for another. The owner of Sundial Bakery is the principal, and the shop manager is the agent. Fig. 6 shows the arrangement.

:fig The owner (principal) and the manager (agent), and what each wants. | figs/fig6_agency.svg

> The two want different things, and that is the source of trouble.

Read Fig. 6 from the left. The owner hires the manager and gives authority, shown by the upper arrow. The manager reports and is paid a reward, shown by the lower arrow. Under the boxes are the two wants. The owner wants more profit. The manager, whose bonus is 2 per cent of sales, wants more sales. These two wants are not the same.

> A worked example shows how the bonus rule can lead the manager to lower prices.

@ Agency problem | A situation in which the agent's aims differ from the principal's.

When the agent's aims differ from the principal's, an **agency problem** arises. An agency problem means a situation in which the agent's aims differ from the principal's. An example will show how this happens. Suppose that in one month the bakery sells 7,500 boxes of buns at Tk 40, and each box costs Tk 24 to make. In a second version of the same month, the manager lowers the price to Tk 34 and sells 9,000 boxes.

> The data given and the question to answer.

*Given.* Normal month: price Tk 40, 7,500 boxes. Lower-price month: price Tk 34, 9,000 boxes. Cost to make each box: Tk 24. The bonus is 2 per cent of sales.
*Find.* In each month, the sales, the manager's bonus, and the money left to the owner after the cost of making the boxes.

> The rule and the arithmetic, step by step.

*Rule.* Sales = price x boxes. Bonus = 0.02 x sales. Money left = (price less cost per box) x boxes.
*Steps (normal).* Sales = 40 x 7,500 = Tk 3,00,000. Bonus = 0.02 x 3,00,000 = Tk 6,000. Money left = (40 less 24) x 7,500 = 16 x 7,500 = Tk 1,20,000.
*Steps (lower price).* Sales = 34 x 9,000 = Tk 3,06,000. Bonus = 0.02 x 3,06,000 = Tk 6,120. Money left = (34 less 24) x 9,000 = 10 x 9,000 = Tk 90,000.

> The answer, and a check that it is sensible.

*Answer.* The manager's bonus rises from Tk 6,000 to Tk 6,120, while the money left to the owner falls from Tk 1,20,000 to Tk 90,000, a loss of Tk 30,000.
*Check.* The lower price loses Tk 6 on each of the 7,500 boxes that would have sold anyway: 7,500 x 6 = Tk 45,000. The extra 1,500 boxes gain 1,500 x 10 = Tk 15,000. The net change is 15,000 less 45,000, a loss of Tk 30,000, and this agrees with the answer.

> The table puts the two months side by side.

:table The same month with two prices
| Item | Normal month | Lower-price month |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price of a box | Tk 40 | Tk 34 |
| Boxes sold | 7,500 | 9,000 |
| Sales | Tk 3,00,000 | Tk 3,06,000 |
| Manager's bonus (2% of sales) | Tk 6,000 | Tk 6,120 |
| Money left to the owner after the cost of the boxes | Tk 1,20,000 | Tk 90,000 |

> The manager gains slightly and the owner loses much, though the manager may not intend harm.

The manager gains a little and the owner loses a great deal. The manager may not intend harm. The bonus rule rewards whatever raises sales, and the manager responds to the reward. The fault lies in the rule, which pays for the wrong result.

> The cost of such a conflict, and of preventing it, is called agency cost.

@ Agency cost | The cost of the conflict, or of preventing it.

The owner has two kinds of loss here. One is the loss of Tk 30,000 caused by the conflict. The other is the effort and money spent on preventing it, such as checking the manager's prices. Together they are **agency cost**, which means the cost of the conflict or of preventing it. Agency costs are a real cost of hiring someone to act for you.

> An incentive is a reward that guides behaviour, and a better rule can align the two aims.

@ Incentive | A reward that guides behaviour.

The owner can reduce the problem with an **incentive**, which means a reward that guides behaviour. A good incentive rewards the result that the owner wants. Suppose the bonus were 5 per cent of the money left to the owner, not 2 per cent of sales. In the normal month the bonus is 0.05 x 1,20,000 = Tk 6,000, the same as before. In the lower-price month it is 0.05 x 90,000 = Tk 4,500, which is less. Now the manager has no reason to lower the price in this way.

> Other ways to reduce the problem are checking, rules and ownership.

There are other ways to reduce an agency problem. The owner may check the reports and prices each week, which takes time. The owner may set a rule, for example that no price may be lowered without permission. Or the owner may share ownership with the manager, so that the manager gains when the firm's value rises. Each method has its own cost, and no method removes the problem completely.

> In a large company the problem is larger, because the owners are many and far from the firm.

In a large company the problem is larger. The shareholders may number in the thousands and cannot watch the managers daily. The managers, as agents, may prefer a larger firm, more comfort for themselves, or safer choices than the shareholders would want. The ideas of this section explain why companies use bonus rules linked to shareholder wealth, and why they require careful reports.

> Before moving on: when owners hire managers, their aims may differ, and the cost of this is an agency cost.

*Before moving on.* The owner who hires a manager is the principal, and the manager is the agent. If the manager's aims differ from the owner's, an agency problem arises. The conflict and the cost of preventing it are agency cost. A good incentive rewards what the owner truly wants.

> The test sentence states the main point.

*You should now be able to say:* "An agency problem arises when the one who acts wants something different from the one who hires."

## A Worked Case: The Second Oven and the Bonus Rule

> This case applies the four decisions, the manager's pay and the incentive to the bakery's February and March.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* In February 2026 Sundial Bakery considers a second oven, with an outlay of Tk 3,00,000. On 1 March 2026 it hires a shop manager at Tk 30,000 a month plus 2 per cent of the month's sales as a bonus. In a later month the manager lowers the price of a box from Tk 40 to Tk 38 and sells 8,500 boxes instead of 7,500. Each box costs Tk 24 to make.

> The questions ask for the decisions, the manager's pay and the effect on the owner.

*Question.* (a) Which of the four decisions does the oven involve? (b) What is the manager's pay in a month with sales of Tk 3,00,000? (c) What happens to the manager's bonus and to the owner's money when the price falls to Tk 38?

> The oven touches all four decisions, and the pay follows from the rule.

*Reasoning, part (a).* Choosing to buy the oven is an investment decision. Choosing how to pay for it is a financing decision. Making sure that flour and wages can still be paid is a working capital decision. Taking money out of the firm, or leaving it in, is the dividend decision. The oven touches all four.
*Reasoning, part (b).* Pay = Tk 30,000 + 0.02 x 3,00,000 = Tk 30,000 + Tk 6,000 = Tk 36,000.

> Part (c) needs the sales, the bonus and the money left, before and after.

*Reasoning, part (c).* Before: sales Tk 3,00,000, bonus Tk 6,000, money left (40 less 24) x 7,500 = Tk 1,20,000. After: sales 38 x 8,500 = Tk 3,23,000, bonus 0.02 x 3,23,000 = Tk 6,460, money left (38 less 24) x 8,500 = 14 x 8,500 = Tk 1,19,000.

> The conclusion states the three answers.

*Conclusion.* (a) The oven involves all four decisions. (b) The manager's pay is Tk 36,000. (c) The bonus rises by Tk 460, from Tk 6,000 to Tk 6,460, while the owner's money left falls by Tk 1,000, from Tk 1,20,000 to Tk 1,19,000.

> The check confirms the differences and the lesson.

*Check.* The bonus change is 6,460 less 6,000, which is 460. The owner's change is 1,19,000 less 1,20,000, which is a fall of 1,000. The manager gains while the owner loses, though the loss here is small. The bonus on sales rewards a result that the owner does not fully want.

## Summary

> The summary restates the chapter in a few lines.

A firm's money decisions fall into four groups: investment, financing, working capital and dividend. The financial manager plans, raises, allocates, controls and reports. Finance decides how efficiently a firm uses its money and whether it survives. Profit maximisation ignores timing, risk and cash, so the better aim is wealth maximisation: to raise the owners' total value over time, for which the share price is a scoreboard. When owners hire managers, an agency problem can arise, and incentives, checking and rules can reduce it at some cost.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Four common mistakes are stated here and corrected.

> The first mistake mixes up the shop manager and the financial manager.

*"The financial manager runs the shop."* The shop manager directs the daily work. The financial manager directs the firm's money. In a small firm one person may do both, but the tasks are different.

> The second mistake thinks profit is the same as the owners' wealth.

*"The firm's aim is the biggest profit."* Profit ignores when it arrives, how sure it is, and whether it is cash. The better aim is the owners' wealth over time.

> The third mistake treats wealth maximisation as permission to do anything.

*"Wealth maximisation means making money at any cost."* A firm that cheats customers or harms its workers loses its good name and its customers, and so loses wealth.

> The fourth mistake thinks that managers always act for the owners.

*"A manager always acts for the owner."* A manager acts for the owner when the rewards and the checks make this the manager's own interest. Where they do not, an agency problem arises.

## In History: When Ownership and Control Parted

> A real study showed that, in large companies, owners and managers had become different people.

In 1932 two American scholars, Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means, published *The Modern Corporation and Private Property*. They studied large American companies. They argued that the ownership of such companies was spread among many shareholders, and that the control of the companies had passed into the hands of salaried managers. The owners provided the money, but they no longer directed the firm.

> The argument raised the question this chapter asks: who watches the managers?

This separation of ownership and control raised a question that remains important: how can many scattered owners be sure that managers act in their interest? The ideas of principal and agent, of incentives and of agency cost are later answers to that question. Section 2.5 applied them to a single bakery, but they apply with greater force to companies with thousands of shareholders.

Berle, A. and Means, G. (1932), *The Modern Corporation and Private Property*, discussed above, first described the separation of ownership and control in large companies. Jensen, M. and Meckling, W. (1976), "Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure", in the *Journal of Financial Economics*, gave the term *agency cost* a precise meaning and developed the theory of the conflict between owners and managers.

4. Give two reasons why good finance matters to the survival of a firm.

6. Explain three weaknesses of profit maximisation.
7. What is wealth maximisation? Why is the share price called a scoreboard, and what are its limits?
