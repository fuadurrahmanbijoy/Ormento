:chapter XV | Cost of Capital
:mood What price does a firm pay for the money it uses?
:desc Every project needs money, and money has a price. This chapter shows how to find the price of each kind of fund, how to combine the prices into one yearly rate, and how to use that rate to test the projects of earlier chapters again.

> A question left open in Chapter 7 now needs an answer.

In Chapter 7 the owner of the bakery compared an oven and a van. Both projects looked good, and the oven won by a small margin. Yet one number in that comparison was only a guess. The owner had chosen a provisional rate of 10% to bring the later cash back to today, and had promised to replace it with a better one.

> The guessed rate decided how large each project's value looked.

Does the guess matter? Suppose the true rate is 15%. Then the value of the oven falls from Tk 79,079 to Tk 35,216. Suppose it is 20%. Then the oven no longer pays for itself. The same cash flows can give a clear yes or a clear no, and only the rate has changed. A project is never judged alone. It is judged against the price of the money that pays for it.

> A fair rate must come from the real price of the firm's funds.

This chapter finds that price. We begin with an idea from daily life, then price each source of money in turn, then combine the prices into one rate. At the end we return to the oven and the van and test them again.

## What the Cost of Capital Is

@! Go slowly here. This idea is new.

> A taka that is borrowed or invested always has a price.

Begin with a student who needs a laptop that costs Tk 60,000. Her uncle lends her Tk 30,000 and asks for Tk 3,000 each year in return. She pays the other Tk 30,000 from her savings. Those savings were earning 6% a year in a bank, so using them means she gives up Tk 1,800 a year. What does the laptop cost her, as a yearly percentage of the money used?

> Each part of the money has its own price in yearly percentage.

The uncle's part costs 10%, because Tk 3,000 is one tenth of Tk 30,000. The savings part costs 6%, because that is the reward she gives up. The two parts are equal in size, so the average price is half of 10% plus half of 6%, which is 8%. The laptop is a good use of money only if it gives her a benefit worth more than 8% a year. We shall use exactly this method for a firm.

> A firm does the same sum, with more kinds of money.

@ Cost of capital | The yearly price, as a percentage, that a firm pays for the money it uses.

A firm also uses money from several places: lenders, owners who put in money, and profit kept in the business. We now give the idea its name. The **cost of capital** means the price a firm pays for its funds, stated as a yearly percentage of the money used. If a firm uses Tk 1,00,000 of funds and the cost of capital is 11%, the funds must earn Tk 11,000 a year to pay their price.

> The word cost has a different meaning here.

> *Word in use.* In daily life, cost is a price paid once. Here it is a yearly percentage.

Notice that the word *cost* has changed its meaning. In earlier chapters, cost meant money spent to make or buy something. Here it is a percentage, a price for using money over time. Keep the two meanings apart. When we say cost of capital, we always mean the yearly percentage.

> Every price has two owners: the one who pays and the one who is paid.

There is one more point, and it is the most important. The price that the firm pays is the reward that its investors receive. A lender who is paid 12% earns 12%. So the firm calls this number its cost, and the investor calls it the **required return**, which means the least reward that the investor will accept for lending or investing. It is one number seen from two sides. The next figure shows this.

:fig The firm's cost of capital and the investor's required return are one number seen from two sides. | fig15_1.svg

> The same rate appears in earlier chapters under other names.

You have met this rate before. In Chapters 4 to 7 we called it the **discount rate**, the rate used to bring later cash back to today. In Chapter 8 and later we called it the required return. All three names describe one idea. A project is worth doing only if its cash flows, brought back to today at this rate, are worth more than its outlay. That is the net present value rule of Chapter 7. The cost of capital is the rate that the rule needs.

> Funds come from three sources, so there are three prices.

@ Capital structure | The mix of debt and equity that a firm uses to pay for its assets.

A firm gets its funds from three kinds of source. The first is debt, which is money borrowed from lenders (Chapter 13). The second is preferred stock, which is shares with a fixed dividend (Chapter 14). The third is common equity, which is the money of the ordinary owners, either put in by them or kept from profit (Chapters 11 and 14). Each source has its own price. The mix of debt and equity that a firm uses is its **capital structure**, and it decides how the separate prices are combined.

> Cheap-looking money may be costly in a way that is hard to see.

Here is a common confusion. Debt shows the lowest price, so it seems wise to use only debt. This is not correct. Each new loan raises the risk of the firm (Chapter 9), and owners and lenders ask for more reward when the risk is higher. So the price of the other sources rises as debt grows. The cost of capital is a price for the whole mix of funds, and not for one source alone.

> The idea looks different from each seat.

Consider the viewpoints in turn. The owner sees the cost of capital as a limit: a project that earns less than this rate takes value away. The lender sees it as a promise, the interest that must be paid on time. The financial manager sees it as a tool, a single rate that lets many different projects be compared fairly. All three are looking at the same number, and each uses it for a different task.

> Two questions to test the idea before moving on.

*Pause and check.* (1) The student's uncle had lent her all Tk 60,000 instead of half, at 10%. What would the laptop have cost her, as a yearly percentage? (2) Her savings earn 6% in the bank. Why is 6% a cost to her, even though she pays nobody? Short answers are given at the back of the book.

> Recap: the cost of capital is one price with three parts.

*Before moving on.* The cost of capital is the yearly price of a firm's funds. For the firm it is a cost, for the investor it is a required return, and in Chapters 4 to 7 it was the discount rate. Debt, preferred stock and common equity each have a price, and the mix decides the total.

> A check the reader can use at once.

*You should now be able to say:* the cost of capital is the yearly percentage that a firm must earn on its funds to satisfy those who supplied them.

## Cost of Debt After Tax

> A loan is the simplest fund to price.

@ Cost of debt | The yearly percentage a firm pays on its borrowed money, after the tax saved is taken into account.

Debt is the easiest source to price, because the lender states the rate. If the bakery borrows Tk 4,00,000 at 12%, the interest is Tk 48,000 a year. Before tax, the **cost of debt** is therefore 12%. For a bond that is traded, the yearly return to a buyer who holds it until maturity is the yield to maturity of Chapter 11, and that is the before-tax cost of debt for a firm that sells such bonds.

> Interest reduces tax, so the real price is lower.

Now recall the tax shield of Chapter 13. Interest is an expense, so it reduces the profit on which the firm pays tax. Every taka of interest therefore saves a part of a taka in tax. If the tax rate is 25%, each taka of interest saves 25 paisa of tax, and the firm really pays only 75 paisa. We call this the cost of debt after tax.

> The rule has one multiplication.

In words: the after-tax cost of debt is the interest rate multiplied by one less the tax rate. In symbols, k(d) = i × (1 − T), where i is the interest rate before tax and T is the tax rate written as a decimal. Here k(d) stands for the cost of debt.

> First example, with small numbers.

*Given.* A firm borrows Tk 1,00,000 at 10% interest. The tax rate is 25%. *Find.* The cost of debt after tax.

> Four steps, each short.

*Steps.* Interest in one year is 10% of Tk 1,00,000, which is Tk 10,000. Tax saved is 25% of Tk 10,000, which is Tk 2,500. The firm really pays Tk 10,000 less Tk 2,500, which is Tk 7,500. As a share of the loan, Tk 7,500 divided by Tk 1,00,000 is 7.5%.

> Answer and a quick test.

*Answer.* The cost of debt after tax is 7.5%. *Check.* Use the rule: 10% × (1 − 0.25) = 10% × 0.75 = 7.5%. Both ways agree, and the answer is lower than 10%, as it should be.

> Sometimes the firm receives less than the face value of a bond.

@ Issue cost | The fees a firm pays to sell new bonds or shares, which reduce the money it receives.

The second example is less tidy. When a firm sells new bonds, it often pays fees to the bank that sells them. These fees are the **issue cost**, sometimes called the flotation cost. The firm then receives less money than the face value, and this amount is the net proceeds.

> The method must use what the firm actually receives.

*Given.* A firm sells debentures with a face value of Tk 1,000, a coupon rate of 11% and a life of 10 years. After issue costs, it receives Tk 950 for each one. The tax rate is 25%. *Find.* The cost of debt after tax.

> Use a short formula that averages the yearly gain.

*Formula.* A close estimate of the before-tax cost is: (yearly interest + yearly share of the gain on repayment) divided by the average of the face value and the net proceeds. The gain on repayment is the face value less the net proceeds, spread over the years.

> The steps show each line.

*Steps.* Yearly interest is 11% of Tk 1,000, which is Tk 110. The gain on repayment is Tk 1,000 − Tk 950 = Tk 50, and over 10 years this is Tk 5 a year. The average of Tk 1,000 and Tk 950 is Tk 975. Before tax the cost is (110 + 5) ÷ 975 = 115 ÷ 975 = 11.79%. After tax it is 11.79% × 0.75 = 8.85%.

> Answer and check.

*Answer.* The cost of debt after tax is about 8.85%. *Check.* The exact yield to maturity, found by trial as in Chapter 11, is 11.88%. After tax this is 8.91%. The short formula is close, and it is accepted in an answer when the question gives no more.

> Three slips are common here.

*Common slips.* First, the reader forgets the tax step and uses the before-tax rate. Second, the reader uses the coupon rate and forgets issue costs. Third, the reader applies the tax adjustment to dividends, which the tax law does not allow as an expense. We return to this last point in the next section.

> The tax saving has a condition.

There is one exception. The tax saving is real only if the firm earns enough profit to pay tax. A firm that makes a loss pays no tax, so interest saves it nothing, and its cost of debt after tax is the full interest rate. In this chapter we assume the firm earns taxable profit.

> The bakery's own debt.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Sundial Bakery borrows at 12% a year (loans of Tk 4,00,000 and Tk 3,00,000 in 2026). At the illustrative tax rate of 25%, the cost of debt after tax is 12% × (1 − 0.25) = 9%.^1

^1: The tax rate of 25% is a placeholder for illustration. The rate that applies depends on the type of business and the law of Bangladesh in force, and must be checked before the book is printed.

> Bank loans with instalments are priced in the same way.

For a term loan repaid in instalments (Chapter 13), the lender states a yearly rate on the balance still owed, and that stated rate is the before-tax cost. The tax step is the same: multiply by one less the tax rate. Only when the firm pays large fees, or receives much less than the face amount, does the cost need the longer method used for bonds. Most exercises that give one interest rate and one tax rate need only the short rule.

> Recap: debt is priced after tax.

*Before moving on.* The cost of debt after tax is the interest rate multiplied by one less the tax rate. If the firm pays issue costs, use the money it receives, not the face value. The saving holds only if the firm pays tax.

## Cost of Preferred and Common Funds

> Preferred stock is the next cheapest source after debt.

@ Cost of preferred stock | The yearly fixed dividend divided by the money the firm receives per preferred share.

Preferred stock was described in Chapter 14. It pays a fixed dividend each year, and the holders are paid before the ordinary owners. The **cost of preferred stock** is the yearly dividend divided by the money the firm receives for each share. In symbols, k(p) = D(p) ÷ N(p), where D(p) is the yearly preferred dividend and N(p) is the net proceeds per share.

> Dividends are not an expense, so no tax step.

Notice that there is no tax adjustment. A dividend is paid out of profit that has already been taxed, so it saves no tax. This is why preferred stock costs more than debt of the same face rate, even though both pay a fixed amount.

> First example, small numbers.

*Given.* A preferred share is sold for Tk 100 and pays a dividend of Tk 10 each year. The firm pays no issue cost. *Find.* The cost of preferred stock. *Steps.* k(p) = 10 ÷ 100 = 0.10. *Answer.* The cost is 10%.

> Second example, with issue costs.

*Given.* A preferred share has a face value of Tk 100 and pays a 10% dividend. The firm receives Tk 95 for each share after issue costs. *Find.* The cost. *Steps.* The yearly dividend is 10% of Tk 100, which is Tk 10. Then k(p) = 10 ÷ 95 = 0.1053. *Answer.* The cost is 10.5%. *Check.* It is higher than 10%, because the firm receives less than the face value.

> Common equity is the hardest fund to price.

@ Cost of equity | The yearly return that the common owners require on the money they have in the firm.

Common equity is harder to price, because the owners are not promised any amount. The firm pays no stated interest and no fixed dividend. Yet owners are not giving their money free. They could have placed it elsewhere, so they expect a reward that matches the risk they accept. The **cost of equity** is the return that the common owners require on the money they have in the firm.

> Profit that is kept in the business is not free.

Profit that the firm keeps is part of the owners' money. It is tempting to say that it costs nothing, because nobody is paid for it. This is a mistake. If the firm had paid the profit out as a dividend, the owners could have invested it at the same level of risk. They require the same return on kept profit as on money they put in newly. So the cost of retained earnings equals the cost of equity.

> Two ways to estimate the cost of equity are in use.

Two methods are used to estimate the cost of equity. The first uses the capital asset pricing model of Chapter 10. The second uses the value of a share that pays a growing dividend, from Chapter 11. Each method has strengths, and a careful person may calculate both and compare.

> Method one uses beta.

*Method one.* In Chapter 10 the required return was the risk-free return plus beta times the market risk premium. In symbols, k(e) = r(f) + β × (r(m) − r(f)), where r(f) is the risk-free return, r(m) is the return of the market and β is beta. For the bakery, the illustrative figures were r(f) = 6%, r(m) = 12% and β = 1.2.

> A small check with round numbers first.

*Example.* If r(f) = 5%, r(m) = 11% and β = 1.0, then k(e) = 5% + 1.0 × (11% − 5%) = 11%. For the bakery, k(e) = 6% + 1.2 × (12% − 6%) = 6% + 7.2% = 13.2%. *Check.* A beta above 1 raises the answer above the market return of 12%, and 13.2% does.

> Method two uses the dividend a share is expected to pay.

*Method two.* In Chapter 11 a share with a dividend that grows at a steady rate has the value P(0) = D(1) ÷ (k(e) − g). Here P(0) is the price now, D(1) is the dividend expected next year and g is the yearly growth rate of the dividend. Rearranging gives k(e) = D(1) ÷ P(0) + g. The first part is the dividend yield, and the second is the growth that the owner expects.

> Worked with two data sets.

*Example 1.* A share sells for Tk 100. The next dividend is Tk 5, and it grows at 5% a year. Then k(e) = 5 ÷ 100 + 0.05 = 0.05 + 0.05 = 10%. *Example 2.* A share sells for Tk 80. The next dividend is Tk 6, with growth of 4%. Then k(e) = 6 ÷ 80 + 0.04 = 0.075 + 0.04 = 11.5%.

> New shares cost more than kept profit.

When a firm sells new common shares, it pays issue costs. Suppose the issue cost in Example 2 is Tk 4 on each share. The firm receives only Tk 76. Then k(e) = 6 ÷ 76 + 0.04 = 0.0789 + 0.04 = 11.9%. So the cost of new shares is higher than the cost of retained earnings, which is 11.5%.

> Estimates of this kind can be wrong.

The two methods rarely give the same answer, and each depends on estimates. Beta, the market return and the growth rate are all uncertain. A private firm such as the bakery has no share price, so only the first method can be used, and with illustrative figures. A person who uses either method should state the numbers, show the arithmetic and accept that the answer is an estimate, not a fact.

> A table of the three costs side by side.

The table below compares the three kinds of fund. Read each row from left to right: it gives the kind of fund, what the investor is promised, the usual tax treatment and the way the cost is found.

:table Three kinds of fund compared
| Fund | Reward to the investor | Tax effect | How the cost is found |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Debt | Interest, fixed, paid first | Interest saves tax | Interest rate × (1 − tax rate) |
| Preferred stock | Fixed dividend, paid before common | None | Dividend ÷ net proceeds |
| Common equity | What is left, not promised | None | CAPM, or next dividend ÷ price + growth |

> Order of cost follows order of risk.

The costs usually follow this order: debt is cheapest, preferred stock is next and common equity is dearest. The reason is the order of claims from Chapters 11 and 14. Lenders are paid first, preferred holders next and common owners last. The one who is paid last carries the most risk, so he asks for the largest reward.

> The bakery's two costs, side by side.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* For the bakery, the cost of debt after tax is 9% and the cost of equity by the capital asset pricing model is 13.2%. The order, with debt below equity, is as expected. The bakery has no preferred stock at this stage.

> Recap: three funds, three methods.

*Before moving on.* Preferred stock costs its dividend divided by what the firm receives, with no tax step. Common equity costs what the owners require, found by beta or by the dividend method. Kept profit has the same cost as the owners' money. Issue costs raise the price of new funds.

## The Weighted Average Cost of Capital

@! Go slowly here. Several parts combine.

> The firm has one rate to find, not three.

A project is paid for by the whole pool of the firm's funds and not by one source. So the firm needs a single rate. Think of a bag of mixed rice, half cheap and half dear. The price of one kilogram of the mix is not the price of the cheap rice. It is the average of the two prices, with each price counted in proportion to its share. The same idea gives the firm one rate.

> Each cost is counted by its share of the funds.

@ Weighted average cost of capital | The average of the costs of all funds, each counted by its share of the total.

The **weighted average cost of capital** means the average of the costs of all the firm's funds, with each cost counted in proportion to the share of funds that it supplies. It is often written WACC. The share of each fund is its **weight**.

> The formula is a sum of products.

In words: multiply each cost by its weight and add the products. In symbols, WACC = w(d) × k(d) + w(p) × k(p) + w(e) × k(e). Here w stands for a weight, and the small letters d, p and e stand for debt, preferred stock and equity. The weights are decimals, and together they must add to 1.

> First example, two funds and clean numbers.

*Given.* A firm has Tk 1,00,000 of debt with an after-tax cost of 8% and Tk 1,00,000 of equity with a cost of 12%. *Find.* The WACC. *Steps.* The total is Tk 2,00,000. Each weight is Tk 1,00,000 ÷ Tk 2,00,000 = 0.5. Then WACC = 0.5 × 8% + 0.5 × 12% = 4% + 6% = 10%. *Check.* The answer lies between 8% and 12%, in the middle, because the weights are equal.

> Second example, three funds with unequal weights.

*Given.* The funds are debt of Tk 3,00,000 (cost after tax 9%), preferred stock of Tk 1,00,000 (cost 10.5%) and common equity of Tk 6,00,000 (cost 13%). *Find.* The WACC. The table sets out the work.

:table Weighted average cost of capital, second example
| Fund | Amount (Tk) | Weight | Cost | Weight × cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Debt | 3,00,000 | 0.30 | 9.0% | 2.70% |
| Preferred stock | 1,00,000 | 0.10 | 10.5% | 1.05% |
| Common equity | 6,00,000 | 0.60 | 13.0% | 7.80% |
| Total | 10,00,000 | 1.00 |  | 11.55% |

> How to read the table.

Read a row at a time. For debt, the weight is Tk 3,00,000 ÷ Tk 10,00,000 = 0.30, and 0.30 × 9.0% = 2.70%. Do the same for each fund, then add the last column. *Answer.* The WACC is 11.55%. *Check.* The weights add to 1.00, and 11.55% lies between the lowest cost, 9%, and the highest, 13%. It lies nearer to 13%, because equity has the largest weight.

> Weights can be taken from books, markets or a plan.

@ Target weights | The shares of debt and equity that a firm plans to hold in the long run.

Which amounts should give the weights? There are three choices. Book weights use the amounts written in the firm's records, which are easy to find. Market weights use the present market value of each fund, which is more accurate for traded securities. **Target weights** are the shares that the firm plans to keep in the long run, and most careful firms prefer them, because the funds of the future are what new projects will use.

> The cost rises as more funds are raised.

There is an exception that every reader should know. The price of funds does not stay fixed as the firm raises more. At first the firm uses retained earnings, which have no issue cost. When those are used up, it must sell new shares, which cost more. Lenders may also ask for higher interest on a large loan. So the cost of the next taka can be higher than the average cost of the earlier ones. In this chapter we assume the rate stays the same for the sums we use.

> Does the mix of funds change the average?

A long debate remains open: can a firm lower its WACC by changing the mix of debt and equity? Interest saves tax, and this favours debt. But debt raises risk, which raises the price of equity and may harm the firm if it cannot pay. Most writers agree that, in practice, there is a mix that is best for each firm, but that it is hard to find. We do not try to find it here.

> The bakery's weights and its new hurdle rate.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* The owner of the bakery plans to hold debt and equity in equal parts as a long-run target, so the weights are 0.5 and 0.5. The costs are 9% for debt after tax and 13.2% for equity. WACC = 0.5 × 9% + 0.5 × 13.2% = 4.5% + 6.6% = 11.1%. This is the new hurdle rate for the bakery.

> A short test with new figures.

*Pause and check.* A firm has debt of Tk 4,00,000 with a cost after tax of 8%, and equity of Tk 6,00,000 with a cost of 12%. Find the weights, and then the WACC. Say why the answer must lie between 8% and 12%. A short answer is given at the back of the book.

> Recap: one weighted rate.

*Before moving on.* The WACC multiplies each cost by its weight and adds the products. The weights must add to 1. Target weights suit new projects best. The rate can rise when the firm must raise more funds.

> A check the reader can test.

*You should now be able to say:* the WACC is one yearly rate that shows what the firm pays, on average, for each taka of its funds.

## Using the Rate to Retest Projects

> The rate is useful only if it changes decisions.

We now return to the question from the start of the chapter. The WACC serves as the hurdle rate of Chapter 7, which means the least return that a project must earn. A project is accepted if its net present value at the WACC is above zero. This is the same as saying that its internal rate of return is greater than the WACC. If the two tests disagree on a ranking, Chapter 7 shows how to settle the matter.

> A project can pass at one rate and fail at another.

*Example.* A project costs Tk 1,00,000 and brings Tk 40,000 at the end of each of three years. At a WACC of 10% the annuity factor for 3 years is 2.4869, so the present value of the inflows is 40,000 × 2.4869 = Tk 99,476. The net present value is 99,476 − 1,00,000 = −Tk 524. The project is rejected, narrowly.

> The earlier rate would have said yes.

*Check.* At a rate of 8% the annuity factor is 2.5771. The present value is Tk 1,03,084, and the net present value is +Tk 3,084. Had the owner guessed 8%, the project would have been accepted. The internal rate of return is about 9.7%, which is below 10%, so the two tests agree on rejection at 10%. A small difference in the rate decided the matter, and this is why the rate must be found with care.

> One rate is right only for projects of the same kind of risk.

The WACC rests on an assumption. It assumes that the new project has about the same risk as the business as a whole, and that the mix of funds does not change. This is true for another oven. It is not true for a very different venture. A risky new product line should be tested at a higher rate than the WACC, as Chapter 10 explained, because investors ask for more reward for more risk. A very safe project may deserve a lower rate.

> A project financed by one loan still uses the common rate.

Another point: suppose a project is paid for with one specific loan at 12%. Should we use its after-tax cost of 9%? No. Funds are pooled. If the firm uses up its borrowing power on this project, the next project will have to be paid for with more costly equity. So both projects should be judged at the rate of the pool, which is the WACC.

> Recap: retest, then decide.

*Before moving on.* Use the WACC as the discount rate in the net present value rule, or compare it with the internal rate of return. Use a higher rate for riskier projects. Always use the rate of the whole pool, not the rate of the loan that happens to be used.

## Worked Case: The Bakery Retests the Oven and the Van

> The case file ties the chapter together.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* In Chapter 7 the owner chose between two projects at a provisional rate of 10%. Each costs Tk 3,00,000 and lasts five years. The oven brings Tk 1,00,000 a year. The van brings Tk 50,000, Tk 80,000, Tk 1,10,000, Tk 1,40,000 and Tk 1,40,000 in years 1 to 5. The owner has now found the hurdle rate, 11.1%.

> Situation, question, reasoning, conclusion, check.

*Situation.* The new rate of 11.1% replaces the guess of 10%. *Question.* Does the oven remain the better choice, and are both projects still worth doing? *Reasoning.* The owner finds the present value of the inflows of each project at 11.1% and subtracts the outlay of Tk 3,00,000. Because the rate is not in the printed tables, the owner uses a calculator for the factors, 1 ÷ 1.111 raised to the year number, as shown in Chapter 3.

> Reading the figures.

Read each row from left to right: the factor turns the inflow of that year into today's taka. The totals are the present values of all the inflows. Because the factors are rounded to four places, the totals differ from a full calculator answer by a few taka. The full calculator totals are Tk 3,68,662 for the oven and Tk 3,64,632 for the van. We use these.

> Conclusion and check with a figure.

*Conclusion.* The net present value of the oven is Tk 3,68,662 − Tk 3,00,000 = Tk 68,662. For the van it is Tk 3,64,632 − Tk 3,00,000 = Tk 64,632. Both are above zero, so both are worth doing, and the oven is still ahead. The next figure shows the net present value of each project at different rates.

> How to read the figure.

The horizontal axis shows the discount rate and the vertical axis shows net present value in thousands of taka. A point on a line gives the value of the project at that rate. The lines fall as the rate rises, because later cash is worth less. The dotted line marks 11.1%. At this rate both lines stand above zero, with the oven above the van.

> The check agrees with the figure.

*Check.* The oven's line crosses zero at an internal rate of return of about 19.9%, and the van's at about 18.0%. Both are well above the hurdle of 11.1%, which agrees with the positive net present values. The two lines cross each other near 8.6%. Below that rate the van would be the better project, and above it the oven. The owner's hurdle is above 8.6%, so the oven stays the first choice, with a lead of about Tk 4,030.

> The result also shows how the figures compare with the guess.

At the guess of 10% the oven led by Tk 2,313. At 11.1% it leads by Tk 4,030. The ranking has not changed, but the values have fallen, from Tk 79,079 to Tk 68,662 for the oven. If the owner had used the cost of the 12% loan before tax, the hurdle would have been too high, and the projects would have looked less attractive than they really are.

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.

The cost of capital is the yearly price a firm pays for its funds. For the firm it is a cost; for the investor it is the required return; in earlier chapters it was the discount rate. Debt costs its interest rate multiplied by one less the tax rate. Preferred stock costs its dividend divided by what the firm receives. Common equity costs what the owners require, estimated by the capital asset pricing model or by the dividend method. Kept profit has the same cost as the owners' money. The weighted average cost of capital combines the costs by weight. It is the hurdle rate for projects of the firm's usual risk.

:box IN SHORT
The weighted average cost of capital is the sum of each fund's cost multiplied by its weight. It serves as the discount rate for projects that have the same risk as the firm.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Using the rate of one loan.

*Using the rate of the loan that pays for the project.* The funds of the firm are pooled. A project is judged at the cost of the pool, which is the WACC, and not at the price of one source.

> Forgetting tax.

*Using the interest rate without the tax step.* The interest saves tax, so the cost of debt after tax is lower than the interest rate. But the step does not apply to dividends.

> Thinking debt is always best.

*Believing that the cheapest source should be used alone.* Debt looks cheapest, but more debt raises the risk and the price of equity. The cost of capital belongs to the whole mix.

> Thinking kept profit is free.

*Treating retained earnings as costless.* The owners could have received that profit and invested it elsewhere. They require the same return as on the money they put in.

> Weights that do not add up.

*Weights that do not add to 1.* If the weights add to more or less than 1, the answer is wrong. Add them first. A second check is that the WACC must lie between the lowest and highest costs.

## In History

> A famous paper changed the way economists think about funds.

In 1958 two American economists, Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller, published a paper called "The Cost of Capital, Corporation Finance and the Theory of Investment". They asked whether a firm could lower its cost of capital merely by changing the mix of debt and equity. Under very simple conditions, with no tax and no cost of failure, they argued that the answer is no. The value of a firm depends on what its assets earn, and not on how the money to buy them was divided. Their later work, which added tax, showed why debt can still have an advantage. The two men received the Nobel Prize in Economics, Modigliani in 1985 and Miller in 1990.^3
