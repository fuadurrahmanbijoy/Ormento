> A shirt, a rise of 20%, and a fall of 20%: where does the price end?
A shopkeeper in a Dhaka market sells a shirt for Tk 100. In the festival season she raises the price by 20%. When the festival ends, she lowers the new price by 20%. A customer watches both changes and says, "Good, the shirt is back at Tk 100." The shopkeeper smiles and says nothing. Is the customer right?

> Work it with a pencil, and the answer changes.
Try it step by step. A rise of 20% on Tk 100 adds Tk 20, so the price becomes Tk 120. A fall of 20% now takes away one fifth of Tk 120, which is Tk 24. The new price is Tk 96. The customer is wrong by four taka. The reason hides in two small words: a percentage is always a percentage *of something*, and the something changed.

> A second puzzle: two true statements about the same profit.
A second puzzle lives in the bakery's books. The owner says, "Our profit rose by 20% this quarter." The bank manager says, "Your profit rose from 10 to 12 per cent of sales, which is a rise of two." Both speak the truth, yet their numbers differ. They are measuring the same event with two different tools. By the end of this chapter you will be able to say exactly which tool each person used.

> Finance runs on a few small tools of arithmetic, which this chapter teaches slowly.
Finance is the study of money, and money is counted with numbers. Later chapters will use a small set of tools again and again: percentages, powers, tables, and one standard layout that carries sales down to profit. None of these is difficult. Each is easy to misuse when we hurry. So this chapter moves slowly, one tool at a time, so that later chapters can move faster.

> You will need only the words from Chapter I.
*You will need:* the words sales, cost and profit from Chapter I, and the picture of the bakery that began there. The arithmetic itself is arithmetic you already know. We arrange it with care and give it names.

> Slow passages are flagged, and each section ends with a short recap.
Two signs will guide you. A note in the side column that says *Go slowly here* marks a passage that deserves a second reading. A short recap called *Before moving on* ends every section. Questions called *Pause and check* appear after the harder passages; their answers are at the back of the book.

## 3.1 Percentages, Ratios and Rounding

> We begin in the market, where a percentage is easy to feel.
A woman buys a shirt marked Tk 800. A sign says "10% off". She wants to know what she will pay. She thinks: ten out of every hundred taka are taken away. There are eight hundreds in 800, so Tk 80 is taken away. She pays Tk 720. This small sum already contains the whole of the first idea in this chapter.

@ Percentage | A part out of 100. So 25% means 25 out of every 100.
> A percentage is a part out of 100.
A **percentage** means a part out of 100. The sign % is read "per cent", which means "for each hundred". So 25% means 25 out of every 100, or one quarter. If 50 of your 200 customers buy cake, then 25 of every 100 customers buy cake, and we say that 25% of your customers buy cake. A percentage lets us compare parts of wholes of different sizes.

> Three different questions use percentages, and beginners often mix them.
Percentages answer three different questions. The first asks: what is 25% of a given amount? The second asks: this part is what percentage of that whole? The third asks: by what percentage did a number change? Each question has its own rule. We take them in turn. In this book every calculation is shown in the same order: *Given*, *Find*, *Formula*, *Steps*, *Answer*, *Check*.

### Finding a percentage of an amount

> To find a percentage of an amount, multiply the amount by the percentage and divide by 100.
The rule in words: the part equals the whole multiplied by the percentage, divided by 100. In symbols, part = whole × p ÷ 100, where p stands for the percentage. The word *whole* means the full amount that the percentage is taken of. In the shirt, the whole was Tk 800 and p was 10.

> First worked example: the bank's interest on the opening loan.
*Given.* On 2 January 2026 the bank lent the bakery Tk 4,00,000 at 12% a year. *Find.* The interest for one year. *Formula.* Interest = whole × p ÷ 100, with the whole equal to the loan and p equal to 12.

> The steps, the answer and a check by a different route.
*Steps.* First, 4,00,000 × 12 = 48,00,000. Second, 48,00,000 ÷ 100 = 48,000. *Answer.* The interest for one year is Tk 48,000. *Check.* Ten per cent of Tk 4,00,000 is Tk 40,000, and 2% is Tk 8,000. Together they make Tk 48,000, so the answer is sensible.

> A second, messier example uses a rate with a decimal part.
*Given.* A loan of Tk 3,75,000 carries interest at 9.5% a year. *Find.* One year's interest. *Steps.* 3,75,000 × 9.5 = 35,62,500, and 35,62,500 ÷ 100 = 35,625. *Answer.* Tk 35,625. *Check.* Ten per cent of Tk 3,75,000 is Tk 37,500, and half of 1% is Tk 1,875. Then 37,500 − 1,875 = Tk 35,625. A decimal in the rate changes nothing about the method.

> Pause and check: find 15% of Tk 2,40,000.
*Pause and check.* Use the same three steps to find 15% of Tk 2,40,000. Write the given, the formula and the check. The answer is at the back of the book.

### Finding what percentage a part is of a whole

> To find what percentage a part is of a whole, divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100.
Now we turn the question around. We know the part and the whole, and we want the percentage. The rule in words: the percentage equals the part divided by the whole, multiplied by 100. In symbols, p = part ÷ whole × 100. This rule is the first rule run backwards, and the check is to run it forwards again.

> Second worked example: how large is the bakery's variable cost compared with its sales?
*Given.* In the first three months of 2026, the bakery's sales were Tk 9,00,000. The cost of flour, sugar and other items that grow with each box baked was Tk 5,40,000. *Find.* What percentage of sales this cost is. *Formula.* p = part ÷ whole × 100.

> The steps, the answer and the check by running the rule forwards.
*Steps.* First, 5,40,000 ÷ 9,00,000 = 0.6. Second, 0.6 × 100 = 60. *Answer.* The cost is 60% of sales. *Check.* Run the first rule forwards: 60% of Tk 9,00,000 is 9,00,000 × 60 ÷ 100 = Tk 5,40,000, which is the part we began with. The answer is correct. We will meet this 60% again in section 3.4.

### Finding a percentage change

@! Go slowly here.
@ Percentage change | The change in a number, shown as a percentage of the old number.
> A percentage change compares the change with the old value, not the new one.
Here is the passage that settles the shirt puzzle, so read it twice. A **percentage change** means the change in a number, shown as a percentage of the old number. A bus fare rises from Tk 20 to Tk 25. The rise is Tk 5. Compare Tk 5 with the *old* fare of Tk 20: it is one quarter, so the fare rose by 25%. The old value is the whole in this rule.

> The rule in words and symbols.
The rule in words: first find the change, which is the new value less the old value. Then divide the change by the old value, and multiply by 100. In symbols, percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. A positive answer is a rise. A negative answer is a fall.

> A second example of the part-to-whole rule, with an awkward answer.
The rule does not always give a round answer. Later we will see that schools bought Tk 2,44,000 of the bakery's Tk 9,00,000 sales in the first quarter. Then p = 2,44,000 ÷ 9,00,000 × 100 = 27.11% to two decimal places. *Check:* 27.11% of 9,00,000 is 2,43,990, which is close to 2,44,000. The small gap of Tk 10 comes from rounding the percentage, and this is normal.

> Third worked example: the price of flour goes up, and then comes back down.
*Given.* The bakery's flour cost Tk 50 for one kilogram. A month later it cost Tk 56. *Find.* The percentage change from Tk 50 to Tk 56, and then the percentage change from Tk 56 back to Tk 50. *Formula.* Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.

> The steps and the answers show that the two changes are not equal.
*Steps for the rise.* The change is 56 − 50 = 6. Then 6 ÷ 50 = 0.12, and 0.12 × 100 = 12. *Steps for the fall.* The change is 50 − 56 = −6. Then −6 ÷ 56 = −0.107142..., and × 100 gives −10.71% to two decimal places. *Answer.* The price rose by 12%, and the return trip is a fall of 10.71%, not 12%.

> The check, and the reason: the old value changed.
*Check.* Increase Tk 50 by 12%: 50 + 6 = 56. Decrease Tk 56 by 10.71%: 10.71% of 56 is about 6.00, so 56 − 6 = 50. Both work. The two percentages differ because the old value in the first case was Tk 50 and in the second case was Tk 56. A rise and a fall of the same size, measured against different old values, give different percentages.

> Fig. 1 shows the shirt puzzle as three bars.
:fig The shirt puzzle. After a 20% rise and a 20% fall the price is Tk 96, not Tk 100. | fig_ch3_percent.svg

> The shirt puzzle is solved: the second 20% was taken of a larger amount.
Now we can settle the shirt. The rise of 20% was taken of Tk 100 and added Tk 20. The fall of 20% was taken of Tk 120 and removed Tk 24. Because the second percentage was taken of a larger whole, it removed more than the first added. To return from Tk 120 to Tk 100, the price must fall by 20 ÷ 120 × 100 = 16.67%, not 20%.

> A fall can be measured in the same way, as a negative change.
A percentage change can also be applied to a rate. Suppose the bank cuts its yearly rate from 12% to 10.5%. The change is 10.5 − 12 = −1.5. Divide by the old rate: −1.5 ÷ 12 = −0.125, so the rate fell by 12.5%. The gap is 1.5 percentage points, and the percentage change is 12.5%. Both statements are correct and they answer different questions.

@ Percentage point | The gap between two percentages. From 10% to 12% is two percentage points.
> Percentage points measure the gap between two percentages, which is not the same as percentage change.
The second puzzle needs one more term. A **percentage point** means the plain gap between two percentages. When the bakery's profit share of sales moves from 10% to 12%, the gap is two percentage points. That is the bank manager's number. But 12 is 20% more than 10, because 2 ÷ 10 = 0.20. That is the owner's number. Later chapters compare interest rates, so remember both tools: points for the gap, percent for the relative change.

> Pause and check: a loan rate moves from 12% to 14%.
*Pause and check.* A loan rate moves from 12% to 14%. State the change in percentage points. Then state the change as a percentage of the old rate. Compare your two answers with those at the back of the book.

> You should now be able to say how a percentage change is found.
*You should now be able to say:* "A percentage change is the change divided by the old value, times 100, and a rise and a fall of equal percentages do not cancel."

### Ratios

@ Ratio | One number compared with another, such as 3 to 2.
> A ratio compares two numbers by showing how many times one contains the other.
A **ratio** means one number compared with another. Ratios are written with a colon, as in 3 : 2, and read "3 to 2". At the start of the bakery, the owner put in Tk 6,00,000 and the bank lent Tk 4,00,000. The ratio of the owner's money to the bank's money is 6,00,000 : 4,00,000. Divide both numbers by 2,00,000 and the ratio becomes 3 : 2, which is the same comparison in smaller numbers.

> A ratio can also be written as one number, or as percentages of the total.
A ratio can be changed into a single number by dividing: 6,00,000 ÷ 4,00,000 = 1.5, so the owner put in 1.5 times as much as the bank. It can also be changed into percentages of the total. The total money was Tk 10,00,000. The owner's share is 6,00,000 ÷ 10,00,000 × 100 = 60%, and the bank's share is 40%. The three forms carry the same information. Later chapters will switch between them without warning, so practise all three.

### Rounding

@ Rounding | Replacing a number by a nearby shorter one, such as 7.1428 by 7.14.
> Rounding shortens a number, and a fixed rule keeps everyone's answers equal.
Many divisions never end. Dividing 1 by 7 gives 0.142857142857..., and no one wants to write it all. **Rounding** means replacing a long number by a nearby shorter one. We choose how many decimal places to keep. Look at the first digit we would drop. If it is 5 or more, increase the last kept digit by one. If it is 4 or less, leave the last kept digit as it is. So 7.1428 becomes 7.14 to two decimal places, and 7.1463 becomes 7.15.

> This book's rounding habits, so that your answers match ours.
In this book, money is rounded to the nearest whole taka unless the amount is small, when we keep paisa. Percentages and ratios are rounded to two decimal places. A word of warning: round only at the end. If you round in the middle of a calculation, the small errors travel into the answer and grow. Section 3.6 shows this with real numbers.

> A recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* A percentage is a part out of 100. Three questions use it: the part of a whole, the percentage a part makes, and the change from an old value. A ratio compares two numbers. Round at the end, to two decimal places for rates.

## 3.2 Powers and Roots; Using a Calculator

@! Go slowly here.
> Repeated growth is repeated multiplication, which is why powers matter to finance.
Suppose a rumour starts with one person. That person tells two friends, and each of those tells two more. After one round, two people know. After two rounds, four know. After three rounds, eight know. Each round multiplies the number by 2. We multiplied 2 by itself three times, and the result is 8. Finance meets this pattern whenever money grows by the same percentage each year. Chapter IV builds on it, so we meet it now in its simplest form.

@ Power | A number multiplied by itself a stated number of times. (In daily life, strength or authority.)
> A power is a number multiplied by itself a stated number of times.
@ Exponent | The small raised figure that says how many times a number is multiplied by itself.
A **power** means a number multiplied by itself a stated number of times. We write the number, and a small raised figure beside it. So 2³ means 2 × 2 × 2, and the answer is 8. We say "2 to the power 3". The raised figure is called the **exponent**. It tells us how many times the number appears in the multiplication. The exponent 2 is also called "squared", and the exponent 3 is also called "cubed".

> The exponent counts the multiplications, not the number itself.
Notice that the exponent counts the *copies* of the number, not the number of times we multiply. In 2³ there are three copies of 2 but only two multiplication signs. A common slip is to confuse 2³ with 2 × 3. The first is 8 and the second is 6. In finance the number is usually close to 1, such as 1.08, and then the powers grow slowly and steadily.

> First worked example: a price that rises 10% each year for three years.
*Given.* A price of Tk 1 rises by 10% each year for three years. After one year, it is multiplied by 1.10. *Find.* The number that Tk 1 is multiplied by after three years. *Formula.* Multiplier = 1.10³ = 1.10 × 1.10 × 1.10.

> The steps, the answer and a check.
*Steps.* First, 1.10 × 1.10 = 1.21. Second, 1.21 × 1.10 = 1.331. *Answer.* After three years, Tk 1 becomes Tk 1.331. *Check.* If each year added a fixed 10 paisa, Tk 1 would reach Tk 1.30. The true answer is a little larger, because later rises are taken of larger amounts, as in the shirt puzzle. So 1.331 is sensible.

> Second worked example: a messier number, 1.08 to the power 5.
*Given.* The number 1.08 raised to the power 5. *Find.* The value of 1.08⁵. *Formula.* 1.08⁵ = 1.08 × 1.08 × 1.08 × 1.08 × 1.08. *Steps.* 1.08² = 1.1664. Then 1.1664 × 1.08 = 1.259712. Then 1.259712 × 1.08 = 1.36048896. Then 1.36048896 × 1.08 = 1.4693280768. *Answer.* 1.08⁵ = 1.4693 to four decimal places.

> The calculator does the same work in one step.
The calculator key for powers is usually marked ^ or xʸ or yˣ. Type 1.08, press the power key, type 5, and press equals. The display shows 1.469328077, which agrees with our hand work. *Check.* The answer must be a little above 1 + 5 × 0.08 = 1.40, because growth on growth adds a little more. It is. Learn where the power key sits on your own calculator before you need it in a hurry.

@ Order of operations | The fixed order in which a calculation is worked: brackets, powers, times and divide, add and subtract.
> A calculation has a fixed order of work, and ignoring it gives wrong answers.
A formula often mixes brackets, powers, multiplication and addition. The **order of operations** is the fixed order in which we work them: brackets first, then powers, then multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right. Consider 1,00,000 × (1 + 0.08)³. The bracket gives 1.08. The power gives 1.08³ = 1.259712. The multiplication gives Tk 1,25,971.

> Typing a formula in the wrong order is a very common slip.
Compare two wrong ways. If you type 1,00,000 × 1 + 0.08³, the calculator works the power first and then multiplies, and your answer is Tk 1,00,000.0005, which shows almost no growth at all. If you type 1.08 × 3, you have multiplied instead of raising to a power and your answer is 3.24. Always write the bracket, or press the bracket keys, and then check that the size of your answer is sensible.

@ Root | The number that, raised to a stated power, gives a given number. (In daily life, part of a plant.)
> A root undoes a power, and finance uses roots to find growth rates.
A power takes a number and builds a larger one. A **root** undoes this, and returns to the number we started with. Since 12 × 12 = 144, the square root of 144 is 12. Since 1.08⁵ = 1.469328, the fifth root of 1.469328 is 1.08. Roots matter to us because the growth rate of money, such as 8% a year, is hidden inside a power. A root uncovers it. Chapter IV does this, and Chapter VIII uses square roots again.

> Third worked example: a square root.
*Given.* The number 8. *Find.* The square root of 8. *Formula.* Find the number that, multiplied by itself, gives 8. *Steps.* Press the square root key (√) on your calculator, then type 8. The display shows 2.828427... *Answer.* The square root of 8 is 2.83 to two decimal places. *Check.* 2.83 × 2.83 = 8.0089, which is very near 8. We will use this exact number in Chapter VIII.

> Other roots are found with a fractional power.
For roots other than the square root, many calculators have a root key. Where this is absent, raise the number to the power of one divided by the root. The fifth root is found by raising to the power 1 ÷ 5 = 0.2. Type 1.469328, press the power key, type 0.2, and press equals. The display shows 1.08. This is the method we will use in Chapter IV to find a rate.

> A power with a minus sign means one divided by the power.
There is one more form. A power with a minus sign in the exponent means one divided by the ordinary power. So 1.08⁻⁵ means 1 ÷ 1.08⁵ = 1 ÷ 1.469328 = 0.680583. Many calculators have a key marked 1/x for the division. Chapter IV needs this form to bring money back from the future. For now, note only that 1.08⁵ makes a number larger and 1.08⁻⁵ makes it smaller.

> A worked example of a minus sign in the exponent.
*Given.* The number 1.10 with the exponent −3. *Find.* The value of 1.10⁻³. *Formula.* 1.10⁻³ = 1 ÷ 1.10³. *Steps.* We found in the first example that 1.10³ = 1.331. Then 1 ÷ 1.331 = 0.7513 to four decimal places. *Answer.* 1.10⁻³ = 0.7513. *Check.* Multiply back: 0.7513 × 1.331 = 1.0000 to four decimals. A number and its reciprocal always multiply to 1.

> Pause and check: powers and roots.
*Pause and check.* Calculate 1.06⁴ by repeated multiplication, and then with the power key. Then find the square root of 50, to two decimal places. The answers are at the back of the book.

> A recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* A power is a number multiplied by itself, and the exponent counts the copies. Follow the order of operations. A root undoes a power, and a minus sign in the exponent means one divided by the power. You should now be able to say: "1.08⁵ means 1.08 multiplied by itself five times, and a root takes me back."

## 3.3 Reading and Using a Table

> A table is an everyday tool, and the bakery's records use it too.
Think of a bus timetable. Down the left side are the stops. Across the top are the times of the buses. To find when the 9 o'clock bus reaches your stop, you run one finger along the row for your stop and another down the column for 9 o'clock, and read the number where they meet. That small habit is all that reading a table requires. Finance uses tables constantly, and Chapter IV introduces tables of numbers that save long calculations.

@ Table | Numbers arranged in rows and columns so that each can be found quickly. (In daily life, a piece of furniture.)
@ Row | One line of a table that runs across the page.
@ Column | One line of a table that runs down the page.
> A table arranges numbers in rows and columns, and each number sits in a cell.
A **table** means numbers arranged in rows and columns so that each can be found quickly. A **row** runs across the page. A **column** runs down the page. The place where a row and a column meet is called a *cell*, and it holds one number. In this book, every table has a caption above it in small capitals, a heading row, and ruled lines only at the top, under the headings, and at the bottom.

> The bakery's boxes of buns are best shown in a table, which we first introduce in words.
The bakery sold the same number of boxes of buns in each of the first three months of 2026, but the boxes went to different buyers. The shop counter sold to walk-in customers. Some boxes went to schools and some to offices. The table below shows boxes sold, with the three channels in rows and the three months in columns. A final row and a final column hold totals.

:table Boxes of buns sold, by channel and month, January to March 2026
| Channel | January | February | March | Quarter |
| Shop counter | 4,000 | 3,800 | 3,700 | 11,500 |
| Schools | 2,000 | 2,100 | 2,000 | 6,100 |
| Offices | 1,500 | 1,600 | 1,800 | 4,900 |
| All channels | 7,500 | 7,500 | 7,500 | 22,500 |

> To read a single cell, find its row, find its column, and read where they meet.
Let us read the table. How many boxes did the schools take in February? Find the row marked Schools and the column marked February. Where they meet, the cell holds 2,100. So the schools took 2,100 boxes in February. To read a row total, look at the last column: the shop counter sold 11,500 boxes in the quarter. To read a column total, look at the last row: in March, all channels together sold 7,500 boxes.

> Totals give a check: the row totals and the column totals must agree.
A table with totals carries its own check. Add the three row totals: 11,500 + 6,100 + 4,900 = 22,500. Add the three monthly totals: 7,500 + 7,500 + 7,500 = 22,500. Both sums give the same grand total, and the grand total sits in the bottom right cell. If the two sums disagree, a number is wrong. This check costs ten seconds and saves many mistakes.

> A table can be built into a second table by using percentages.
Tables become useful when we ask questions of them. What share of all boxes did each channel take? Divide each channel's quarter total by the grand total and multiply by 100, as in section 3.1. For the shop counter, 11,500 ÷ 22,500 × 100 = 51.11%. The next table gives all three shares, and the sales in taka, because each box sold for Tk 40.

:table Share of boxes and sales, by channel, first quarter 2026
| Channel | Boxes | Share of boxes | Sales (Tk) |
| Shop counter | 11,500 | 51.11% | 4,60,000 |
| Schools | 6,100 | 27.11% | 2,44,000 |
| Offices | 4,900 | 21.78% | 1,96,000 |
| All channels | 22,500 | 100.00% | 9,00,000 |

> The shares must sum to 100%, and the sales must agree with the quarter's total.
Check the table. The three shares add to 51.11 + 27.11 + 21.78 = 100.00. The three sales amounts add to Tk 9,00,000, which is the quarter's sales in the profit layout of section 3.5. And 22,500 boxes × Tk 40 = Tk 9,00,000. Two different routes reach the same total. When several routes agree, we trust the numbers.

> Another common table gives a number to use according to which band a case falls in.
A second kind of table gives a number to *use*. Imagine that the bakery's van charges a delivery fee by distance: Tk 20 for 0 to 5 kilometres, Tk 35 for 6 to 10 kilometres, and Tk 60 for 11 to 20 kilometres. A school is 8 kilometres away. Eight lies in the band 6 to 10, so the fee is Tk 35. Chapter IV uses tables in this way, to find a number that we then multiply.

> Always read the title, the headings and the units of a table before reading a number.
A habit worth forming: read the caption and the headings before reading any number. The caption of the second table says that sales are in taka and that the period is the first quarter. Without this, a cell such as 4,60,000 has no meaning. Also check whether a table shows amounts, percentages or both, because a column of shares looks much like a column of boxes.

> Pause and check: reading the channel table.
*Pause and check.* From the first table, find the percentage change in the boxes sold to offices from January to March. Then find the share of all boxes that schools took in February. The answers are at the back of the book.

> A recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* A table has rows, columns and cells, and you read a cell where a row and a column meet. Totals give a check. A table can be turned into shares with the rules of section 3.1. You should now be able to say: "I can find any number in a table and check the totals."

## 3.4 Fixed and Variable Costs

@! Go slowly here.
> A tea seller shows the idea in daily life before we use it in the bakery.
A woman runs a tea stall. She pays Tk 6,000 each month for the stall, whether she sells 100 cups or 3,000. She also pays Tk 4 for the tea leaves, milk and sugar in each cup. In a slow month she sells 1,000 cups, and in a busy month she sells 2,000. Her stall cost does not change. Her cost for ingredients changes with every cup. Two kinds of cost live side by side in her little business.

@ Fixed cost | A cost that stays the same however much the firm makes or sells.
> A fixed cost stays the same as the amount sold changes.
A **fixed cost** means a cost that stays the same however much the firm makes or sells. The stall rent of Tk 6,000 is a fixed cost. In a bakery, the shop rent and the monthly wages of permanent staff are fixed costs. They must be paid in a quiet month and in a busy one.

@ Variable cost | A cost that rises and falls with the amount the firm makes or sells.
@ Unit | One item of what the firm sells, such as one box of buns.
> A variable cost changes in step with the number of units made.
A **variable cost** means a cost that rises and falls with the amount the firm makes or sells. The tea leaves, milk and sugar are variable costs. We measure the amount in **units**: a unit is one item of what the firm sells, such as one cup of tea or one box of buns. If each unit carries a variable cost of Tk 4, then 1,000 units carry Tk 4,000 and 2,000 units carry Tk 8,000.

> Total cost is the fixed cost plus the variable cost per unit times the number of units.
The rule in words: total cost equals the fixed cost plus the variable cost of each unit multiplied by the number of units. In symbols, total cost = F + v × q, where F is the fixed cost, v is the variable cost per unit, and q is the number of units. The tea stall with 1,000 cups: 6,000 + 4 × 1,000 = Tk 10,000. With 2,000 cups: 6,000 + 4 × 2,000 = Tk 14,000.

> Cost for each unit falls as more units are made, because the fixed cost is shared.
Look at the cost of one cup. At 1,000 cups, Tk 10,000 ÷ 1,000 = Tk 10 per cup. At 2,000 cups, Tk 14,000 ÷ 2,000 = Tk 7 per cup. Doubling the cups did not double the cost, because the rent was shared among more cups. This fact will matter greatly in Chapter IX, where we ask why a small rise in sales can bring a large rise in profit.

> The bakery's own numbers: a fixed cost of Tk 80,000 a month and a variable cost of Tk 24 a box.
Now the bakery. In the first quarter of 2026 the bakery's fixed cost was Tk 2,40,000, which is Tk 80,000 each month. Each box of buns cost Tk 24 in ingredients and other variable items, and each box sold for Tk 40. We will build a table of total cost at three levels of monthly output.

:table Monthly cost of making boxes of buns
| Boxes made | Fixed cost (Tk) | Variable cost (Tk) | Total cost (Tk) | Cost per box (Tk) |
| 5,000 | 80,000 | 1,20,000 | 2,00,000 | 40.00 |
| 7,500 | 80,000 | 1,80,000 | 2,60,000 | 34.67 |

> How to read the cost table.
Read the middle row. At 7,500 boxes, the variable cost is 24 × 7,500 = Tk 1,80,000. Add the fixed cost of Tk 80,000 and the total cost is Tk 2,60,000. Divide by 7,500 boxes and each box carries Tk 34.67. Look down the last column: the cost per box falls as the boxes rise, from Tk 40.00 to Tk 32.00. The fixed cost is the reason.

> Contribution is what each unit sold adds toward the fixed costs and the profit.
@ Contribution | The selling price of one unit less its variable cost.
**Contribution** means the selling price of one unit less its variable cost. A box sells for Tk 40 and carries Tk 24 of variable cost, so each box has a contribution of 40 − 24 = Tk 16. Each box sold adds Tk 16 toward paying the fixed cost. Once the fixed cost is paid, further contribution is profit before interest and tax. At 7,500 boxes the total contribution is 7,500 × 16 = Tk 1,20,000. Subtract the fixed cost of Tk 80,000 and Tk 40,000 remains each month.

> A second example of contribution, with a larger price.
*Given.* A cake sells for Tk 500 and carries Tk 320 of variable cost. *Find.* The contribution of one cake, and its share of the price. *Steps.* 500 − 320 = Tk 180. Then 180 ÷ 500 × 100 = 36%. *Answer.* Each cake contributes Tk 180, which is 36% of its price. *Check.* Variable cost is 320 ÷ 500 = 64% of the price, and 100% − 64% = 36%.

> A cost is not fixed or variable by its name, but by how it behaves.
Here are the exceptions, and they matter. First, no cost is fixed for ever. If the bakery needs a second oven (Chapter II), the rent of extra space and the wages of extra staff would rise, so the fixed cost climbs in steps. We say a cost is fixed *within a range of output and for a period of time*. Second, some costs are mixed. An electricity bill may have a fixed monthly charge plus a charge for each unit of power used. We separate such a cost into its fixed part and its variable part.

> The same cost can look different from the owner's and the manager's seats.
Viewpoint helps. The owner sees the fixed cost as a promise that must be paid even when no one buys. The shop manager sees the variable cost as the part that moves with her work: each box she sells brings a cost of Tk 24 but also adds Tk 16 of contribution. Both views are correct, and both use the same two labels.

> Pause and check: sort the costs.
*Pause and check.* The bakery pays shop rent, the cost of flour, the wages of two permanent bakers, and the packaging for each box. Classify each as fixed or variable. The answer is at the back of the book.

> A recap of the section, with the sentence to test yourself.
*Before moving on.* A fixed cost stays the same as sales change, and a variable cost rises with each unit. Total cost is F plus v times q. Contribution is selling price less variable cost for each unit. You should now be able to say: "Fixed cost is shared among more units as output rises, so the cost of each unit falls."

## 3.5 The Profit Layout: Sales to Net Profit

> The profit layout is a fixed ladder that carries sales down to profit, one step at a time.
Every firm in this book reports its profit in the same order. We call this order the *profit layout*. It starts with sales at the top. At each step, one kind of cost is taken away, and a new subtotal appears. The last subtotal is the profit that belongs to the owners. Learning the ladder once will save you from confusion in Chapters VI, IX, XIII, XIV and XV.

> The first steps: from sales, take variable cost to find contribution, then fixed cost to find EBIT.
@ EBIT | Profit before interest and tax: sales less variable cost and fixed cost.
The first step takes the variable cost from sales. The result is the total contribution, as in section 3.4. The second step takes the fixed cost from the contribution. We give the result a name: **EBIT**, which means profit before interest and tax. The letters stand for Earnings Before Interest and Tax. EBIT is the profit of the firm's work itself, before we count what it pays to lenders and to the government.

> Interest expense is the price of borrowed money, and it is taken next.
@ Interest expense | The money a firm pays lenders for using their money.
@ Profit before tax | Profit left after interest expense and before tax is paid.
The third step takes the interest. **Interest expense** means the money a firm pays to its lenders for using their money. The bakery's bank loan was Tk 4,00,000 at 12% a year, and interest for one year is Tk 48,000 (section 3.1). The first quarter is one quarter of a year, so interest expense is Tk 48,000 × 3 ÷ 12 = Tk 12,000. What remains is **profit before tax**, the profit that the government will tax.

> Tax is the government's share of profit, and it is taken fourth.
@ Tax | Money a firm must pay the government on its profit.
@ Net profit | The profit left after interest and tax have been paid.
The fourth step takes the tax. **Tax** means money that a firm must pay to the government on its profit. The rate depends on the law and on the kind of company, and it changes from time to time.^1 In our illustrations we use a rate of 25% of profit before tax. For the bakery, 25% of Tk 1,08,000 is Tk 27,000. What remains is **net profit**, the profit left after interest and tax.

> Net profit is the last line before payments to special owners.
@ Preferred dividend | A fixed payment to a special kind of owner, taught in Chapter XIV.
The profit layout has one more line, which we only preview. Some firms have owners of a special kind who are promised a fixed payment before the ordinary owners receive anything. That payment is called a **preferred dividend**. Chapter XIV explains this kind of owner. The bakery has none, so the line is zero. After this line, what remains belongs to the ordinary owners.

> First, a small clean example of the ladder, with round numbers.
Before the bakery, take a small stationery shop with round numbers. *Given.* Sales Tk 1,00,000; variable cost Tk 60,000; fixed cost Tk 20,000; interest expense Tk 5,000; tax at 25%. *Steps.* Contribution = 1,00,000 − 60,000 = 40,000. EBIT = 40,000 − 20,000 = 20,000. Profit before tax = 20,000 − 5,000 = 15,000. Tax = 15,000 × 25% = 3,750. Net profit = 15,000 − 3,750 = Tk 11,250. *Check.* Net profit is below EBIT by interest and tax together: 5,000 + 3,750 = 8,750, and 20,000 − 8,750 = 11,250.

> A table lays the whole ladder out for the bakery's first quarter of 2026.
The table below follows the ladder with the bakery's figures. The first column names the line, the second shows the working, and the third shows the amount in taka. Read it from top to bottom, and notice that each subtotal is the previous subtotal less one cost.

To read Fig. 3, begin at the top. The long shaded bar is sales of Tk 9,00,000. > Percentages of sales show how each cost compares with sales, using section 3.1.
We can describe the ladder in percentages of sales. Variable cost is 60%. Contribution is 40%. EBIT is 1,20,000 ÷ 9,00,000 × 100 = 13.33%. Net profit is 81,000 ÷ 9,00,000 × 100 = 9.00%. Each line can be compared with sales in this way, and the same method will compare one firm with another. The owner and the bank manager of our opening puzzle used exactly such percentages.

> Two common confusions: EBIT is not net profit, and the order cannot change.
Two warnings. First, EBIT and net profit are different numbers. EBIT is Tk 1,20,000 and net profit is Tk 81,000. The gap is the interest and the tax. A firm that borrows heavily can have a high EBIT and a small net profit. Second, tax is taken from profit *after* interest, because the law lets a firm deduct interest before it calculates tax. Reversing the order would give a wrong tax.

> A recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* The ladder runs: sales, less variable cost, contribution, less fixed cost, EBIT, less interest, profit before tax, less tax, net profit, less preferred dividend. You should now be able to say: "EBIT is the profit before interest and tax, and net profit is what remains after both."

## 3.6 Decimals, Units and Sensible Checking

> Numbers in this book follow the lakh style of grouping.
Bangladesh groups large numbers in lakhs and crores, and so does this book. After the first three digits from the right, the commas come every two digits. So Tk 6,00,000 is six lakh, which is 600,000 in the international style. One lakh is 1,00,000, and one crore is 100 lakh, written 1,00,00,000. When you read a number with commas, count the digits carefully, because one misplaced comma changes the amount tenfold.

> A percentage, a decimal and a fraction are three ways to write one number.
A percentage can be written as a decimal or as a fraction, and later chapters use all three. To change a percentage to a decimal, divide by 100: 12% = 0.12 and 8.5% = 0.085. To change it to a fraction, write it over 100: 12% = 12/100 = 3/25. To go back, multiply the decimal by 100. Formulas in this book use the decimal form, so a rate of 8% enters a formula as 0.08.

> Every number needs its label, and every answer needs a size check.
Always write what a number measures: Tk, boxes, per cent, years. A bare 7,500 might be boxes or taka. Mixing labels is a common cause of wrong answers. For example, a percentage must be changed to a decimal before it is multiplied: 12% becomes 0.12. A good habit is to estimate before you calculate. If you expect an answer near 1,26,000 and the calculator shows 12,600, a decimal point or a comma has gone astray.

> Rounding early lets small errors grow, as this example shows.
Now we show why rounding should come last. *Given.* We want 1,00,000 × 1.08⁵, and the value of 1.08⁵ is 1.469328. *Wrong route.* Round 1.469328 to 1.47 and multiply: 1,00,000 × 1.47 = Tk 1,47,000. *Right route.* Keep all the digits: 1,00,000 × 1.469328 = Tk 1,46,933. The early rounding caused an error of Tk 67 on a sum of one lakh. On a larger sum, or in a longer calculation, the error would be larger.

> A short list of three checking habits.
Three habits make you reliable. First, estimate the size of the answer before calculating. Second, check the answer by a different route, such as running a rule backwards or comparing two totals. Third, ask whether the answer is sensible: a profit larger than the sales, or a negative cost, is a signal to stop and look again. These habits cost very little time and they will be used in every chapter that follows.

> A recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* Lakh grouping puts commas every two digits after the first three. Label every number. Estimate first, keep all digits until the end, and check by another route.

## The Worked Case: The Bakery's First Quarter

> The case file for the first quarter, and the owner's question.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* Sundial Bakery, Dhaka. Quarter from 1 January to 31 March 2026. Sales Tk 9,00,000 (22,500 boxes at Tk 40). Variable cost Tk 5,40,000 (Tk 24 a box). Fixed cost Tk 2,40,000. Interest Tk 12,000 (loan Tk 4,00,000 at 12% a year). Illustrative tax rate 25%. The owner asks what net profit would be if sales rose by 10% with the same unit prices and the same costs per box.

> The situation, the question and the plan for the case.
*Situation.* The owner has seen the profit layout and wants to plan the next quarter. *Question.* If the number of boxes sold rises by 10%, by what percentage does net profit change? *Reasoning plan.* We will rebuild the layout with the new number of boxes. The fixed cost and the interest expense do not change when boxes rise, because they are fixed. Variable cost rises with the boxes. We will then compare the net profit with the old one using the percentage change rule.

> Step one: the new boxes and the new sales.
*Given.* Boxes rise by 10%: 22,500 × 1.10 = 24,750 boxes. *Steps.* Sales = 24,750 × 40 = Tk 9,90,000. Variable cost = 24,750 × 24 = Tk 5,94,000. Contribution = 9,90,000 − 5,94,000 = Tk 3,96,000. Check: contribution per box is Tk 16, and 24,750 × 16 = Tk 3,96,000.

> Step two: the fixed cost and interest stay the same; tax and net profit follow.
*Steps.* The fixed cost remains Tk 2,40,000. EBIT = 3,96,000 − 2,40,000 = Tk 1,56,000. Interest expense remains Tk 12,000. Profit before tax = 1,56,000 − 12,000 = Tk 1,44,000. Tax at 25% = Tk 36,000. Net profit = 1,44,000 − 36,000 = Tk 1,08,000.

> Step three: compare the two net profits with the percentage change rule.
*Steps.* The old net profit was Tk 81,000. The change is 1,08,000 − 81,000 = Tk 27,000. Then 27,000 ÷ 81,000 = 0.3333, and × 100 gives 33.33%. *Answer.* A rise of 10% in boxes sold raises net profit by 33.33%. *Check.* EBIT rose from Tk 1,20,000 to Tk 1,56,000, which is a rise of 30%. Net profit rose by even more, because the interest of Tk 12,000 did not rise.

> The conclusion and the lesson for later chapters.
*Conclusion.* Sales rose by 10% but net profit rose by 33.33%. The reason lies in section 3.4: the fixed cost and the interest were shared over more boxes, so each extra box brought its full Tk 16 of contribution into profit. The same effect works downwards, and a fall of 10% would cut profit sharply. Chapter IX explains this magnifying effect and measures it. For now, notice only that the percentage change in profit can differ greatly from the percentage change in sales.

## Summary

> A summary of the chapter's tools.
A percentage is a part out of 100. It answers three questions: the part of a whole, the percentage a part makes, and the change from an old value. A rise and a fall of equal percentages do not cancel, because each is taken of a different whole. A ratio compares two numbers. A power is a number multiplied by itself, a root undoes it, and a minus sign in the exponent means one divided by the power. Round only at the end.

> A summary of the table, cost and profit ideas.
A table is read where a row and a column meet, and totals give a check. A fixed cost stays the same as sales change, and a variable cost changes with each unit. Contribution is price less variable cost for each unit. The profit layout runs from sales, through contribution, EBIT, profit before tax and net profit. Fixed costs and interest make profit change by a larger percentage than sales.

> A café, and a fall of 10% in sales.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: taking a percentage of the wrong whole.
*Taking the percentage of the new value.* A fall of 20% is taken of the value *before* the fall. Many students divide by the new value, and so get the wrong percentage. Always ask: what is the old value?

> Mistake two: believing a rise and a fall of equal percentages cancel.
*Believing that +20% and −20% cancel.* They do not. After a rise of 20% and a fall of 20%, Tk 100 becomes Tk 96. The second percentage is taken of a different amount.

> Mistake three: mixing percentage points and percent.
*Mixing percentage points with per cent.* A rate that moves from 10% to 12% has risen by two percentage points and by 20%. Say which you mean.

> Mistake four: rounding in the middle of a calculation.
*Rounding too early.* Keep all digits until the final line. Rounding 1.469328 to 1.47 changed an answer by Tk 67 on one lakh.

> Mistake five: confusing EBIT with net profit, and fixed with variable costs.
*Treating all costs as variable.* If every cost rose with sales, profit would rise by the same percentage as sales. Fixed costs make it rise faster. *Treating EBIT as the last line.* Interest and tax still have to be paid after EBIT.

> Mistake six: typing a formula in the wrong order.
*Ignoring the order of operations.* Brackets come first, then powers. A formula such as 1,00,000 × (1.08)³ must be typed with its bracket, and the result must look sensible.
