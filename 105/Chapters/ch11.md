:chapter XI | Valuing Bonds and Shares
:mood What is a promise worth today?
:desc This chapter shows how to put a price on a security by adding up the present value of everything it will pay. It teaches the features of a bond, the value of a bond with yearly and half-yearly interest, and why a bond sells at a discount, at a premium or at par. It then values a share from its dividends and compares bonds with shares.

> A neighbour offers to sell you a promise of future money, and you must name a fair price.

A neighbour who owns a small shop offers you a piece of paper. On it he has written that he will pay the holder Tk 100 at the end of each year for five years, and Tk 1,000 at the end of the fifth year as well. He asks Tk 1,000 for the paper. You stare at it. The total of the payments is Tk 1,500, and you are asked to pay Tk 1,000 now. Is that a good price, a poor price, or a fair one?

> The answer needs two things you already have: the timing of money and the return that you could earn elsewhere.

The answer cannot come from adding the payments. A taka paid in five years is worth less than a taka today, as Chapter 4 showed, and the paper's risk matters too, as Chapter 10 showed. To price the paper you must know when each payment arrives and what return you could earn on another use of your money of equal risk. You have both tools. The first is present value. The second is the required return.

> This chapter turns those two tools into a method for pricing any promise of future money.

This chapter joins the two tools. We start with a single idea that prices every promise of money: its value today is the present value of all that it will pay. We then use the idea on two kinds of paper. The first is the **bond**, which pays a fixed sum each year and repays a fixed sum at the end. The second is the share, which pays a dividend that can rise over time. At the end we put them side by side.

> Before this chapter, you should know present value, annuity factors, and the required return.

*You will need:* from Chapter 5, the present value of an annuity and of a single sum, together with the factor tables; from Chapter 10, the required return, which is the same idea as the discount rate.

## 11.1 Value and Price: The Fundamental Idea

@! Go slowly here.
> A farmer's fruit tree gives a plain picture of how an asset is valued.

Begin with a fruit tree. A farmer owns a mango tree that will give fruit worth Tk 5,000 every year for the next ten years, and then it will die. What is the tree worth to him today? He could sell it for a price, and a buyer would think carefully. The buyer would not pay Tk 50,000, the total of the ten harvests, because the later harvests arrive later. He would pay the present value of ten harvests of Tk 5,000, discounted at the return that he could earn on another use of the money. The tree is worth what it will give, brought back to today.

> The value of anything that pays money later is the present value of its payments.

@ Intrinsic value | What an asset is worth, found by adding the present value of everything it is expected to pay.

This idea has a name. The **intrinsic value** of an asset means what the asset is worth, found by adding up the present value of everything it is expected to pay. It is a value that comes from the asset's own future payments. It does not come from what other people happen to be offering today. The rule has three parts, and each one is something you have already learned.

> The rule has three parts: the payments, their timing, and the discount rate.

First, list the payments the asset will make, and the year of each. Second, bring each payment back to today by dividing by (1 + r) for each year of waiting, where r is the discount rate. Third, add the results. In symbols, the intrinsic value is V = CF1 ÷ (1 + r) + CF2 ÷ (1 + r)² + ... + CFn ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ, where CF1 is the payment at the end of year 1, and so on. The discount rate r is the required return of Chapter 10: the least return that investors will accept for an asset of this risk.

> A security is a tradable claim to future payments, such as a bond or a share.

@ Security | A tradable claim to future payments, such as a bond or a share.

We use the rule for **securities**. A security is a tradable claim to future payments, such as a bond or a share. People buy and sell securities every day, and the price paid for one is its market price. The intrinsic value is the value that the rule gives. The price and the value are different things, and the next paragraph shows why the difference matters.

> Price is what people pay, and value is what the payments justify.

If the price is below the intrinsic value, the security is cheap, because you pay less than its payments are worth. If the price is above it, the security is dear. Chapter 10 told us that in an efficient market the two stay close, because prices already absorb what is known. We use the intrinsic value as the standard, and we treat any gap between it and the price as a signal to look more closely.

> An everyday bridge: a loan to a friend is a security with one payment.

An everyday bridge may help. You lend Tk 10,000 to a friend, who promises to repay Tk 11,000 in one year. If your required return is 10 per cent, the value of the promise is Tk 11,000 ÷ 1.10 = Tk 10,000, which is exactly what you lent. If your required return were 15 per cent, the value would be Tk 11,000 ÷ 1.15 = Tk 9,565 (to the nearest taka). The promise is the same in both cases, but its value to you differs, because your required return differs. We shall see the same thing with bonds.

> Before moving on: the value of a security is the present value of what it will pay, discounted at the required return.

*Before moving on.* The intrinsic value of a security is the present value of all its expected payments, found with the required return. You should now be able to say: "A promise of Tk 1,100 in one year is worth Tk 1,000 today if my required return is 10 per cent."

## 11.2 Bond Features

> A bond is a loan split into small units, so that many lenders can each take a part.

Chapter 1 explained that a lender gives money and expects it back with interest. Suppose a firm needs Tk 10 crore. Few lenders have that much. The firm can split the loan into 1,00,000 units of Tk 1,000 each and sell the units one by one to many lenders. A unit of such a loan is a bond. A bond is a loan split into units that can be traded. The person who buys one has lent Tk 1,000 to the firm, and may later sell the unit to someone else.

> The firm that issues a bond is called the issuer, and the buyer is the bondholder.

The firm or government that borrows by selling bonds is the **issuer**, and the person who holds a bond is the bondholder. The bondholder is a lender and not an owner. This matters, because lenders are paid before owners, and they have no vote in the firm. Chapter 13 describes the kinds of long-term debt, including debentures and mortgages, and Chapter 14 describes owners' shares. In this chapter we take only the features that are needed to value a bond.

> Four features fix a bond's payments: face value, coupon rate, interest payment, and maturity.

@ Face value | The sum that a bond repays at its maturity; also called par.
@ Coupon rate | The yearly interest rate on a bond's face value.
@ Maturity | The date on which a bond is repaid.

Four features fix everything that a bond pays. The **face value** means the sum that the bond repays at the end. It is also called *par*, and it is the amount on which interest is calculated. The **coupon rate** means the yearly interest rate on the face value. The coupon rate times the face value gives the **coupon**, which is the yearly interest payment in taka. The **maturity** means the date on which the bond is repaid, so the time to maturity is the number of years left until that date. For example, a bond with a face value of Tk 1,000 and a coupon rate of 10 per cent pays a coupon of Tk 100 each year.^1

> A bond's coupon is fixed when the bond is issued, and it does not change when the market changes.

The coupon is fixed on the day that the bond is issued. If market rates later rise, the coupon stays the same. This single fact explains the main surprise of the chapter, which is that the value of a bond rises and falls with the market even though the bond's payments do not. We return to it in section 11.4.

> A bond's payments can be drawn on a timeline.

The figure below shows a five-year bond on a timeline. Read it from left to right. Year 0 is today. The short arrows are the coupons of Tk 100 at the end of years 1 to 4. At the end of year 5 there are two payments: the last coupon of Tk 100 and the face value of Tk 1,000, which the tallest arrow stands for. The box underneath says what we shall do with the timeline.

:fig The payments of a five-year bond with a face value of Tk 1,000 and a coupon rate of 10 per cent. | fig11_bond_timeline.svg

> The same bond is an annuity plus a single sum, which the reader already knows how to value.

The timeline shows a pattern that you know. The coupons are equal payments at equal gaps: that is an annuity, from Chapter 5. The face value is a single sum, paid once, from Chapter 4. So the value of a bond is the present value of an annuity plus the present value of a single sum. No new arithmetic is needed. In words and in symbols:

> The bond-value formula in words and symbols.

value of a bond = coupon × annuity factor + face value × present value factor.

In symbols, V = C × AF(r, n) + F × PVF(r, n). Here C is the yearly coupon in taka, F is the face value, r is the required return for one year, n is the number of years to maturity, AF is the present value annuity factor, and PVF is the present value factor of a single sum.

> A bond's risk depends on the issuer, and the required return reflects it.

Different bonds have different risk. A bond from a government that borrows in its own money is nearly risk-free. A bond from a young firm is not. The required return in the formula is chosen to match the bond's risk, using the ideas of Chapter 10: the risk-free return plus a premium that grows with risk. Some bonds are also backed by an asset that the lender may take if the issuer does not pay, and others are not. Chapter 13 describes these differences.

> Before moving on: a bond's face value, coupon rate and maturity fix its payments.

*Before moving on.* A bond is a loan split into tradable units. Its face value, coupon rate and maturity fix every payment that it will make. You should now be able to say: "A bond with a face value of Tk 5,000 and a coupon rate of 8 per cent pays Tk 400 each year."

## 11.3 Valuing a Bond

@! Go slowly here.
> A first example uses the smallest numbers, so the method is the only thing to learn.

We value a bond by following the same steps each time. We shall start with a very short bond so that you can see every line of arithmetic, before we use the factor tables for longer ones. The method is the idea of section 11.1: write down the payments, bring each back to today, and add.

> First example: a three-year bond whose coupon rate equals the required return.

*Worked example 1.* *Given.* A bond has a face value of Tk 1,000, a coupon rate of 10 per cent, and three years to maturity. Interest is paid at the end of each year. The required return is 10 per cent. *Find.* The value of the bond. *Formula.* V = the sum of each payment divided by (1 + r) for each year of waiting.

> Steps, answer and check for the first example.

*Steps.* The coupon is 10% × Tk 1,000 = Tk 100. Year 1: Tk 100 ÷ 1.10 = Tk 90.91. Year 2: Tk 100 ÷ 1.21 = Tk 82.64. Year 3: the coupon and the face value arrive together, Tk 1,100 ÷ 1.331 = Tk 826.45. The sum is Tk 90.91 + Tk 82.64 + Tk 826.45 = Tk 1,000.00. *Answer.* The bond is worth Tk 1,000. *Check.* When the required return equals the coupon rate, a bond should be worth its face value, because it pays exactly the return that investors want. Tk 1,000 is the face value.

> Second example: the same bond when the required return is higher.

*Worked example 2.* *Given.* The same bond, but the required return is 12 per cent. *Find.* The value. *Formula.* The same.

> Steps, answer and check for the second example.

*Steps.* Year 1: Tk 100 ÷ 1.12 = Tk 89.29. Year 2: Tk 100 ÷ 1.2544 = Tk 79.72. Year 3: Tk 1,100 ÷ 1.404928 = Tk 782.96. The sum of these rounded lines is Tk 951.97. The exact value, kept to more places, is Tk 951.96, and a difference of one paisa from rounding is normal. *Answer.* The bond is worth about Tk 952, which is less than its face value. *Check.* The required return is above the coupon rate, so the bond must be worth less than Tk 1,000. It is.

> For longer bonds, the factor method is faster than year-by-year work.

Year-by-year work becomes slow for a bond of five or ten years, so we use the factor method of Chapter 5, which needs only two multiplications. The factors for bonds are given here to six places, because a bond's face value is large and small rounding differences would show.

> The bakery's case: spare cash into a bond.

:case Case file, illustrative. In 2026 Sundial Bakery has spare cash and considers a bond with a face value of Tk 1,000, a coupon rate of 10 per cent, and five years to maturity. Interest is paid at the end of each year. The bakery's required return for a bond of this risk is 12 per cent a year, which is the illustrative discount rate carried through earlier chapters.

> Third example: the bakery's bond, with the factor method.

*Worked example 3: the bakery's bond.* *Given.* F = Tk 1,000; coupon rate 10%, so C = Tk 100; n = 5 years; r = 12%. *Find.* The value of the bond. *Formula.* V = C × AF(12%, 5) + F × PVF(12%, 5).

> Steps, answer and check for the bakery's bond.

*Steps.* From the factor tables, AF(12%, 5) = 3.604776 and PVF(12%, 5) = 0.567427. The coupons are worth Tk 100 × 3.604776 = Tk 360.4776. The face value is worth Tk 1,000 × 0.567427 = Tk 567.4270. The sum is Tk 360.4776 + Tk 567.4270 = Tk 927.9046, which we write as Tk 927.90. *Answer.* The bond is worth Tk 927.90. *Check.* Year by year, the lines are Tk 89.29, Tk 79.72, Tk 71.18, Tk 63.55 and Tk 56.74 for the coupons (sum Tk 360.48), and Tk 567.43 for the face value, which gives the same total to within a paisa. The value is below Tk 1,000, as it must be when the required return of 12 per cent is above the coupon rate of 10 per cent.

> Fourth example: half-yearly interest changes the periods, the rate and the coupon.

Many bonds pay interest twice a year. In this case three things change together. The number of periods is doubled, the rate for one period is half of the yearly rate, and each coupon is half of the yearly coupon. If the numbers in one place change and the others do not, the answer is wrong, which is the most common slip.

*Worked example 4.* *Given.* The bakery's bond, but interest is paid every six months. F = Tk 1,000; yearly coupon rate 10%, so each coupon is Tk 50; n = 5 years, which is 10 half-years; the required return is 12% a year, which is 6% for each half-year. *Find.* The value. *Formula.* V = C × AF(i, N) + F × PVF(i, N), where i is the rate for one period and N is the number of periods.

> Steps, answer and check for the half-yearly bond.

*Steps.* AF(6%, 10) = 7.360087 and PVF(6%, 10) = 0.558395. The coupons are worth Tk 50 × 7.360087 = Tk 368.0044. The face value is worth Tk 1,000 × 0.558395 = Tk 558.3950. The sum is Tk 926.3994, which we write as Tk 926.40. *Answer.* The bond is worth Tk 926.40. *Check.* The value is slightly lower than the Tk 927.90 of the yearly bond. This is right: the half-yearly bond pays its coupons earlier, which should raise its value, but a required return of 12 per cent a year is treated here as 6 per cent for each half-year, which is a little less than 12 per cent compounded yearly, and so the discounting is stronger. The two answers are close, which is what we expect.^2

The coupon and the face value of the bond do not change, but its value does change when the required return changes. The table below shows the bakery's bond at four required returns. Read each row across: the required return, the factors, the value, and a word for the bond's status, which section 11.4 explains.

The figure below draws these values and the values between them. > Slips with bond values: mixing periods, forgetting the face value, and using the wrong factor.

*Common slips.* A frequent slip is to discount the coupons and forget the face value, which gives a value far too low. A second is to use the yearly rate with half-yearly periods, as in section 11.3 above. A third is to use the annuity factor for the face value, or the single-sum factor for the coupons. The coupons are an annuity, and the face value is a single sum. Always draw the timeline before you use the factors.

> Before moving on: the value of a bond is its coupons as an annuity plus its face value as a single sum.

*Before moving on.* The value of a bond is the present value of its coupons, found with the annuity factor, plus the present value of its face value, found with the single-sum factor. For half-yearly interest, double the periods and halve both the rate and the coupon. You should now be able to say: "A bond that pays Tk 80 a year and Tk 1,000 at the end is worth less than Tk 1,000 if the required return is above 8 per cent."

## 11.4 Discount, Premium and Par; Yield to Maturity

> A puzzle: how can a bond be sold for less than its face value and still be a fair deal?

Suppose the bakery buys its bond for Tk 927.90, which is less than the Tk 1,000 face value. A student might think that the bakery was cheated, or that the seller made a gift. Neither is true. The price is fair, because the bond pays a coupon of 10 per cent while the bakery requires 12 per cent. The lower price makes up the difference. The bakery will receive Tk 1,000 at the end though it paid only Tk 927.90, and that extra gain lifts its return from 10 per cent to 12 per cent.

> A bond sells at a discount, a premium or par, according to how its coupon rate compares with the required return.

@ Selling at a discount | Selling for less than the face value, which happens when the coupon rate is below the required return.
@ Selling at a premium | Selling for more than the face value, which happens when the coupon rate is above the required return.

The rule has three cases. A bond is **selling at a discount** when its value is less than its face value, and this happens when the coupon rate is below the required return. A bond is **selling at a premium** when its value is more than its face value, and this happens when the coupon rate is above the required return. A bond is *selling at par* when its value equals its face value, and this happens when the two rates are equal. The table below sets out the three cases, with the bakery's bond as the example.

:table Bond status and the required return (bakery's bond, coupon rate 10%)
| If the required return is... | Compared with the coupon rate | The bond sells at... | Value (Tk) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12% | Above | A discount | 927.90 |
| 10% | Equal | Par | 1,000.00 |
| 8% | Below | A premium | 1,079.85 |

> The reason is that the coupon is fixed, while the required return moves with the market.

Why does the status change? The coupon is fixed when the bond is issued. The required return is not fixed, because it follows the market. Suppose market rates rise, so that new bonds of the same risk pay 12 per cent. Nobody will pay Tk 1,000 for a bond that pays only 10 per cent when a new one pays 12 per cent. The price of the old bond must fall until a buyer earns 12 per cent on the lower price. If market rates fall to 8 per cent, the opposite happens. The old bond's coupon of 10 per cent is attractive, and the price rises.

> A bond's value moves in the opposite direction to the required return.

This reasoning gives the most important rule about bonds: when required returns rise, bond values fall, and when required returns fall, bond values rise. The figure of the previous section showed it. A student who has learned the rule can say, on hearing that market interest rates have gone up, that holders of existing fixed-coupon bonds have lost value on paper.

> Time also matters: a bond's value moves toward its face value as maturity nears.

Time to maturity changes the value as well. Keep the required return at 12 per cent and the coupon rate at 10 per cent, and vary the years left. The bond is worth Tk 982.14 with one year left, Tk 966.20 with two, Tk 951.96 with three, Tk 927.90 with five, and Tk 887.00 with ten. The shorter the time, the closer the value is to Tk 1,000. On the day of maturity, the bond pays exactly its face value, and the discount has disappeared.

:fig How the value of a 10 per cent bond moves toward Tk 1,000 as maturity nears, when the required return stays at 12 per cent. | fig11_pull_to_par.svg

> The figure shows the bars rising toward the face value as the years left fall.

The figure above shows the five values as bars. Read it from the right: with ten years left the bar is lowest, and each step to the left brings the bar closer to the dashed face-value line. The discount of the bond is a gain that arrives, little by little, as the years pass.

> Yield to maturity is the yearly return from buying at today's price and holding to maturity.

@ Yield to maturity | The yearly return a buyer earns by paying today's price and holding the bond until it is repaid.

A buyer often knows the price and wants the return. The **yield to maturity** means the yearly return that a buyer earns by paying today's price and holding the bond until it is repaid. In the bakery's bond, the price of Tk 927.90 gives a yield to maturity of exactly 12 per cent, because 12 per cent is the discount rate that makes the present value of the payments equal to the price. Yield to maturity is therefore the required return seen from the other side: given the value, we find the rate; before, given the rate, we found the value.

> Finding a yield needs trial and error, as a worked example shows.

*Worked example 5.* *Given.* The bakery's bond (face value Tk 1,000; coupon rate 10%; five years) is offered at Tk 900. *Find.* The yield to maturity, to the nearest tenth of a per cent. *Method.* Try rates until the value of the bond is close to Tk 900.

> Steps, answer and check for the yield.

*Steps.* At 12% the value is Tk 927.90, which is above Tk 900, so the yield is above 12%. At 13%, AF = 3.517231 and PVF = 0.542760, so the value is Tk 351.72 + Tk 542.76 = Tk 894.48, which is below Tk 900, so the yield is below 13%. The price of Tk 900 lies between Tk 927.90 and Tk 894.48. The fall from 12% to 13% is Tk 33.42, and the price needs a fall of Tk 27.90 from the value at 12%. That is 27.90 ÷ 33.42 = 0.83 of the way, so the yield is about 12.8%. *Answer.* The yield to maturity is about 12.8 per cent. *Check.* At 12.8% the value is Tk 901.04, and at 12.9% it is Tk 897.75, so Tk 900 lies between them. The yield is higher than the coupon rate of 10 per cent, which it must be for a bond selling at a discount.

> Yield to maturity assumes the issuer pays in full and that coupons can be invested at the same rate.

*Limits.* The yield to maturity assumes that the issuer pays every coupon and the face value in full, and on time. A bond whose issuer may not pay should be valued with a higher required return. The yield also assumes that the buyer holds to maturity. A buyer who sells early earns a return that depends on the price at the time of sale. The word yield is also used for other measures in some books; in this book it always means yield to maturity.

> Before moving on: a bond sells at a discount if its coupon rate is below the required return, and its yield is the rate that makes value equal to price.

*Before moving on.* A bond sells at a discount when the coupon rate is below the required return, at a premium when it is above, and at par when the two are equal. The value falls when the required return rises, and it moves toward the face value as maturity nears. You should now be able to say: "A bond with a 9 per cent coupon sells at a discount when buyers require 11 per cent."

## 11.5 Valuing Shares

@! Go slowly here.
> A share gives the owner a claim on the firm, and its payments are dividends, not coupons.

Chapter 2 introduced the shareholder, who owns a part of a company, and the dividend, which is a part of profit paid out to owners. We now value a share in the same way that we valued a bond: by the present value of what it will pay. The share pays dividends. But there are three differences from a bond. A dividend is not fixed in advance, and the firm may raise it, cut it, or skip it. A share has no maturity, because the firm may continue for many years. And a share has no face value to be repaid, because the owner may only sell it to another buyer.

> The ordinary kind of share has a word in use: common stock.

@ Common stock | The ordinary ownership shares of a company. Word in use: in daily life, stock means goods kept in a shop.

The ordinary kind of share is called **common stock**. It means the ordinary ownership shares of a company, and it carries the right to dividends after others are paid, and the right to vote. In this chapter we need only the dividends. Chapter 14 describes the rights of the owner in full, together with preferred shares, which are a different kind.

> First case: a share that pays the same dividend every year for ever.

Begin with the simplest case. A firm is expected to pay the same dividend, year after year, for ever. The share is then a perpetuity, which Chapter 5 described briefly: an annuity that never ends. The present value of such a stream is found by a simple rule. In words, and in symbols:

> The formula for a share with a constant dividend.

value of a share = yearly dividend ÷ required return.

In symbols, V = D ÷ r, where D is the dividend each year and r is the required return, written as a decimal.

> Worked example 6: a constant dividend of Tk 6 and a required return of 12 per cent.

*Worked example 6.* *Given.* A share pays a dividend of Tk 6 at the end of each year for ever. The required return is 12 per cent. *Find.* The value of the share. *Formula.* V = D ÷ r.

> Steps, answer and check for the constant-dividend share.

*Steps.* V = Tk 6 ÷ 0.12 = Tk 50. *Answer.* The share is worth Tk 50. *Check.* A buyer who pays Tk 50 and receives Tk 6 every year earns 6 ÷ 50 = 12 per cent a year, which is the required return.

> A different check: a buyer who holds the share for one year only.

Another way to see the answer is to suppose that a buyer holds the share for one year and then sells it. She receives the dividend of Tk 6 and the selling price of Tk 56, and she wants 12 per cent. The value today is (Tk 6 + Tk 56) ÷ 1.12 = Tk 55.36. Here the selling price of Tk 56 was assumed and was not found. If the buyer in turn expected to sell for the value found by the rule, the one-year view would give back Tk 50. Every holder, in the end, is paid by the dividends that follow.

> Dividends often grow, and growth raises the value of a share.

Most firms hope to raise their dividends as they grow. The bakery's profit, for example, may rise as it opens more shops. A share whose dividend grows is worth more than one with the same first dividend that does not grow. The simplest case is a dividend that grows at the same per cent each year for ever. This case is called **dividend growth**: the dividend of each year is the last year's dividend times (1 + g), where g is the yearly growth rate.

> The growing-dividend formula is the constant-dividend formula with growth taken away from the required return.

The value is found by a rule that is a small change in the one before. In words: the value of a share is the dividend expected next year, divided by the required return less the growth rate. In symbols, V = D1 ÷ (r − g), where D1 is the dividend expected at the end of the coming year, r is the required return, and g is the yearly growth rate of the dividend. The rule needs r to be larger than g. If g were as large as r or larger, the formula would give a meaningless or negative answer, and a growth rate that high could not continue for ever.

> Worked example 7: a dividend of Tk 6 growing at 4 per cent a year.

*Worked example 7.* *Given.* The first dividend, due at the end of the coming year, is D1 = Tk 6. It then grows by 4 per cent each year. The required return is 12 per cent. *Find.* The value of the share. *Formula.* V = D1 ÷ (r − g).

> Steps, answer and check for the growing share.

*Steps.* r − g = 0.12 − 0.04 = 0.08. V = Tk 6 ÷ 0.08 = Tk 75. *Answer.* The share is worth Tk 75. *Check.* Without growth, the same first dividend gives Tk 50. Growth raises the value to Tk 75, which is more, as it should be. The dividends in the first years would be Tk 6.00, Tk 6.24, Tk 6.49, and so on.

> Worked example 8: starting from the dividend just paid, which must be grown by one year.

*Worked example 8.* *Given.* A firm has just paid a dividend of Tk 5 (call it D0). Dividends are expected to grow at 6 per cent each year. The required return is 14 per cent. *Find.* The value of the share. *Formula.* First find D1 = D0 × (1 + g). Then V = D1 ÷ (r − g).

> Steps, answer and check for the second growing share.

*Steps.* D1 = Tk 5 × 1.06 = Tk 5.30. r − g = 0.14 − 0.06 = 0.08. V = Tk 5.30 ÷ 0.08 = Tk 66.25. *Answer.* The share is worth Tk 66.25. *Check.* A buyer pays Tk 66.25 and expects Tk 5.30 as the first dividend, which is a yield of 8 per cent, and growth of 6 per cent supplies the rest of the 14 per cent. 8% + 6% = 14%.

> The table shows how sensitive the value is to the growth rate.

The table below uses the first dividend of Tk 6 and the required return of 12 per cent, and changes only the growth rate. Read each row across: the growth rate, the gap between the required return and the growth, and the value. Notice how the value rises faster and faster as the growth rate approaches the required return.

:table Value of a share with a first dividend of Tk 6 and a required return of 12 per cent, at different growth rates
| Growth rate | r − g | Value (Tk) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0% | 0.12 | 50.00 |
| 2% | 0.10 | 60.00 |
| 4% | 0.08 | 75.00 |
| 6% | 0.06 | 100.00 |
| 8% | 0.04 | 150.00 |

> A share with a dividend that is fixed for some years and then grows is valued in two parts.

Some firms are known to pay a fixed dividend for a few years, and then to grow. The bakery's shop network, for example, may take two years to open before dividends start to rise. The share is then valued in two steps: first find what the share will be worth at the end of the fixed years, and then bring everything back to today. This is a more advanced idea, and a beginner may return to it after the rest of the chapter has been mastered.

> Worked example 9: a fixed dividend for two years, then growth.

*Worked example 9.* *Given.* The dividend is Tk 6 at the end of year 1 and Tk 6 at the end of year 2. From year 3 it grows at 4 per cent a year for ever, so D3 = Tk 6 × 1.04 = Tk 6.24. The required return is 12 per cent. *Find.* The value of the share today. *Formula.* The value at the end of year 2 is D3 ÷ (r − g). Then the value today is the present value of the two dividends and of that end-of-year-2 value.

> Steps, answer and check for the two-stage share.

*Steps.* The value at the end of year 2 is Tk 6.24 ÷ 0.08 = Tk 78. Now bring everything back. Dividend 1: Tk 6 ÷ 1.12 = Tk 5.36. Dividend 2: Tk 6 ÷ 1.2544 = Tk 4.78. The value of Tk 78 at the end of year 2: Tk 78 ÷ 1.2544 = Tk 62.18. The sum is Tk 5.36 + Tk 4.78 + Tk 62.18 = Tk 72.32. *Answer.* The share is worth Tk 72.32 today. *Check.* The answer lies between Tk 50 (no growth ever) and Tk 75 (growth from the first year), which is where we expect it, because growth begins late.

> The limits of share valuation are the estimates of dividends and the required return.

*Limits.* Share valuation is far less certain than bond valuation. A bond's payments are fixed in a contract, but a share's dividends are only estimates. A small change in the growth rate changes the value a great deal, as the table showed. The required return is also an estimate, and Chapter 10 showed how it is found. The result is a guide to the value and not a fact. A firm that pays no dividend today can still have a value, because investors expect dividends in the future, or a sale of the firm; the rule is then used with more care.

> Slips with share values: using the wrong dividend, and letting growth equal the required return.

*Common slips.* The most frequent slip is to use the dividend just paid in place of the one expected next year. The formula needs D1, the dividend of the coming year. If you are given D0, you must multiply by (1 + g) first. Another slip is to enter the required return as a whole number (12) rather than a decimal (0.12), which gives an answer a hundred times too small.

> Before moving on: a share is worth its expected dividends brought to today; growth raises the value.

*Before moving on.* A share is valued as the present value of its dividends. With a constant dividend the value is D ÷ r. With a dividend growing at g each year, the value is D1 ÷ (r − g). You should now be able to say: "A share with a first dividend of Tk 10 and growth of 5 per cent is worth Tk 100 when the required return is 15 per cent."

## 11.6 Bonds and Shares Compared

> A bondholder is a lender, a shareholder is an owner, and this difference runs through everything else.

We now set the two securities beside each other. The table below does this. Read each row across: the question, the answer for a bond, and the answer for a share. Every row follows from the one difference at the top: the bondholder lent money and the shareholder owns a part of the firm.

:table Bonds and shares compared
| Question | Bond | Share (common stock) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does the holder have? | A loan to the firm | A part of the firm |
| What does it pay? | A fixed coupon, and the face value at maturity | A dividend, which may rise, fall or stop |
| Is there a maturity? | Yes | No |
| Who is paid first? | The bondholder, before owners | The shareholder, after lenders |
| Is there a vote? | No | Yes |
| What is the risk to the holder? | Lower; payments are fixed by contract | Higher; dividends and price are uncertain |
| How is its value found? | Coupons as an annuity plus face value as a single sum | Dividends, with or without growth, brought to today |
| What return is required? | Lower | Higher |

> The required return is lower for a bond than for a share, because its risk to the holder is lower.

The last row connects to Chapter 10. Because bondholders are paid first, and their payments are fixed, a bond is usually less risky than a share of the same firm. A lower risk means a lower premium, and so a lower required return. A share must offer a higher expected return, or no investor would hold it. The firm, in turn, pays more for the owners' money than for the lenders' money, and Chapter 15 returns to this fact when it prices the firm's funds.

> The two securities differ in what the firm must do.

The two securities also differ from the firm's side. A firm must pay a bond's coupons and its face value, even in a bad year, or the lenders can take action against it. It need not pay dividends in a bad year. A firm that borrows by selling bonds therefore takes on a fixed payment, which is the financial risk of Chapter 9. A firm that raises money by selling shares takes on no fixed payment, but it shares its profits and its control with a wider group of owners.

> The valuation method is the same for both, and only the payments differ.

The valuation method is the same for both securities: list the payments, bring each back to today at the required return, and add. For the bond the payments are known, and the difficulty is the rate. For the share the rate is also an estimate, and so are the payments. A student who has understood this has understood the whole chapter. Chapters 13 and 14 describe how firms raise long-term money from lenders and from owners, and Chapter 15 puts a single price on all of it.

> Before moving on: a bond and a share are valued in the same way, but the bond has fixed payments and lower risk.

*Before moving on.* Bondholders are lenders, with fixed payments and priority; shareholders are owners, with uncertain dividends and more risk. Both are valued by present value at the required return. You should now be able to say: "A share needs a higher expected return than a bond of the same firm because it carries more risk."

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas in a few lines.

The intrinsic value of a security is the present value of all its expected payments, found with the required return. A bond has a face value, a coupon rate and a maturity. Its value is the coupons, as an annuity, plus the face value, as a single sum: V = C × AF + F × PVF. For half-yearly interest, double the periods and halve the rate and the coupon. A bond sells at a discount when its coupon rate is below the required return, at a premium when it is above, and at par when they are equal. Its value falls when the required return rises, and moves toward the face value as maturity nears. The yield to maturity is the rate that makes the value equal to the price. A share with a constant dividend is worth D ÷ r, and a share with a dividend growing at g is worth D1 ÷ (r − g). The bakery's bond, with a coupon of 10 per cent and a required return of 12 per cent, is worth Tk 927.90, and it sells at a discount.

## Think It Through

> A problem: pricing a bond at two required returns, and judging an offer.

The bakery is offered a bond with a face value of Tk 1,000, a coupon rate of 9 per cent, and four years to maturity. Interest is paid at the end of each year. First, find the value of the bond if the required return is 12 per cent. Then find its value if the required return falls to 9 per cent. State in each case whether the bond sells at a discount, a premium or par. Finally, the bond is offered at Tk 950. Should the bakery buy it, if the required return is 12 per cent? Give a reason in two sentences. (Hint: at 12%, AF(12%, 4) = 3.037349 and PVF(12%, 4) = 0.635518, so the value is Tk 90 × 3.037349 + Tk 1,000 × 0.635518 = Tk 273.36 + Tk 635.52 = Tk 908.88, a discount. At 9% the bond is worth Tk 1,000, at par. At Tk 950 the price is above the value of Tk 908.88, so the bond is dear and should not be bought.)

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five common misunderstandings and their corrections.

*"A bond's price is always its face value."* The face value is repaid at the end. The value in between changes with the required return.

> The coupon rate and the required return are different ideas.

*"The coupon rate is the return the buyer earns."* It is only the rate of interest on the face value. The buyer's return depends on the price paid, and the yield to maturity measures it.

> A discount bond is not a bad bond.

*"A bond selling at a discount is a poor bond."* It sells at a discount because its coupon rate is below the required return. The price is fair for that rate.

> A dividend is not the same as a profit.

*"A share is worth its profit."* A share is worth the dividends it will pay, brought to today. A firm may keep part of its profit, as Chapter 14 shows, and growth then comes from the profit that is kept.

> The growth formula has a limit.

*"The share formula works for any growth rate."* It needs the growth rate to be less than the required return. A growth rate equal to or above the required return cannot last for ever.

## In History

> The first public offer of shares to many ordinary buyers.

In 1602 the Dutch East India Company, a trading firm of the Netherlands, was formed to send ships to Asia. A single voyage was costly and dangerous, and a few rich merchants could not carry the risk alone. The company's charter allowed members of the public to buy shares, and more than a thousand investors did so in the first offering.^3 Because buyers wished to sell when they needed cash, a market for the shares grew in Amsterdam, and a dedicated exchange building was later built there. The company's shares did not repay a fixed sum or pay a fixed coupon. Their value rested on the dividends that the voyages were expected to bring, which is the idea of this chapter.
