:chapter IX | Leverage and Risk
:mood Why does a small push at one end sometimes move the other end a great deal?
:desc This chapter shows how fixed costs, and fixed payments to lenders, magnify the swings in a firm's profit. It teaches how to find the sales at which a firm earns no profit, and how to measure the magnifying effect in three ways. It ends by asking how much of this effect a careful firm should accept.

> Two shops sell the same goods; both sales rise by one-tenth, but only one profit leaps.

Two small shops stand side by side in the same market. Both sell tea and biscuits. Last month, the sales of each shop rose by one-tenth. The owner of the first shop found that her profit rose by one-tenth as well. The owner of the second shop found that his profit rose by almost one half. Nobody worked harder. No price changed. Why did the second shop gain so much more?

> The answer lies in how each shop pays its costs: by the cup or by the month.

The answer lies in the way each shop pays for its work. The first shop pays for almost everything by the amount it sells. Its helper is paid for each cup served, and its landlord takes a share of sales. When sales rise, its costs rise in step, and so does its profit. The second shop pays a fixed rent and a fixed monthly wage, whatever it sells. When sales rise, those costs stay where they are. Almost every extra taka of sales becomes an extra taka of profit.

> The same fixed payments that help in a good month hurt in a bad month.

The second shop has a hard side, too. Suppose sales fall by one-tenth. The first shop's profit falls in step. The second shop's profit falls by almost one half, because the rent and the wages must still be paid in full. A fixed payment behaves like a long plank resting on a stone. A small push at one end moves the other end a great deal, in either direction. Finance takes a word from this picture: the word is *lever*.

> This chapter measures that effect, so that a firm can decide how much of it to accept.

In this chapter we study two kinds of fixed payment. The first kind is the fixed cost of running the business. The second kind is the fixed payment to people who lent money. We study each one alone, and then both together. By the end you will be able to find the sales at which a firm earns no profit. You will also be able to say, in numbers, how many times a change in sales is magnified in profit.

> Before this chapter, you should know fixed and variable cost, EBIT and the profit layout, and the idea of risk.

*You will need:* from Chapter 3, the difference between fixed cost and variable cost, and the profit layout from sales down to net profit (including EBIT, which you have met and which means profit before interest and tax); from Chapter 8, the idea of risk as the chance that results differ from hopes, and the coefficient of variation.

## 9.1 Business Risk and Financial Risk

> A cart seller faces two uncertainties: how many people buy, and what she must repay.

Begin with a seller of snacks who has a cart near a bus station. On some days many people buy, and on other days few do. She cannot know in advance. Now suppose she borrowed Tk 20,000 to buy the cart, and she must repay Tk 1,000 every week, whatever she sells. She has two separate worries. The first is about her customers. The second is about her debt. Firms have the same two worries, and finance gives each one a name.

> Business risk is the risk that comes from the firm's own work.

@ Business risk | The risk that a firm's EBIT differs from what was hoped, because of its own work.
@ Leverage | In daily life, the extra force a lever gives. In finance, the magnifying effect of fixed payments on profit.

The first name is **business risk**. Business risk means the chance that a firm's EBIT will be different from what was hoped, because of the firm's own work. It is also called operating risk, but this book keeps the name business risk. The word **leverage** needs a note here. In daily life it means the extra force that a lever gives. In finance it means the magnifying effect that fixed payments have on profit, and we study it from section 9.3 onward.^1

> Business risk has many causes; the share of fixed cost is one that the firm controls.

What causes business risk? Look at the bakery. The price of flour may rise. Fewer customers may come in the rainy season. A new rival may open in the next street. The electricity may fail for a day. Each of these changes sales or cost, and so each changes EBIT. One more cause matters greatly for this chapter: the share of the firm's costs that are fixed. The more cost is fixed, the more a change in sales is magnified in EBIT. A firm that sells a similar product in a similar market can still have more business risk than its neighbour, only because it has more fixed cost.

> Financial risk is the extra risk that comes from borrowing.

@ Financial risk | The extra risk owners carry because the firm has promised fixed payments to lenders.

The second name is **financial risk**. Financial risk means the extra risk that the owners carry because the firm has promised fixed payments to lenders. The bakery shows how. It owes its bank interest of Tk 7,000 every month. If sales are poor and EBIT is small, the bank still expects Tk 7,000. The owners receive only what is left after the bank is paid. A firm with no loans has no financial risk of this kind.

> The two risks sit in different places in the profit layout.

Chapter 3 gave the profit layout, which starts at sales and ends at net profit. Business risk acts in the upper half of that layout, between sales and EBIT. Financial risk acts in the lower half, between EBIT and net profit, where interest and similar payments are taken away. A figure in section 9.5 draws this idea in one picture.

> A table sets the two risks side by side.

The table below sets the two risks side by side. Read each row from left to right: the first column names the question, and the next two columns give the answer for each risk.

:table Business risk and financial risk compared
| Question | Business risk | Financial risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where does it come from? | The firm's own work: customers, costs, rivals | Fixed payments promised to lenders |
| Where in the profit layout? | Between sales and EBIT | Between EBIT and net profit |
| Can the firm choose it? | Only in part; much belongs to the kind of work | Yes; the firm chooses how much to borrow |
| Who bears it? | Everyone who has money in the firm | Mostly the owners, after lenders are paid |

> The owner, the lender and the manager each see the two risks from a different seat.

The two risks look different from different seats. The owner sees both, because the owner is paid last. The lender cares most about financial risk, because the lender wants to know that the fixed payment will be met. The manager must live with business risk and must also decide how much financial risk to add.

> The two risks combine, so a firm with high business risk should think twice before borrowing heavily.

The two risks are not separate boxes. They combine, and one can make the other worse. A firm with an uncertain EBIT that also owes a large fixed payment may fail in a bad year. For this reason, firms with high business risk usually borrow less. Business risk can be reduced a little, for example by selling several products, but most of it comes with the kind of work. Financial risk is a choice.

> Before moving on: business risk is about EBIT; financial risk is about borrowing.

*Before moving on.* Business risk comes from the work of the firm and shows in EBIT. Financial risk comes from fixed payments to lenders and shows below EBIT. The firm can choose its financial risk more freely than its business risk.

## 9.2 The Break-Even Point

> A first question: how much must we sell before we earn any profit?

How many cups must a tea seller sell before the first taka of profit? Every seller asks this question before the first day of trade, because the rent and the pot are already paid for. The answer is a number, and finding it is the first technique of this chapter.

> The break-even point is the sales level at which profit is exactly zero.

@ Break-even point | The level of sales at which profit is exactly zero.

The **break-even point** means the level of sales at which profit is exactly zero. Below it, the firm makes a loss. Above it, the firm makes a profit. In this chapter, "profit" at the break-even point means EBIT, and "fixed cost" means the fixed cost of running the business. Interest is a separate payment, which we study in section 9.4.

> Every unit sold brings a contribution; the break-even point is where contributions cover the fixed cost.

Chapter 3 defined contribution as the selling price less the variable cost for each unit. Each unit sold adds its contribution towards paying the fixed cost. At first the contributions are used to cover the fixed cost. When they have covered it all, every further unit adds to profit. The break-even point is the number of units at which the contributions exactly equal the fixed cost. In words:

> The formula in words, then in symbols.

break-even units = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit.

> The symbols used in the formula.

In symbols, let F be the fixed cost, P the selling price of one unit, and V the variable cost of one unit. Then the break-even units are Q = F ÷ (P − V). We use Q for a number of units.

> First example: a tea stall, with small and clean numbers.

*Worked example 1: a tea stall.* *Given.* A tea stall charges Tk 10 for a cup. The tea, milk and sugar in one cup cost Tk 6. The stall pays Tk 2,000 each day for rent and the helper, whatever it sells. *Find.* The number of cups the stall must sell each day to break even. *Formula.* Break-even units = fixed cost ÷ (price − variable cost).

> Steps, answer and check for the tea stall.

*Steps.* The contribution per cup is Tk 10 − Tk 6 = Tk 4. The break-even units are Tk 2,000 ÷ Tk 4 = 500 cups. *Answer.* The stall must sell 500 cups a day to break even. *Check.* At 500 cups the stall earns 500 × Tk 4 = Tk 2,000 of contribution, which exactly equals the fixed cost, so profit is zero. At 501 cups, profit is Tk 4.

> Now the bakery, whose figures we carry through the chapter.

:case Case file, illustrative. Sundial Bakery bought its second oven with a further bank loan of Tk 3,00,000 at 12% a year. Its total debt is now Tk 7,00,000, and its interest is Tk 7,000 a month. A box of buns sells for Tk 40, and the variable cost of one box is Tk 24. The fixed cost of running the bakery is Tk 1,20,000 a month. The bakery plans to sell 10,000 boxes a month.

> Second example: the bakery's break-even point, with a check on both sides.

*Worked example 2: the bakery.* *Given.* P = Tk 40; V = Tk 24; F = Tk 1,20,000 a month. *Find.* The break-even boxes each month, and the sales in taka at that level. *Formula.* Q = F ÷ (P − V).

> Steps, answer and check for the bakery.

*Steps.* The contribution of one box is Tk 40 − Tk 24 = Tk 16. The break-even boxes are Tk 1,20,000 ÷ Tk 16 = 7,500 boxes. The sales at that level are 7,500 × Tk 40 = Tk 3,00,000. *Answer.* The bakery breaks even at 7,500 boxes a month, which is Tk 3,00,000 of sales. *Check.* At 7,000 boxes, contribution is 7,000 × Tk 16 = Tk 1,12,000, which is Tk 8,000 less than the fixed cost, so EBIT is a loss of Tk 8,000. At 8,000 boxes, contribution is Tk 1,28,000, so EBIT is a profit of Tk 8,000. Zero lies between them, at 7,500.

> Break-even sales can also be found from the share of each taka of sales that is contribution.

There is a second way to state the answer in taka. Each box sells for Tk 40 and contributes Tk 16, so 40 per cent of every taka of sales is contribution. The fixed cost divided by that share gives the break-even sales: Tk 1,20,000 ÷ 0.40 = Tk 3,00,000. The two ways agree, which is a useful check.

> The chart draws the break-even point as the place where two lines cross.

The chart below shows the same answer as a picture. To read it, look at the foot of the picture first: it shows the boxes sold in a month. The upright side shows money in taka. The rising line from the corner is sales. The dashed line is the fixed cost, which does not move. The second rising line is total cost, which is the fixed cost plus the variable cost of the boxes sold. Where sales meet total cost, profit is zero. To the left of that point the total cost line is above the sales line, which means loss. To the right the sales line is above, which means profit.

:fig Break-even chart for the bakery: sales and total cost cross at 7,500 boxes. | fig9_break_even.svg

> The same idea finds the sales needed for a target profit.

The same idea answers a second question. How many boxes must the bakery sell to earn a chosen EBIT? Each box contributes Tk 16. The contributions must first cover the fixed cost of Tk 1,20,000, and then supply the target. In words: units needed = (fixed cost + target EBIT) ÷ contribution per unit.

> Worked example for a target EBIT of Tk 56,000.

*Worked example.* *Given.* F = Tk 1,20,000; contribution per box = Tk 16; target EBIT = Tk 56,000 a month. *Find.* The boxes needed. *Steps.* Fixed cost plus target is Tk 1,20,000 + Tk 56,000 = Tk 1,76,000. Divide by Tk 16: 1,76,000 ÷ 16 = 11,000 boxes. *Answer.* The bakery must sell 11,000 boxes a month. *Check.* At 11,000 boxes, contribution is 11,000 × Tk 16 = Tk 1,76,000. Less the fixed cost of Tk 1,20,000, EBIT is Tk 56,000, which is the target.

> Break-even results depend on the assumptions behind them.

The break-even point rests on assumptions, and a careful reader should know them. The price and the variable cost of a unit are taken as the same at every level of output. The fixed cost is taken as fixed over the range of sales studied, but a firm that grows far enough must buy another oven, and its fixed cost then jumps. The method as shown suits one product; a firm with many products needs an average contribution. These limits do not make the method wrong. They tell us to use it for planning within a sensible range.

> Three common slips: price instead of contribution, wrong period, and interest inside the fixed cost.

*Common slips.* Some students divide the fixed cost by the price instead of by the contribution; this gives a break-even point that is far too low. Others mix periods, using a yearly fixed cost with a monthly sales figure. A third slip is to include interest in the fixed cost and then to count it again in section 9.4. In this chapter, fixed cost means fixed cost of running the business only.

> Before moving on: break-even units equal fixed cost divided by contribution per unit.

*Before moving on.* The break-even point is where contributions exactly cover the fixed cost. You find it by dividing the fixed cost by the contribution of one unit. You should now be able to say: "If the fixed cost is Tk 60,000 and a unit contributes Tk 12, the firm must sell 5,000 units to break even."

## 9.3 Operating Leverage

@! Go slowly here.
> Two ways of paying a worker show why fixed costs magnify results.

A tailor needs one more worker. She can pay the worker Tk 20 for each shirt sewn, or Tk 6,000 a month whatever is sewn. With the first plan, a month with twice as many shirts costs twice as much in wages. With the second plan, a month with twice as many shirts costs the same wages. The second plan leaves her more profit in a busy month, and less in a quiet month. The more of her costs are fixed, the more her profit swings. We now measure that swing.

> Operating leverage is the magnifying effect of fixed operating costs on EBIT.

@ Operating leverage | How fixed costs of running the business magnify changes in EBIT.

**Operating leverage** means the way that fixed costs of running the business magnify the effect of a change in sales on EBIT. A firm with large fixed costs and small variable costs has high operating leverage. A firm with no fixed cost has none. We begin with a table, because the table shows the effect before any formula does.

> The table gives the bakery's EBIT at three levels of sales.

The table below shows the bakery's monthly results at three levels of sales. The middle row is the plan of 10,000 boxes. The first row is a fall of one-quarter, and the last row is a rise of one-quarter. Read each row across: sales, less variable cost, equals contribution, less the fixed cost, equals EBIT.

:table The bakery's monthly EBIT at three levels of sales (Tk)
| Boxes sold | Sales | Variable cost | Contribution | Fixed cost | EBIT |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7,500 | 3,00,000 | 1,80,000 | 1,20,000 | 1,20,000 | 0 |
| 10,000 | 4,00,000 | 2,40,000 | 1,60,000 | 1,20,000 | 40,000 |
| 12,500 | 5,00,000 | 3,00,000 | 2,00,000 | 1,20,000 | 80,000 |

> A rise of one-quarter in sales doubles EBIT; a fall of one-quarter removes it.

Now compare the rows. From the middle row to the last row, sales rise from Tk 4,00,000 to Tk 5,00,000, which is a rise of 25 per cent. EBIT rises from Tk 40,000 to Tk 80,000, which is a rise of 100 per cent. From the middle row to the first row, sales fall by 25 per cent, and EBIT falls from Tk 40,000 to zero, which is a fall of 100 per cent. A change of 25 per cent in sales became a change of 100 per cent in EBIT. The fixed cost of Tk 1,20,000 did not move, and so it magnified the change.

> The degree of operating leverage tells by how many times EBIT changes when sales change.

@ Degree of operating leverage (DOL) | The per cent change in EBIT caused by a one per cent change in sales.

We give this magnifying factor a name. The **degree of operating leverage (DOL)** means the per cent change in EBIT for each one per cent change in sales. In words and then in symbols:

> The formula for DOL, in two equal forms.

DOL = per cent change in EBIT ÷ per cent change in sales.

> For the bakery, DOL = 100 ÷ 25 = 4.0, and a second form needs only one level of sales.

For the bakery, DOL = 100 ÷ 25 = 4.0. There is a second form, which needs only one level of sales. DOL = contribution ÷ EBIT. Contribution is the total contribution of all units sold, and EBIT is measured at the same level of sales. Both forms give the same answer.

> Worked example 3: DOL for the bakery at 10,000 boxes.

*Worked example 3.* *Given.* At 10,000 boxes the bakery has a contribution of Tk 1,60,000 and an EBIT of Tk 40,000. *Find.* The DOL. *Formula.* DOL = contribution ÷ EBIT.

> Steps, answer and check for DOL.

*Steps.* DOL = Tk 1,60,000 ÷ Tk 40,000 = 4.0. *Answer.* The DOL at 10,000 boxes is 4.0: each one per cent change in sales changes EBIT by four per cent. *Check.* A rise of 25 per cent in sales should raise EBIT by 4.0 × 25 = 100 per cent, which is what the table of EBIT at three levels of sales shows. A second check: 10,000 boxes are 2,500 boxes above break-even, which is one-quarter of 10,000; and 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4.0.

> The DOL is different at each level of sales; it is largest near break-even.

The DOL is not one fixed number for a firm. Use the same formula at 12,500 boxes: contribution is Tk 2,00,000 and EBIT is Tk 80,000, so DOL = 2.5. The further sales rise above break-even, the smaller the DOL becomes. Near break-even, EBIT is small and a small change in sales is a large change in EBIT, so the DOL is very large. At exactly 7,500 boxes EBIT is zero, and the DOL cannot be found at all.

> A firm with no fixed cost has a DOL of 1, so profit moves in step with sales.

Now test the idea at its lowest limit. Suppose a firm sells 10,000 units at Tk 40, with a variable cost of Tk 28 and no fixed cost. Its contribution is Tk 1,20,000 and its EBIT is also Tk 1,20,000. DOL = 1,20,000 ÷ 1,20,000 = 1.0. Its EBIT changes by exactly the same per cent as its sales. A DOL of 1.0 is the least that any firm can have, and it means no magnifying at all.

> An exercise compares two ways of organising costs that give the same EBIT at the planned sales.

Operating leverage is partly a choice, as an exercise shows. Suppose the bakery could instead buy its buns from a contract baker. It would then have a fixed cost of only Tk 40,000 a month, but a variable cost of Tk 32 for each box. At 10,000 boxes the contribution is 10,000 × (Tk 40 − Tk 32) = Tk 80,000, and EBIT is Tk 80,000 − Tk 40,000 = Tk 40,000. This is the same EBIT as the bakery's own plan. The table below compares the two plans at three levels of sales.

:table Two ways of organising costs: the bakery's own plan and the contract-baker plan (EBIT in Tk a month)
| Boxes sold | Own plan (fixed Tk 1,20,000, variable Tk 24) | Contract plan (fixed Tk 40,000, variable Tk 32) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 7,500 | 0 | 20,000 |
| 10,000 | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| 12,500 | 80,000 | 60,000 |

> The plan with more fixed cost has a larger DOL: it does better in good months and worse in bad months.

At 10,000 boxes the two plans earn the same EBIT, but their risks differ. The own plan has a DOL of 4.0. The contract plan has a DOL of Tk 80,000 ÷ Tk 40,000 = 2.0. If sales fall to 7,500 boxes, the contract plan still earns Tk 20,000, and the own plan earns nothing. If sales rise to 12,500 boxes, the own plan earns Tk 80,000 and the contract plan earns Tk 60,000. The plan with more fixed cost does better when sales are high and worse when they are low. A manager who fears weak sales may prefer the plan with less fixed cost, even though it pays less in a good month.

> Operating leverage magnifies gains and losses in the same way.

Operating leverage works in both directions. A high DOL is welcome when sales rise, and it is dangerous when sales fall. This is why we speak of it as a source of risk and not only as a source of gain. A bakery with a DOL of 4.0 and a bad season of 20 per cent fewer sales loses 80 per cent of its EBIT.

> Slips with DOL: using it far from its base, and forgetting that the fixed cost may jump.

*Common slips.* The DOL is found at one level of sales, and it applies to changes from that level. Using the DOL found at 10,000 boxes to judge a change from 12,500 boxes gives a wrong answer. A second slip is to forget the limit set in section 9.2: if sales grow so much that a new oven must be bought, the fixed cost rises, and the old DOL no longer holds.

> Before moving on: DOL = contribution ÷ EBIT, and it measures how much EBIT is magnified.

*Before moving on.* Fixed operating costs magnify changes in EBIT. The DOL measures the magnification at one level of sales, and it equals contribution divided by EBIT. You should now be able to say: "A DOL of 3.0 means that a fall of 10 per cent in sales causes a fall of 30 per cent in EBIT."

## 9.4 Financial Leverage

@! Go slowly here.
> A family with a shop loan shows the same magnifying effect, now below EBIT.

A family buys a small shop with a loan. After paying the shop's running costs, the shop brings in Tk 10,000 each month. The loan costs Tk 4,000 a month, whatever happens. The family keeps Tk 6,000. Suppose the Tk 10,000 rises by 10 per cent to Tk 11,000. The family now keeps Tk 7,000, which is a rise of 16.7 per cent. Suppose it falls by 10 per cent to Tk 9,000. The family keeps Tk 5,000, a fall of 16.7 per cent. The fixed loan payment magnified the change, in the same way that fixed costs did in the last section.

> Financial leverage is the magnifying effect of fixed financing payments on the owners' profit.

@ Financial leverage | How fixed payments to lenders magnify changes in the owners' profit.

**Financial leverage** means the way that fixed payments to lenders magnify the effect of a change in EBIT on the profit left for the owners. The difference from operating leverage is where it acts. Operating leverage links sales to EBIT. Financial leverage links EBIT to net profit.

> The bakery's monthly profit layout, from EBIT to net profit, shows the effect in numbers.

:case Case file, illustrative. At 10,000 boxes the bakery has an EBIT of Tk 40,000 a month. Its debt of Tk 7,00,000 at 12% a year costs Tk 7,00,000 × 0.12 ÷ 12 = Tk 7,000 a month in interest. The illustrative tax rate is 25 per cent of profit before tax.

> The next table follows EBIT of Tk 40,000 and Tk 80,000 down to net profit.

The table below follows the bakery's EBIT down the profit layout, once at the planned level and once at the level of 12,500 boxes. Read each column from top to bottom: EBIT, less interest, equals profit before tax, less tax, equals net profit.

:table From EBIT to net profit at two levels of sales (Tk a month)
| Item | At 10,000 boxes | At 12,500 boxes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EBIT | 40,000 | 80,000 |
| Less interest | 7,000 | 7,000 |
| Profit before tax | 33,000 | 73,000 |
| Less tax at 25% | 8,250 | 18,250 |
| Net profit | 24,750 | 54,750 |

> EBIT doubled, but net profit rose by more than double.

EBIT rose by 100 per cent, from Tk 40,000 to Tk 80,000. Net profit rose from Tk 24,750 to Tk 54,750. That is a rise of Tk 30,000 on Tk 24,750, or 121.2 per cent. The interest of Tk 7,000 did not change, and so the whole of the extra EBIT reached the owners, apart from tax.

> The degree of financial leverage tells by how many times net profit changes when EBIT changes.

@ Degree of financial leverage (DFL) | The per cent change in net profit caused by a one per cent change in EBIT.

The **degree of financial leverage (DFL)** means the per cent change in net profit for each one per cent change in EBIT. For a firm with interest only, a shorter form is available:

> The formula for DFL when the firm pays interest only.

DFL = EBIT ÷ (EBIT − interest).

> Worked example 4: DFL for the bakery with interest only.

*Worked example 4.* *Given.* EBIT is Tk 40,000 a month and interest is Tk 7,000 a month. *Find.* The DFL. *Formula.* DFL = EBIT ÷ (EBIT − interest).

> Steps, answer and check for DFL.

*Steps.* EBIT − interest = Tk 40,000 − Tk 7,000 = Tk 33,000. DFL = Tk 40,000 ÷ Tk 33,000 = 1.2121, which we write as 1.21. *Answer.* The DFL is 1.21: each one per cent change in EBIT changes net profit by 1.21 per cent. *Check.* A rise of 100 per cent in EBIT should raise net profit by 1.2121 × 100 = 121.2 per cent, which agrees with the table above. Tax does not change the answer, because tax is the same share of profit before tax at both levels.

> Preferred dividends are another fixed payment, but they are paid from profit after tax.

Some firms owe a second kind of fixed payment. A preferred dividend is a fixed payment to a special kind of owner, who is paid before the ordinary owners. Chapter 14 teaches this kind of owner fully; here we need only one fact. A preferred dividend is paid out of profit after tax, whereas interest is paid before tax. To compare the two fairly with EBIT, we must raise the preferred dividend to the amount of EBIT that is needed to pay it. We do this by dividing by (1 − t), where t is the tax rate.

> The full DFL formula includes interest, preferred dividends and tax.

The full formula for DFL is therefore: DFL = EBIT ÷ (EBIT − interest − preferred dividend ÷ (1 − t)).

> Worked example 5: a what-if exercise with a preferred dividend of Tk 3,000.

*Worked example 5.* *Given.* Suppose, as an exercise only, that the bakery also owed a preferred dividend of Tk 3,000 a month. EBIT is Tk 40,000, interest is Tk 7,000, and t = 0.25. *Find.* The DFL. *Formula.* DFL = EBIT ÷ (EBIT − interest − preferred dividend ÷ (1 − t)).

> Steps, answer and check for DFL with a preferred dividend.

*Steps.* The preferred dividend divided by (1 − t) is Tk 3,000 ÷ 0.75 = Tk 4,000. The bottom line is Tk 40,000 − Tk 7,000 − Tk 4,000 = Tk 29,000. DFL = Tk 40,000 ÷ Tk 29,000 = 1.3793, which we write as 1.38. *Answer.* The DFL rises from 1.21 to 1.38. *Check.* If EBIT doubles to Tk 80,000, the bottom line becomes Tk 69,000, which is 2.379 times Tk 29,000: a rise of 137.9 per cent, as 1.3793 × 100 predicts. The extra fixed payment raised the magnifying effect.

> If EBIT falls to the level of the fixed payments, profit disappears.

Financial leverage also cuts the other way. If EBIT falls to Tk 7,000, the bakery's interest uses all of it and profit before tax is zero. If EBIT falls further, the bakery makes a loss, and it may be unable to pay its lender on time. A lender who is not paid can take action against the firm. This is the real danger of financial risk, and it is why the DFL grows very large as EBIT approaches the fixed payments.

> Without any interest or preferred dividend, the DFL is 1.0.

A firm with no loans and no preferred shares has a DFL of 1.0, because its net profit moves in the same per cent as its EBIT. Why would any firm borrow, then? A firm borrows because lenders supply money that the owners cannot supply, and because the firm may earn more on that money than the interest it pays. Chapters 13 and 14 treat these reasons in detail. Here we only note that the gain and the danger arrive together.

> Before moving on: DFL = EBIT ÷ (EBIT − fixed financing payments), after adjusting dividends for tax.

*Before moving on.* Financial leverage magnifies changes in EBIT into larger changes in net profit, because interest and preferred dividends are fixed. The DFL measures the magnification. You should now be able to say: "A DFL of 1.5 means that a fall of 10 per cent in EBIT causes a fall of 15 per cent in net profit."

## 9.5 Total Leverage

> The two effects work in a chain, so we can find the combined effect.

A change in sales passes through two stages before it reaches the owners. At the first stage, operating leverage turns the change in sales into a larger change in EBIT. At the second stage, financial leverage turns the change in EBIT into a still larger change in net profit. The combined effect is the product of the two effects.

> Total leverage is the two effects together, and the DTL measures it.

@ Total leverage | The combined effect of operating and financial leverage on net profit.
@ Degree of total leverage (DTL) | The per cent change in net profit caused by a one per cent change in sales.

**Total leverage** means the combined effect of operating leverage and financial leverage. The **degree of total leverage (DTL)** means the per cent change in net profit for each one per cent change in sales. It is found by multiplying the two earlier measures:

> The formula for DTL: multiply, never add.

DTL = DOL × DFL.

> A direct form of the DTL formula also exists.

There is a direct form as well: DTL = contribution ÷ (EBIT − interest − preferred dividend ÷ (1 − t)).

> Worked example 6: the bakery's DTL, found two ways.

*Worked example 6.* *Given.* At 10,000 boxes, contribution is Tk 1,60,000; DOL is 4.0; DFL is 1.2121 (interest only). *Find.* The DTL. *Formula.* DTL = DOL × DFL.

> Steps, answer and check for DTL.

*Steps.* DTL = 4.0 × 1.2121 = 4.848, which we write as 4.85. By the direct form, DTL = Tk 1,60,000 ÷ Tk 33,000 = 4.848. *Answer.* The DTL is 4.85: each one per cent change in sales changes net profit by 4.85 per cent. *Check.* A rise of 25 per cent in sales should raise net profit by 4.848 × 25 = 121.2 per cent, and the table of profit layouts above shows exactly that. If the bakery also owed the preferred dividend of Worked example 5, the direct form gives Tk 1,60,000 ÷ Tk 29,000 = 5.52.

The figure below draws the chain. Read it from top to bottom, in the same order as the profit layout of Chapter 3. The bracket on the right of the upper half is operating leverage. The bracket on the right of the lower half is financial leverage. The long bracket on the left covers the whole chain, and it is total leverage.

The table below gathers the three measures. Use it as a summary and not as a substitute for the worked examples above.

> The most common error is to add the two measures instead of multiplying.

*Common slips.* The most common error is to add the DOL and the DFL. The bakery's 4.0 plus 1.21 gives 5.21, which is wrong; the right answer is 4.85. The reason is that the second effect acts on an already enlarged change, so the two effects multiply. Another slip is to take the DTL from different levels of sales for the two parts: both measures must be found at the same starting level.

> Before moving on: the DTL multiplies the two leverages and shows how a change in sales reaches the owners.

*Before moving on.* The DTL equals the DOL multiplied by the DFL. It tells by how many times a change in sales is magnified in net profit. You should now be able to say: "A DOL of 2.0 and a DFL of 1.5 give a DTL of 3.0, so a rise of 10 per cent in sales raises net profit by 30 per cent."

## 9.6 Why Leverage Matters to a Firm

> Leverage widens the range of results, which is the same as raising risk.

Chapter 8 taught that risk means the chance that results differ from hopes, and that the standard deviation and the coefficient of variation measure it. Leverage connects directly to that idea. Leverage does not change the average result by itself. It widens the spread of results around the average. A wider spread is a higher risk. We now show this in numbers.

> Worked case: sales may rise or fall by one-quarter with equal chance.

:case Case file, illustrative. Suppose the sales of the bakery have an equal chance, 0.5 each, of being 25 per cent below plan (7,500 boxes) or 25 per cent above plan (12,500 boxes). We compare the spread of sales, of EBIT, and of profit before tax. Interest is Tk 7,000. We use profit before tax so that the effect of tax does not hide the effect of leverage.

> The next table gives the two outcomes, the expected value, the standard deviation and the coefficient of variation.

The table below gives the results. Each line shows the low outcome, the high outcome, the expected value (the average, weighted by chance), the standard deviation (the usual spread around the average) and the coefficient of variation (spread per unit of expected result). The numbers for the low and high outcomes come from the two earlier tables.

:table Spread of results when sales are 25 per cent below or above plan (Tk)
| Item | Low | High | Expected value | Standard deviation | Coefficient of variation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sales | 3,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 4,00,000 | 1,00,000 | 0.25 |
| EBIT | 0 | 80,000 | 40,000 | 40,000 | 1.00 |
| Profit before tax | −7,000 | 73,000 | 33,000 | 40,000 | 1.21 |

> The coefficient of variation grows by the DOL, and then by the DFL.

*Reasoning.* The expected value of sales is half of 3,00,000 plus half of 5,00,000, which is Tk 4,00,000. Each outcome lies Tk 1,00,000 from it, so the standard deviation is Tk 1,00,000 and the coefficient of variation is 1,00,000 ÷ 4,00,000 = 0.25. For EBIT, the outcomes are 0 and 80,000; the expected value is Tk 40,000; each outcome lies Tk 40,000 from it; and the coefficient is 1.00. For profit before tax, the outcomes are −7,000 and 73,000; the expected value is Tk 33,000; each outcome lies Tk 40,000 from it; and the coefficient is 40,000 ÷ 33,000 = 1.21.

> Conclusion and check: risk per taka rose by exactly the DOL and then the DFL.

*Conclusion.* The coefficient of variation rose from 0.25 for sales to 1.00 for EBIT, which is 4.0 times as large, the DOL. It rose again to 1.21, which is 1.21 times as large, the DFL. Overall, it is 4.85 times its starting value. *Check.* 0.25 × 4.848 = 1.21. The coefficient of variation of profit before tax equals the coefficient of variation of sales multiplied by the DTL. Leverage did not change the average outcome in the way that a better product would. It made the same swing in sales a far greater swing in profit.

> A firm cannot remove its business risk, but it can choose its financial risk.

What should a firm do with this knowledge? Business risk is mostly fixed by the kind of work the firm does, and by the machines it needs. Financial risk is mostly chosen, by the decision on how much to borrow. A sensible firm looks at the one when deciding the other. A firm with high fixed costs, such as a factory or an airline, should usually keep its debts modest, because the two leverages multiply. A firm with steady sales and low fixed costs can carry more debt more safely.

> Different people care about leverage for different reasons.

The same facts look different to different people. An owner sees the chance of a large gain and the chance of a large loss. A lender sees the chance that EBIT will fall below the interest and the loan will not be paid. A manager must balance both views, and must also remember that the people who work in the firm lose their jobs if the firm fails.

> Leverage is neither good nor bad; it is a tool that raises both gains and dangers.

Leverage is not a fault. It is a tool that raises gains in good years and losses in bad years. The DOL, the DFL and the DTL do not say whether the risk is worth taking. They say how large the risk is. The decision belongs to the firm, and it uses a further idea, which we study in Chapter 10: the return that owners and lenders require for the risk they carry. Chapters 13 and 14 describe the sources of long-term money, and Chapter 15 puts a price on that money.

> Before moving on: leverage widens the spread of profit, and the firm chooses how much of it to add.

*Before moving on.* Leverage widens the spread of profit around its average. The coefficient of variation of profit is the coefficient of variation of sales multiplied by the DTL. Business risk is mostly given; financial risk is mostly chosen.

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas in a few lines.

Business risk comes from the firm's own work and shows in EBIT. Financial risk comes from borrowing and shows below EBIT. The break-even point is the sales at which profit is zero; it equals fixed cost divided by contribution per unit. Operating leverage, measured by DOL = contribution ÷ EBIT, magnifies changes in sales into larger changes in EBIT. Financial leverage, measured by DFL = EBIT ÷ (EBIT − interest − preferred dividend ÷ (1 − t)), magnifies changes in EBIT into larger changes in net profit. Total leverage, measured by DTL = DOL × DFL, joins the two. For the bakery at 10,000 boxes, the break-even point is 7,500 boxes, DOL is 4.0, DFL is 1.21 and DTL is 4.85.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five common misunderstandings and their corrections.

*"Leverage means debt."* Leverage is any magnifying effect of fixed payments. Operating leverage comes from fixed costs and needs no loan. Financial leverage comes from fixed payments to lenders.

> The DOL changes with the level of sales.
*"The DOL is a fixed number for a firm."* It depends on the level of sales at which it is found, and it falls as sales rise above break-even.

> The two leverages multiply.
*"The DTL is the DOL plus the DFL."* The two effects multiply. The bakery's DTL is 4.0 × 1.21 = 4.85, and not 5.21.

> High operating leverage magnifies gains as well as losses.
*"A high DOL is always bad."* It magnifies gains as much as losses. It is a risk, and the firm may accept it for a good reason, for example when the fixed cost buys a cheaper cost per unit.

> Divide fixed cost by contribution.
*"Break-even sales equal fixed cost."* Fixed cost must be divided by the contribution per unit, or by the share of each taka of sales that is contribution.

## In History

> A fund that borrowed more than twenty times its own money.

Long-Term Capital Management was an American investment fund that began trading in 1994. By early 1998 it had borrowed about US$125 billion against about US$4.7 billion of its own money, according to one study of the case.^2 With so little of its own money compared with its debt, a fall of less than four per cent in the value of what it held could erase its own money. That is our own arithmetic, using the same idea as this chapter: a fixed claim by lenders magnifies every change for the owners. In 1998 markets moved against the fund. In September 1998 the Federal Reserve Bank of New York brought the fund's main lenders together, and they put in about US$3.6 billion of new money to prevent a disorderly collapse. The Federal Reserve did not lend its own money.^3 The fund's history is a large-scale picture of financial leverage working in the wrong direction.
