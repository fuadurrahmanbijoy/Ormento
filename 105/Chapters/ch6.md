:chapter VI | Choosing Big Purchases: Process and Cash Flows
:mood Before the money is spent, the plan must be drawn.
:desc Some purchases last for years and cannot be returned. This chapter explains how a firm plans such purchases, the steps it follows, and which cash flows belong in the plan. We then draw the cash-flow timeline of two choices facing the bakery, so that Chapter 7 can judge them.

> The bakery has money for one large purchase and two good options.

On 1 July 2026, Sundial Bakery has two options. The first is a second oven. The second is a delivery van. Each costs Tk 3,00,000, and each is expected to serve for five years. The bank will lend the bakery only Tk 3,00,000 for new equipment, so the owner can buy one option but not both. Both look useful. Which should she choose?

> A wrong choice lasts for years, so the plan must come before the purchase.

The puzzle is harder than it seems. A purchase of flour can be corrected next week, when the next order is placed. An oven cannot. It will occupy the kitchen for five years, and its cost has already left the bank account. The owner needs a way to look ahead, to write down what each option will cost and bring, and only then to decide. That way of looking ahead has a name, and this chapter teaches it.

> This chapter prepares the numbers; Chapter 7 chooses between them.

We shall not choose between the oven and the van in this chapter. The choice needs the techniques of Chapter 7. Our task here is earlier and, in practice, harder: to decide *which* cash flows belong in the plan, and to place them on a timeline. A mistake in this step cannot be repaired by clever arithmetic later.

## 6.1 What Capital Budgeting Is and Why It Matters

> Capital expenditure is spending on assets that serve for many years.

@ Capital expenditure | Money spent on assets that serve for more than a year.

A firm spends money in two broad ways. Some spending is used up quickly: flour, wages and electricity are consumed within days or weeks. Other spending buys something that serves for many years: an oven, a van, a building. We call this second kind **capital expenditure**. It is money spent on assets that will serve the firm for more than one year.

> A project is a single plan to make one such purchase.

@ Project | One investment plan, such as buying a single oven.

Each such plan has a name. A **project** is a single investment plan, such as the purchase of one oven, together with the cash it is expected to bring. The bakery's second oven is one project. The delivery van is another. A firm may have many projects in view at the same time.

> Capital budgeting is the planning and choosing of long-lived projects.

@ Capital budgeting | Planning and choosing long-lived investments.

**Capital budgeting** is the planning and choosing of long-lived investments. It answers one question for each project: should the firm spend this money now, in return for cash over the coming years? Chapter 2 named this the *investment decision*. Capital budgeting is the careful method for making it.

> Five features make these decisions more serious than daily spending.

Why does this decision deserve its own chapter? Five features set it apart from everyday spending. First, the sums are large compared with the firm's size. Second, the effects last for years, so a poor choice is paid for repeatedly. Third, the decision is hard to reverse: a used oven sells for much less than its price. Fourth, the future is uncertain, so every forecast may prove wrong. Fifth, these decisions shape what the firm will be, because the firm becomes what it buys.

> The table contrasts daily spending with capital expenditure.

The table below contrasts the two kinds of spending. Read each row across: the first column names a feature, and the next two columns say how it appears in daily spending and in capital expenditure.

:table Daily spending compared with capital expenditure
| Feature | Daily spending (flour) | Capital expenditure (oven) |
| Size | Small | Large |
| Life of the benefit | Days or weeks | Many years |
| Can it be undone? | Easily | With difficulty and loss |
| Needs a formal plan? | Seldom | Always |

> One exception: small purchases of long life need little formality.

There is an exception. Not every long-lived purchase needs a full plan. A new shop sign costing Tk 2,000 serves for years, yet no firm would draw a five-year timeline for it. Firms set a limit: above a certain size, a formal plan is required; below it, the manager may decide quickly. The size of the limit depends on the size of the firm.

## 6.2 Kinds of Projects and the Steps of the Process

> Projects are related to one another in three important ways.

Projects rarely stand alone. Whether the firm may accept one depends on what else it wishes to do. The relations between projects matter for three ideas, named in the next three paragraphs.

> An independent project does not affect the others.

@ Independent project | A project whose acceptance does not affect other projects.

An **independent project** is a project whose acceptance does not affect any other project. If the bakery wished to buy a new shop sign and also a second oven, and the two had no effect on each other, each would be judged on its own merits. The firm could accept both, one or neither.

> Mutually exclusive projects rule one another out.

@ Mutually exclusive project | A project that rules out another if it is chosen.

A **mutually exclusive project** is a project that rules out another if it is chosen. A firm that buys the oven cannot also buy the van with the same loan. The two compete for the same limited money. Mutually exclusive projects must be compared with one another, and only the best may be chosen.

> Capital rationing means the firm has too little money for all its projects.

@ Capital rationing | A limit on the money available for projects.

**Capital rationing** is a limit on the money available for projects. It occurs when the firm has more worthwhile projects than money. The bakery's position is an example: the bank will lend only Tk 3,00,000 for new equipment, so the oven and the van become mutually exclusive in practice, although they might be independent if money were plentiful.

> A table sets the three relations side by side.

The table below sets the three ideas side by side, with an example from the bakery for each.

:table Three relations between projects
| Idea | Meaning | Bakery example |
| Independent | Choosing one does not affect another | A new shop sign and a second oven |
| Mutually exclusive | Choosing one rules out another | A second oven or a delivery van |
| Capital rationing | Money limits how many can be chosen | Tk 3,00,000 available for projects costing Tk 6,00,000 |

> The process has five steps, from finding ideas to reviewing results.

Now the process itself. A firm that handles projects well follows five steps, shown in the figure below. Read the figure from left to right. Each box is a step, and each arrow means that the next step follows. The dashed arrow at the bottom returns from the last step to the first, to show that the process repeats. The next paragraphs describe each step.

:fig The five steps of capital budgeting. | ch6_fig1.svg

> Step one: ideas come from many places.

*Step 1, find ideas.* Projects begin as ideas. They come from staff who see a problem, from customers who ask for something new, and from equipment that is wearing out. A firm that welcomes ideas from every level finds more good projects than a firm that waits for the owner to think of them.

> Step two: estimate the cash flows each project will bring.

*Step 2, estimate cash flows.* For each project, someone must forecast the money it will cost and the money it will bring in each future year. This step is the hardest and the most important, because every later step relies on it. A forecast will be wrong in some degree, so the person making it should say how sure he or she is. The rest of this chapter concerns this step.

> Step three: judge each project by a rule.

*Step 3, judge the project.* The forecast cash flows are put through a decision rule, such as the payback period or net present value. Chapter 7 teaches these rules. A rule turns a timeline of cash flows into a verdict: accept, reject, or rank.

> Step four: someone with authority decides.

*Step 4, decide.* The verdict is only advice. A person with authority, the owner of the bakery or, in a larger firm, a committee or the board, makes the decision. Larger projects go to higher levels, because the damage of a wrong choice grows with the sum.

> Step five: start the work, then compare results with the forecast.

*Step 5, start the work and review.* If the project is accepted, the firm buys the asset and begins. Months and years later, the firm compares the actual cash flows with the forecast. The review is not a search for blame. Its purpose is to improve the next forecast, which is why the dashed arrow in the figure leads back to step one.

## 6.3 Relevant Cash Flows: Outlay, Yearly Inflows and End of Life

> Why capital budgeting uses cash flows, not profit.

A project may be described in two ways: by the profit it earns or by the cash it brings. Capital budgeting uses cash. The reason is practical. A firm cannot pay wages, repay a bank or reward its owner with *profit*; it can do so only with *cash*. Chapter 1 defined a cash flow as money actually moving in or out. Profit contains items, such as depreciation, which do not move money at all, as we shall see in section 6.4.

> A project's cash flows fall into three kinds.

Every project produces cash flows of three kinds, in an order that follows the life of the asset. The figure below shows them on a timeline. Read it as before: the line moves through time, an arrow pointing up is money received, and an arrow pointing down is money paid out. Today's outlay is at the left, the yearly receipts are in the middle, and the final sum is at the right.

:fig The three kinds of cash flow in a project that lasts five years. | ch6_fig2.svg

> The initial outlay is all the money spent at the start.

@ Initial outlay | All the money spent at the start of a project.

The **initial outlay** is all the money spent at the start of a project, to make the asset ready for use. It includes the price of the asset and every other cost needed to begin. Suppose an oven costs Tk 2,50,000, delivery costs Tk 5,000, and installation costs Tk 10,000. The initial outlay is Tk 2,65,000, not Tk 2,50,000. The outlay is a cash flow *out*, so we write it with a minus sign.

> Operating cash flow is the cash a project brings each year.

@ Operating cash flow | The cash a project brings in each year of its life.

The **operating cash flow** is the cash a project brings in each year of its working life. It is the extra cash received from sales, less the extra cash paid for materials, wages and other running costs, and after the extra tax that the project causes. Section 6.4 shows how to find it. For the oven, it is the extra cash earned each year by baking more.

> Terminal cash flow is cash at the very end of the project.

@ Terminal cash flow | Cash received or paid at the end of a project's life.

The **terminal cash flow** is cash received or paid at the end of the project's life. The most common case is the sale of the old asset. If the oven can be sold for Tk 20,000 after five years, that sum is a terminal cash flow of plus Tk 20,000, received at the end of year 5. Money tied up in extra stock may also be released at the end. To keep the arithmetic clear, this book ignores tax on such sales.

> The bakery's two options are assumed to be worth nothing at the end.

For the bakery's two options, we assume that the oven and the van have no resale value at the end of five years, and that no extra stock is needed. Their terminal cash flows are therefore zero. This assumption keeps the case small; the method is the same when a terminal sum exists.

> Many students forget that the outlay is a cash flow too.

Many students find this step confusing, because the outlay is a payment, not a profit or a cost in the ordinary sense. They forget to place it on the timeline, and the project then looks free. Rule: every taka that leaves or enters the firm because of the project appears on the timeline, with its own date and sign.

## 6.4 Sunk Costs, Extra Costs, Depreciation and Tax Effects

> Only the extra cash caused by the project belongs in the plan.

@ Incremental cash flow | The extra cash flow caused by the project alone.

Which cash flows belong in the plan? A simple test answers the question. Compare the firm's cash *with* the project and *without* it. The difference, year by year, is the **incremental cash flow**: the extra cash flow caused by the project alone. Only incremental flows belong on the timeline.

> The test counts extra costs and ignores costs that do not change.

The test works in both directions. If the oven raises the electricity bill by Tk 20,000 a year, that extra cost counts, because the project causes it. If the bakery pays Tk 30,000 a year in shop rent whether or not it buys the oven, the rent does not count, because the rent is the same with and without the project. A project that takes sales away from the firm's other products also has a cost: the lost sales are a negative incremental flow.

> A sunk cost has already been spent and cannot return.

@ Sunk cost | Money already spent that cannot be recovered.

A **sunk cost** is money already spent that cannot be recovered. Suppose that last month the bakery paid Tk 5,000 for a survey of delivery routes for the van. That money is gone whether or not the van is bought. It is therefore not an incremental flow, and it must stay off the timeline. This rule feels strange, since the money was real. But the decision concerns only the future; the past cannot be changed by choosing either option.

> Depreciation spreads an asset's cost over its working life.

@ Depreciation | Spreading an asset's cost over the years of its life.

**Depreciation** means spreading an asset's cost over the years of its life. One simple method is the *straight-line* rule: depreciation each year equals the cost, less the expected resale value, divided by the number of years. The oven costs Tk 3,00,000, has no resale value, and serves for five years, so its yearly depreciation is Tk 3,00,000 ÷ 5 = Tk 60,000.

> Depreciation is a charge in the profit statement, but no cash moves.

An important fact follows. The Tk 3,00,000 left the bank on the day of purchase; that was the initial outlay. When the accountant charges Tk 60,000 of depreciation in each later year, no money leaves the bank. Depreciation is a *non-cash charge*. It reduces profit, but it does not reduce cash. For this reason, a project's operating cash flow is not the same as its profit.

> Depreciation still matters, because it lowers the tax paid.

Yet depreciation affects cash in one real way: it lowers taxable profit, and so it lowers the tax the firm pays. The tax saved is a true cash gain. Suppose a machine costs Tk 1,00,000, serves for five years, and has no resale value. Its yearly depreciation is Tk 20,000. Suppose the machine earns Tk 60,000 a year before depreciation, and the tax rate is 25%.^1

^1: The tax rate of 25% is illustrative. The actual rate depends on the type of firm and on the law in force. The straight-line rule is also used here for teaching; tax law prescribes its own depreciation rates. Both should be checked against current Bangladesh rules before use.

> The table shows the tax with and without the depreciation charge.

The table below computes the tax in two ways: first as if depreciation were not allowed, then as the law allows. The difference is the tax saving from depreciation.

:table Tax with and without the depreciation charge (Tk a year)
| Item | Without depreciation | With depreciation |
| Earnings before depreciation | 60,000 | 60,000 |
| Less depreciation | 0 | 20,000 |
| Taxable profit | 60,000 | 40,000 |
| Tax at 25% | 15,000 | 10,000 |
| Cash left after tax | 45,000 | 50,000 |

> The saving equals depreciation multiplied by the tax rate.

The tax saving is Tk 15,000 − Tk 10,000 = Tk 5,000. This equals the depreciation multiplied by the tax rate: Tk 20,000 × 25% = Tk 5,000. The cash left with depreciation allowed is Tk 50,000, which is Tk 5,000 more than without. A firm that earns no taxable profit cannot use this saving in that year, because it has no tax to reduce. This exception matters for small or loss-making firms.

> A simple route from profit to cash: add back depreciation.

There is a quick route from profit to cash. Start with the profit after tax, then add back the depreciation, because depreciation was subtracted although no cash left. In words: operating cash flow = profit after tax + depreciation. Here the project's profit after tax is Tk 40,000 − Tk 10,000 = Tk 30,000, and adding Tk 20,000 of depreciation gives Tk 50,000, the same as the last row above.

:table Worked example 6.1: operating cash flow of a machine
| Step | Working |
| Given | Earnings before depreciation Tk 60,000 a year; depreciation Tk 20,000 a year; tax rate 25% |
| Find | The yearly operating cash flow |
| Formula | Operating cash flow = profit after tax + depreciation |
| Step 1 | Profit before tax = Tk 60,000 − Tk 20,000 = Tk 40,000 |
| Step 2 | Tax = 25% × Tk 40,000 = Tk 10,000; profit after tax = Tk 30,000 |
| Step 3 | Operating cash flow = Tk 30,000 + Tk 20,000 |
| Answer | Tk 50,000 a year |
| Check | Earnings Tk 60,000 less tax Tk 10,000 = Tk 50,000, the same answer by a second route |

> Interest on a loan does not belong in the project's cash flows.

One more rule completes the section. Interest paid on a loan, and the repayment of the loan itself, are *not* part of the project's operating cash flow. They belong to the financing decision, not to the investment decision. Chapter 7 charges each project for the cost of its funds through the discount rate. If interest were also subtracted here, the project would be charged twice. In the profit layout of Chapter 3, this means starting from EBIT, which is profit before interest and tax.

> Four tests decide whether an item belongs on the timeline.

*Before moving on.* Four tests tell us whether an item belongs on the timeline. Is it cash? Is it caused by the project? Is it in the future? Is it not interest on the loan? An item that fails any test stays off. You should now be able to say why depreciation is not a cash flow but still changes the cash flow.

## 6.5 Building a Project's Cash-Flow Timeline

> Four steps build a timeline for any project.

A timeline can be built for any project in four steps. First, list the initial outlay at the start, with a minus sign. Second, estimate the operating cash flow for each year. Third, add the terminal cash flow to the last year. Fourth, draw the line with each flow at its date. We shall now follow these steps for the bakery's two options.

> The oven's yearly cash flow is built from its extra profit and its depreciation.

For the oven, the bakery's forecast is that the extra baking will add profit of Tk 53,333 a year before interest and tax. This figure is rounded to the nearest taka. Tax at 25% takes Tk 13,333, leaving Tk 40,000 of profit after tax. The oven's depreciation is Tk 60,000 a year, as found in section 6.4. Adding the depreciation back gives the operating cash flow.

:table Worked example 6.2: the oven's yearly operating cash flow
| Step | Working |
| Given | Outlay Tk 3,00,000; life 5 years; no resale value; extra EBIT Tk 53,333 a year; tax rate 25% |
| Find | The operating cash flow for each year |
| Formula | Operating cash flow = profit after tax + depreciation |
| Step 1 | Depreciation = Tk 3,00,000 ÷ 5 = Tk 60,000 |
| Step 2 | Tax = 25% × Tk 53,333 = Tk 13,333; profit after tax = Tk 40,000 |
| Step 3 | Operating cash flow = Tk 40,000 + Tk 60,000 |
| Answer | Tk 1,00,000 a year, for years 1 to 5 |
| Check | EBIT Tk 53,333 + depreciation Tk 60,000 − tax Tk 13,333 = Tk 1,00,000 |

> The van brings uneven cash, with a loss after tax in year one.

The van is different. Its sales grow as customers learn of the delivery service, so its cash flows rise year by year. The profit after tax is a loss of Tk 10,000 in year 1, then Tk 20,000, Tk 50,000, Tk 80,000 and Tk 80,000. The van also depreciates by Tk 60,000 a year. Adding that back gives the operating cash flows in the table below.

:table The van's operating cash flows (Tk)
| Year | Profit after tax | Add back depreciation | Operating cash flow |
| 1 | −10,000 | 60,000 | 50,000 |
| 2 | 20,000 | 60,000 | 80,000 |
| 3 | 50,000 | 60,000 | 1,10,000 |
| 4 | 80,000 | 60,000 | 1,40,000 |
| 5 | 80,000 | 60,000 | 1,40,000 |

> A loss in profit can still be a gain in cash.

Look at year 1. The van's profit after tax is a loss, yet its cash flow is a gain of Tk 50,000. The reason is depreciation: it reduced the profit by Tk 60,000, but no cash left. This is a clear example of why capital budgeting follows cash, not profit.

> Both timelines are now complete and can be drawn.

With both operating cash flows known, and with a terminal cash flow of zero for each, we may place everything on the timeline. The table below lists the flows year by year. Money paid out carries a minus sign; money received carries none.

| 1 | 1,00,000 | 50,000 |
| 2 | 1,00,000 | 80,000 |

| 4 | 1,00,000 | 1,40,000 |

The figure below shows the same flows as bars. Each timeline has a horizontal line for time. A shaded bar above the line is money received in that year; its height shows its size. > The totals favour the van, but totals alone mislead us.

The van returns Tk 5,20,000 in total, and the oven returns Tk 5,00,000. A hasty reader would choose the van. But Chapters 4 and 5 taught us that when money arrives matters as much as how much arrives. The van's larger sums come later, and later sums are worth less today. The totals therefore cannot settle the choice. We shall use present value in Chapter 7 to do so.

> The two timelines have different shapes, and Chapter 5 supplies a tool for each.

The two timelines differ in shape, and Chapter 5 gave a tool for each. The oven's five equal receipts form an ordinary annuity, so one annuity factor will value them. The van's receipts form a mixed stream, so each receipt must be discounted separately and the results added. Chapter 7 uses both tools at a provisional discount rate of 10%.

## Summary

> The summary restates the chapter's main ideas.

Capital budgeting is the planning and choosing of long-lived projects. It matters because the sums are large, the effects last, and the decision is hard to undo. Projects may be independent or mutually exclusive, and money may be rationed. The process has five steps: find ideas, estimate cash flows, judge, decide, and review. Only incremental cash flows belong in the plan: the initial outlay, the yearly operating cash flows, and any terminal cash flow. Sunk costs, unchanged costs and interest are left out. Depreciation is a non-cash charge, but it lowers tax, so operating cash flow equals profit after tax plus depreciation.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Error one: counting a sunk cost.

*Counting money already spent.* A manager who has paid for a survey feels that the money must be justified, and so adds it to the project. Correction: a sunk cost cannot change with the decision, so it never belongs on the timeline.

> Error two: using profit instead of cash.

*Using profit instead of cash.* A project's accounting profit includes depreciation, which is not cash. Correction: add depreciation back to profit after tax, or start from earnings and subtract only the cash tax.

> Error three: subtracting interest.

*Subtracting interest as a project cost.* A student sees that the oven is bought with a loan, and deducts the interest from each year's cash flow. Correction: financing is charged later, through the discount rate. Deducting interest too would charge the project twice.

> Error four: counting costs that do not change.

*Counting costs that continue anyway.* Shop rent, the owner's salary and similar overheads often appear in a project's cost sheet. Correction: if the amount is the same with and without the project, it is not incremental and must be omitted.

## In History

> A great canal shows how long investors may wait for cash.

In 1858 a company was formed in France to build a canal across the Isthmus of Suez, in Egypt, linking the Mediterranean Sea with the Red Sea. The company's leader was Ferdinand de Lesseps, a French diplomat. The company raised money by selling shares. The canal opened to ships in November 1869, more than ten years after the company was formed, and only then could the company begin to earn tolls. Until that day the investors had paid out money and received no operating cash. They were judging the project by the cash flows they expected far in the future, which is the central idea of capital budgeting.
