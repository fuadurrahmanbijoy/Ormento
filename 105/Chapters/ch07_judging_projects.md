:chapter VII | Judging Projects
:mood A list of cash flows is only a list until a rule turns it into a decision.
:desc This chapter teaches how a firm decides whether a long-lived purchase is worth its cost. It begins with the quick test of payback, builds the trusted test of net present value, and adds two close relatives. It ends by asking what to do when the tests disagree.

> The oven and the van tempt the owner with two different sets of yearly cash.

The owner of Sundial Bakery has Tk 3,00,000 to spend and two ways to spend it. An oven would bring in Tk 1,00,000 in each of five years. A van would bring in a smaller sum at first and larger sums later: Tk 50,000, Tk 80,000, Tk 1,10,000, Tk 1,40,000 and Tk 1,40,000. Chapter VI showed how to find these yearly cash flows. This chapter asks what to do with them.

> A plain addition of the inflows seems to favour the van.

Begin with the plainest test: add up what each choice brings in. The oven brings in Tk 5,00,000 over five years. The van brings in Tk 5,20,000. The van gives Tk 20,000 more, so it seems to be the better choice. Yet the oven is the better choice, and we will show why before the chapter ends. Something in the plain test must be wrong.

> The fault lies in adding sums that arrive at different times.

The fault lies in the adding. Chapter IV taught that a taka received later is worth less than a taka received today. The van's larger sums come later, so the plain total flatters it. A fair test must first give each sum its true value today, and only then add.

> Five tests are taught here, from the quick and crude to the careful.

This chapter teaches five ways of judging a project. Each turns a timeline of cash flows into a decision. The first, payback, is quick but crude. The second, net present value, is the most trusted. The next two, the internal rate of return and the profitability index, are close relatives of the second. The last section asks what to do when the tests give different answers.

## Payback: How Long Until the Money Returns

### The question

> The owner's first question is how long it takes to get the money back.

Every investment begins with a payment. The oven costs Tk 3,00,000 at the start. A careful owner asks a simple question at once: how long will it take to get this money back? The answer to that question is a number of years, and it is the oldest test of a project.

@ Payback period | The time a project takes to bring back its initial outlay.

> The payback period is the number of years needed to recover the outlay.

The number of years has a name. The **payback period** is the length of time a project takes to bring back its initial outlay from its yearly cash inflows. A short payback means the money returns quickly. A long payback means the owner must wait.

> The rule compares the payback with a limit the owner sets in advance.

The owner then needs a rule. The owner chooses a cut-off, which is the longest wait that the firm will accept. A project whose payback is equal to or shorter than the cut-off is accepted. A project whose payback is longer is rejected. If two projects compete for the same money, the one with the shorter payback is preferred.

### Even inflows

> When the inflows are equal, one division gives the payback.

When every year brings the same inflow, the payback needs one division. We use a small example with clean numbers so that the method is the only new thing. The firm may buy a mixer for its kitchen.

> The first example uses a mixer that costs Tk 1,00,000.

*Given.* The mixer costs Tk 1,00,000 at the start. It brings in Tk 50,000 at the end of each of three years. *Find.* The payback period. *Formula.* Payback period equals the initial outlay divided by the yearly inflow.

> Dividing the outlay by the yearly inflow gives two years.

*Steps.* Payback = Tk 1,00,000 ÷ Tk 50,000 = 2 years. *Answer.* The mixer pays back its cost in 2 years. *Check.* Two years of inflow are 2 × Tk 50,000 = Tk 1,00,000, which is exactly the outlay.

### Uneven inflows

> When the inflows differ, we keep a running total instead.

Often the inflows are not equal, and one division no longer works. We then keep a running total of the cash received and watch the unpaid part of the outlay shrink. We take a display case as the example. It costs Tk 1,50,000 and brings in Tk 40,000, Tk 60,000 and Tk 80,000 at the ends of years 1, 2 and 3.

:table The display case: outlay still unpaid at the end of each year (Tk)
| Year | Cash inflow | Total received so far | Outlay still unpaid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | none | 0 | 1,50,000 |
| 1 | 40,000 | 40,000 | 1,10,000 |
| 2 | 60,000 | 1,00,000 | 50,000 |
| 3 | 80,000 | 1,80,000 | none |

> The outlay is fully repaid during year 3, so we need a fraction of that year.

The table shows that the outlay is repaid during year 3. After two full years Tk 50,000 is still unpaid. Year 3 brings in Tk 80,000, so the unpaid Tk 50,000 needs 50,000 ÷ 80,000 = 0.625 of that year. We assume that cash arrives evenly through the year. The payback is therefore 2 years plus 0.625 of a year, which is 2.625 years, or about 2.6 years.

> The check is to confirm that two full years leave less than the outlay received.

*Check.* After two years the firm has received Tk 1,00,000, which is less than Tk 1,50,000. After three years it has received Tk 1,80,000, which is more. The payback must lie between two and three years, and 2.6 years does.

### What payback does not tell us

> Payback is quick and easy, but it has three weaknesses.

Payback is popular because it is quick, easy to explain, and easy to compute by hand. It also tells the owner how soon cash comes back, which matters when money is short or the future is hard to foresee. But the test has three weaknesses, and a student must know all three.

> The first weakness is that payback treats a taka now and a taka later alike.

The first weakness is that payback ignores the time value of money. A taka received in year 1 counts the same as a taka received in year 2, although Chapter IV showed that they are not worth the same.^1 The second weakness is that payback ignores everything after the payback date. The third is that the cut-off is a matter of habit and not of reasoning. Nothing in the test tells the owner whether three years is the right limit or four.

> Two projects can have the same payback and very different value.

The second weakness needs an example. Two projects, D and E, each cost Tk 1,00,000. Their inflows are the same for the first three years. Project E then continues to earn, and project D stops.

:table Two projects with the same payback (Tk)
| Year | Project D | Project E |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| 2 | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| 3 | 20,000 | 20,000 |
| 4 | 0 | 80,000 |
| 5 | 0 | 80,000 |
| Payback | 3.0 years | 3.0 years |

> Payback cannot tell D from E, though E is far the better project.

Payback gives both projects 3.0 years. Yet project E earns Tk 1,60,000 more after the payback date, and project D earns nothing. A test that cannot tell these two apart is a poor guide when it stands alone. We return to D and E after we meet a better test.

### Using payback sensibly

> The cut-off is chosen by judgement, and it should fit the firm's own position.

How should an owner choose the cut-off? There is no formula. A firm that is short of cash may want its money back quickly, so it sets a short cut-off such as two years. A firm whose machines grow old fast may also choose a short cut-off, because the machine may be useless after a few years. A firm with a long-lived building may accept a longer wait. The cut-off is a judgement about the firm's own position, and it should never be copied from another firm without thought.

> Payback is best used as a first filter and a measure of waiting, and not as the final test.

Payback therefore has a modest but real use. It is a quick filter, which removes projects that wait too long before the owner studies the others in detail. It also states the waiting time in a form that every owner understands. But it is never enough to decide a large purchase alone. An owner who relies on payback only will reject slow projects that are very valuable, and will accept fast projects that add little.

### Common slips with payback

> Four slips are common when finding a payback period.

Four slips are common. The first is to add all the inflows and compare the total with the outlay, instead of keeping a running total year by year. The second is to forget the fraction of the last year, and to answer 3 years when the answer is 2.6. The third is to divide the unpaid amount by the wrong year's inflow, such as the first year's. The fourth is to forget the unit: 2.625 is a number of years, and 0.625 of a year is 0.625 × 12 = 7.5 months. A check against the running-balance table will find all four.

^1: A variation called discounted payback first reduces each inflow to its present value, and then counts the years. For the oven, discounting at 10% gives about 3.75 years instead of 3.0. It removes the first weakness, but not the second.

## Net Present Value: The Central Test

### The idea

@! Go slowly here. This is the main idea of the chapter.

> We bring every cash flow to the same date, which is today.

The opening puzzle showed that sums received at different dates cannot be added fairly. The remedy comes from Chapter IV. We bring every sum back to today by discounting, so that all the sums are measured on the same date. After that, adding them is fair.

> Discounting shrinks each later sum, and it shrinks the later ones more.

Look at the oven again. It brings in Tk 1,00,000 at the end of each of five years. At a discount rate of 10% a year, the Tk 1,00,000 of year 1 is worth Tk 90,909 today. The Tk 1,00,000 of year 5 is worth only Tk 62,092 today. The later the sum, the smaller its value today. The figure below shows this shrinking.

:fig The oven's yearly inflow of Tk 1,00,000, and what each inflow is worth today at a discount rate of 10%. | figs/ch7_discounting.svg

> Adding the values today gives the present value of all the inflows.

Adding the five values gives about Tk 3,79,079. This is the present value of the oven's inflows. It is what the oven's whole stream of cash is worth to the owner today. The oven costs Tk 3,00,000 today. The inflows are worth more than the cost, and the difference is the gain.

@ Net present value | The present value of a project's inflows minus its initial outlay.

> The gain, measured in taka today, is the net present value.

That gain has a name and a symbol. The **net present value**, or NPV, of a project is the present value of its cash inflows minus its initial outlay. It states how many taka today the project adds to the owner's wealth, or removes from it. Chapter II taught that a firm should aim to raise the wealth of its owners. NPV measures exactly that aim for one project.

### The formula

> The formula discounts each inflow and then subtracts the outlay.

In words: net present value equals the present value of all cash inflows, minus the initial outlay. In symbols, let CF1 be the cash inflow in year 1, CF2 the inflow in year 2, and so on up to CFn in the last year n. Let r be the discount rate written as a decimal, so that 10% is 0.10. Then:

> The formula in symbols adds the discounted inflows and subtracts the outlay.

NPV = CF1 ÷ (1 + r) + CF2 ÷ (1 + r)² + ... + CFn ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ − Outlay.

> The outlay is not discounted, and equal inflows can use the annuity factor.

The outlay is paid today, at the start, so it has no discounting. Each inflow is divided by (1 + r) once for each year of waiting. Chapter V gave a shorter way for equal inflows. If the yearly inflow is the same in every year, then the present value of the inflows is the yearly inflow multiplied by the annuity factor for r and n.^2

^2: The tables show factors to four decimals. In this chapter we multiply by the unrounded factor from a calculator and round the answer to the nearest taka, so that our answers match the case file. A student who uses the four-decimal table factor may find an answer that differs by a few taka. This is rounding and not a mistake.

@ Accept-reject rule | Accept a project if its NPV is above zero; reject it if it is below zero.

> The decision rule is to accept a project whose NPV is above zero.

The decision follows at once. The **accept-reject rule** says: accept a project if its NPV is above zero, and reject it if its NPV is below zero. A positive NPV means that the inflows are worth more today than the outlay. The project adds wealth. A negative NPV means that the project takes wealth away.

> A fixed routine of six moves keeps the working in order.

A fixed routine keeps the working in order, and it does not change from one project to another. First, draw the timeline and list every cash flow with its year. Second, write the discount rate as a decimal. Third, find the present value of each inflow, or use the annuity factor when the inflows are equal. Fourth, add the present values. Fifth, subtract the initial outlay. Sixth, apply the accept-reject rule and write the answer in a sentence, with its unit, which is the taka.

### A first example with clean numbers

> We test the mixer from the payback section at a discount rate of 10%.

We return to the mixer, so that the method is the only thing to learn. *Given.* The outlay is Tk 1,00,000. The inflow is Tk 50,000 at the end of each of years 1, 2 and 3. The discount rate is 10%. *Find.* The NPV, and whether to accept. *Formula.* NPV equals the yearly inflow times the annuity factor, minus the outlay.

> The factor for 10% and three years is about 2.4869, and the steps follow.

*Steps.* The annuity factor for 10% and 3 years is 2.4869 in the table, and 2.486852 on a calculator. Present value of the inflows = Tk 50,000 × 2.486852 = Tk 1,24,343. Then NPV = Tk 1,24,343 − Tk 1,00,000 = Tk 24,343. *Answer.* The NPV is Tk 24,343. It is above zero, so the rule says accept.

> The check adds the three discounted inflows one by one.

*Check.* Discount each inflow separately: Tk 50,000 ÷ 1.10 = Tk 45,455; Tk 50,000 ÷ 1.21 = Tk 41,322; Tk 50,000 ÷ 1.331 = Tk 37,566. These add to about Tk 1,24,343, the same present value as before.

### A second example with messier numbers

> The display case has uneven inflows, so each is discounted by itself.

Now the display case, whose inflows are uneven. No annuity factor can be used, because the inflows differ. Each inflow must be discounted separately. *Given.* The outlay is Tk 1,50,000. The inflows are Tk 40,000, Tk 60,000 and Tk 80,000 in years 1, 2 and 3. The discount rate is 10%. *Find.* The NPV.

:table Net present value of the display case at 10% (Tk)
| Year | Cash inflow | Divide by | Present value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 40,000 | 1.10 | 36,364 |
| 2 | 60,000 | 1.21 | 49,587 |
| 3 | 80,000 | 1.331 | 60,105 |
| Total present value of inflows | | | 1,46,056 |
| Less initial outlay | | | 1,50,000 |
| Net present value | | | −3,944 |

> The inflows are worth Tk 1,46,056 today, which is less than the cost.

The present value of the inflows is Tk 1,46,056, and the outlay is Tk 1,50,000. The NPV is Tk 1,46,056 − Tk 1,50,000 = −Tk 3,944. *Answer.* The NPV is below zero, so the rule says reject. *Check.* The undiscounted inflows add to Tk 1,80,000, which is above the outlay, so a careless addition would say accept. Discounting reverses the verdict, as it should, because the largest sum comes last.

> This project paid back in 2.6 years, yet its NPV is negative.

Notice what has happened. The display case paid back in 2.6 years, which would satisfy an owner with a cut-off of three years. Its NPV is negative all the same. Payback said yes, and NPV said no. The sums after the payback date were too small to cover the cost of waiting.

### The two projects of the last section

> NPV separates D from E, which payback could not do.

Now go back to projects D and E, which payback could not separate. At 10%, project D has a present value of inflows of Tk 84,448 and an NPV of −Tk 15,552. Project E has a present value of inflows of Tk 1,88,763 and an NPV of Tk 88,763. The test that counts all the cash flows sees the difference at once.

### What a zero NPV means

> An NPV of zero means the project earns exactly the discount rate.

Suppose the NPV is exactly zero. The inflows are then worth the same as the outlay, once the waiting is paid for. The project earns exactly the discount rate, and no more. It neither adds wealth nor removes it. The discount rate is the least return the owner will accept, so zero is the dividing line between accept and reject.

> Estimates of cash flows are uncertain, so a small NPV needs care.

One warning belongs here. Every cash flow in the future is an estimate. An NPV that is only a few thousand taka above or below zero can change sign when an estimate changes a little. A large positive or negative NPV is a stronger signal. Chapter VIII begins to measure this uncertainty.

### Where the discount rate comes from

> The discount rate is the return the owner could earn on the money elsewhere.

Every NPV needs a discount rate, and the answer depends on it. What does the rate stand for? It stands for what the money could earn if it were not spent on this project. If the owner could place Tk 3,00,000 in a safe investment that pays 8%, then a project must at least match that return to be worth the trouble. The rate is therefore the price of waiting, as Chapter IV explained.

> The rate should also allow for the risk of the project.

A risky project needs a higher rate than a safe one, because the owner asks for more reward when the result is less sure. A rate that is too low makes weak projects look good. A rate that is too high makes good projects look weak. In this chapter we are given a provisional rate of 10%, so that the method can be learned first. Chapter X shows how risk shapes the return that investors require, and Chapter XV finds one rate for the whole firm.

### Net present value adds up

> The NPV of several projects is the sum of their separate NPVs.

An important property of NPV is that it adds. If the firm accepts two independent projects, the wealth added by the pair is the sum of the two NPVs. The mixer adds Tk 24,343, and a second project that adds Tk 10,000 would bring the pair to Tk 34,343. The payback period and the internal rate of return do not have this property. It is one more reason why NPV matches the goal of raising the owners' wealth.

### Common slips with NPV

> Four slips are common when finding an NPV.

Four slips are common. The first is to discount for the wrong number of years, for example by dividing the year 3 inflow by 1.10 only twice. The second is to read the wrong row or column of the factor table, such as 12% in place of 10%. The third is to add the outlay instead of subtracting it. The fourth is to mix periods: if the rate is 10% a year and the cash flows are half-yearly, then the rate and the number of periods must both be changed, as Chapter IV showed. A check by discounting one year by hand will find most of these slips.

## Accept, Reject and Rank

### Three situations

> The same NPV rule is used in three different situations.

Chapter VI separated projects into kinds. The NPV rule works for all of them, but it is applied a little differently in each. We take the three situations in turn: projects that stand alone, projects that compete, and projects that share a limited sum of money.

> Independent projects are each accepted whenever their NPV is above zero.

If the projects are independent, the decision about one does not affect the others. The firm applies the accept-reject rule to each project, one at a time. Every project with a positive NPV is accepted, because each adds wealth. If the mixer and the display case were independent, the firm would accept the mixer and reject the display case.

> Mutually exclusive projects are ranked, and the highest NPV wins.

If the projects are mutually exclusive, choosing one rules out the other, as with the oven and the van. Here the rule has two stages. First, discard any project with a negative NPV. Then, among those left, choose the one with the highest NPV. A higher NPV means more wealth added, so the highest NPV is the best use of the money.

> A small example shows that the larger outlay is not always the better choice.

An example shows the second stage at work. The firm may buy a small oven or a large oven, and it cannot buy both. The small oven costs Tk 2,00,000 and brings in Tk 90,000 at the end of each of three years. The large oven costs Tk 3,00,000 and brings in Tk 1,30,000 at the end of each of three years. The discount rate is 10%, and the annuity factor for 3 years is 2.486852.

:table A small oven and a large oven at 10% (Tk)
| | Small oven | Large oven |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial outlay | 2,00,000 | 3,00,000 |
| Present value of inflows | 2,23,817 | 3,23,291 |
| Net present value | 23,817 | 23,291 |

> Both NPVs are positive, but the small oven is slightly better.

Both ovens have a positive NPV, so neither is rejected. The small oven has the higher NPV by Tk 526, so it is chosen. The large oven earns more cash, but it also costs Tk 1,00,000 more. The extra Tk 40,000 a year is worth Tk 99,474 today, which is less than the extra Tk 1,00,000 spent. A larger project is not better because it is larger. It is better only if it adds more wealth.

> When money is limited, we choose the group of projects with the largest total NPV.

Capital rationing means that the firm has less money than it has good projects. The firm cannot accept every project with a positive NPV. It must choose the group of projects whose total outlay fits within the money available, and whose total NPV is the largest. We test this with three independent projects and a budget of Tk 4,00,000.

:table Three projects and a budget of Tk 4,00,000 (Tk)
| Project | Outlay | Net present value | NPV for each taka of outlay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R | 1,00,000 | 50,000 | 0.50 |
| S | 2,00,000 | 1,20,000 | 0.60 |
| T | 2,00,000 | 80,000 | 0.40 |

> Only some groups fit the budget, and one group gives the largest total NPV.

The three projects together cost Tk 5,00,000, which is more than the budget. We list the groups that fit. Projects S and T cost Tk 4,00,000 and give a total NPV of Tk 2,00,000. Projects S and R cost Tk 3,00,000 and give Tk 1,70,000. Projects R and T cost Tk 3,00,000 and give Tk 1,30,000. The best group is S and T, with a total NPV of Tk 2,00,000.

> A group should be judged by its total NPV, not by one project at a time.

Notice that project R, taken by itself, has a higher NPV for each taka of outlay than project T. Yet the best group contains T and not R. The reason is that R leaves Tk 1,00,000 unused, and nothing else in the list can use it. The firm should judge the whole group, and not each project alone. If the unused money could be placed elsewhere to earn something, the answer might change.

## The Internal Rate of Return and the Profitability Index

### The internal rate of return

> NPV gives an answer in taka, but some owners prefer an answer in per cent.

NPV gives its answer in taka. Many owners and bankers prefer an answer in per cent, because a rate is easy to compare with the interest on a loan. The next test gives such an answer. It asks a different question: what yearly return does the project itself earn?

@ Internal rate of return | The discount rate at which a project's NPV is exactly zero.

> The internal rate of return is the discount rate that makes NPV zero.

The **internal rate of return**, or IRR, is the discount rate at which a project's NPV is exactly zero. At the IRR, the present value of the inflows equals the outlay. The IRR is therefore the yearly return that the project earns on the money tied up in it. It is called internal because it depends only on the project's own cash flows, and not on any outside rate.

> A simple case shows that IRR is a growth rate.

A simple case shows the idea. Suppose the firm pays Tk 1,00,000 today and receives a single sum of Tk 1,21,000 after two years. Chapter IV taught that Tk 1,00,000 growing at a rate r for two years becomes Tk 1,00,000 × (1 + r)². This equals Tk 1,21,000 when r is 10%, because 1.10 × 1.10 = 1.21. The IRR is therefore 10%.

> For equal inflows, the factor method finds the IRR from the table.

When the inflows are equal, we can find the IRR from the annuity table. For the oven, the outlay is Tk 3,00,000 and the yearly inflow is Tk 1,00,000, for five years. The needed factor is the outlay divided by the yearly inflow, which is 3,00,000 ÷ 1,00,000 = 3.0000. We look along the row for 5 years and find the rate that gives this factor.

> The table row shows that the factor 3.0 lies between 19% and 20%.

In the 5-year row, the factor at 19% is 3.0576 and the factor at 20% is 2.9906. Our factor of 3.0000 lies between them, so the IRR lies between 19% and 20%. It is closer to 20%. A straight-line estimate gives (3.0576 − 3.0000) ÷ (3.0576 − 2.9906) = 0.0576 ÷ 0.0670 = 0.86. The IRR is about 19.9%.

> For uneven inflows, we use trial and error with different rates.

When the inflows are uneven, no table row helps. We try a rate, compute the NPV, and then try another rate. If the NPV is positive, the rate is too low. If it is negative, the rate is too high. For the van, the NPV is about Tk 183 at 18%, and −Tk 15,342 at 20%. The IRR is slightly above 18%. A calculator or spreadsheet gives 18.0%.

@ Hurdle rate | The least return a project must earn to be accepted.

> The IRR is compared with the hurdle rate, which is the least return required.

How does the owner use the IRR? The owner compares it with a **hurdle rate**, which is the least return a project must earn to be accepted. In this chapter the hurdle rate is the same 10% as our discount rate. The IRR rule says: accept the project if its IRR is above the hurdle rate, and reject it if the IRR is below. The oven's IRR of 19.9% is well above 10%, and so is the van's 18.0%. Both are accepted.

### The profitability index

@ Profitability index | The present value of a project's inflows divided by its initial outlay.

> The profitability index shows the present value gained for each taka spent.

The last test is simple. The **profitability index**, or PI, is the present value of a project's inflows divided by its initial outlay. It shows how many taka of present value the project returns for each taka spent. For the oven, PI = Tk 3,79,079 ÷ Tk 3,00,000 = 1.264. For the van, PI = Tk 3,76,766 ÷ Tk 3,00,000 = 1.256.

> The PI rule is equivalent to the NPV rule for accepting or rejecting.

The PI rule says: accept a project if its PI is above 1, and reject it if the PI is below 1. This is the same decision as the NPV rule. The PI is above 1 exactly when the present value of the inflows is above the outlay, and that is exactly when the NPV is positive. The PI is useful when money is limited, because it shows the return for each taka spent.

> A small example shows that the PI follows from the NPV.

Take a counter extension that costs Tk 80,000 and whose inflows have a present value of Tk 1,00,000. Its PI is 1,00,000 ÷ 80,000 = 1.25. Its NPV is Tk 1,00,000 − Tk 80,000 = Tk 20,000, which is positive, as the PI above 1 promised. Each taka spent returns Tk 1.25 of present value.

### Two cautions about the IRR

> The IRR can mislead when projects differ in size.

The IRR is popular, but it needs two cautions. The first concerns size. A high rate on a small sum can add less wealth than a lower rate on a large sum. The next section shows an example. When projects differ in size, the NPV should decide.

> The IRR can also fail when the cash flows change direction more than once.

The second caution concerns the pattern of cash flows. Most projects pay out first and receive afterwards, so the sign of the cash flow changes once, from negative to positive. A project with a large payment in the middle of its life, such as the cost of a major repair, changes sign more than once. Such a project can have more than one IRR, and the rates have no clear meaning. The NPV has no such difficulty. It gives a single answer for a given discount rate.

> One picture shows both projects at every discount rate.

The next figure draws the NPV of the oven and of the van at many discount rates, from 0% to 25%. Each curve falls as the rate rises, because a higher rate shrinks the later sums more. Each curve meets the zero line at its IRR. The picture will help in the next section.

## When the Methods Disagree

### Payback against NPV

> Different tests can give different answers, so we need a way to choose.

The tests do not always give the same answer. A student must know why this happens and which test to trust. We study three kinds of disagreement, each with a small example. In each case the projects are mutually exclusive, and the discount rate is 10%.

> Projects P and Q show payback and NPV giving opposite answers.

First, payback against NPV. Projects P and Q each cost Tk 2,00,000. Project P returns its money fast and then earns little. Project Q returns its money more slowly and then earns a great deal.

:table Projects P and Q at a discount rate of 10% (Tk)
| | Project P | Project Q |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Year 1 | 1,20,000 | 40,000 |
| Year 2 | 1,00,000 | 60,000 |
| Year 3 | 10,000 | 80,000 |
| Year 4 | 10,000 | 1,00,000 |
| Year 5 | 10,000 | 1,20,000 |
| Payback | 1.8 years | 3.2 years |
| Net present value | 12,288 | 88,868 |
| Internal rate of return | 14.0% | 23.3% |

> Payback prefers P, while NPV and IRR both prefer Q.

Payback prefers project P, because it returns the money in 1.8 years. NPV prefers project Q, by a wide margin: Tk 88,868 against Tk 12,288. The IRR also prefers Q. Project P is quick, but it adds little wealth. Project Q is slow, but it adds a great deal. The decision should follow the wealth added, so the firm chooses Q.

### IRR against NPV

> Projects of different size can be ranked differently by IRR and NPV.

Second, IRR against NPV. Project A costs Tk 1,00,000 and returns Tk 60,000 in each of three years. Project B costs Tk 2,00,000 and returns Tk 1,00,000 in each of four years. At 10%, project A has an NPV of Tk 49,211 and an IRR of 36.3%. Project B has an NPV of Tk 1,16,987 and an IRR of 34.9%.

> IRR prefers the smaller project A, but NPV prefers the larger project B.

The two tests now disagree. IRR ranks A first, because its rate is higher. NPV ranks B first, because it adds more taka of wealth. The profitability index agrees with NPV: it is 1.49 for A and 1.58 for B. The IRR hides the fact that B uses Tk 1,00,000 more of the owner's money.

> The extra money in B earns more than the discount rate, so B is better.

We can test the choice directly. Choosing B instead of A means spending an extra Tk 1,00,000 and receiving the extra inflows. The extra NPV is Tk 1,16,987 − Tk 49,211 = Tk 67,776, which is positive. The extra Tk 1,00,000 therefore earns more than 10%. The owner should take B, unless the money has a better use elsewhere.

### A change in the discount rate

> The best project can change when the discount rate changes.

Third, a change of rate. Return to the figure of NPV against the discount rate. At a rate of 0%, the van has the higher NPV: Tk 2,20,000 against Tk 2,00,000. This is just the plain total of the opening puzzle, less the outlay. As the rate rises, the van's curve falls faster, because its larger sums come later. The two curves cross at a rate of about 8.6%.^3

> Above 8.6% the oven has the higher NPV, and below it the van does.

Below 8.6% the van is better. Above 8.6% the oven is better. Our discount rate is 10%, which is above the crossing point, so the oven wins. The ranking depends on the rate, and so the rate must be chosen with care. Chapter XV will find a more careful rate for the bakery and will test the choice again. Because the oven wins at every rate above 8.6%, a modest rise in the rate will not change the choice.

^3: This crossing rate is often called the crossover rate. Some books call it the Fisher rate, after Irving Fisher, whom the section In History names.

### Which test to trust

> NPV is trusted most because it is measured in taka and uses all the cash flows.

When the tests disagree, the firm follows NPV. There are three reasons. NPV counts every cash flow, and not only those before a date. It gives each flow its true value today. And its answer is in taka, so it states how much wealth the project adds, which is what the goal of the firm requires.

> The other tests still have a place as second opinions.

The other tests are not useless. Payback is a quick first filter, and it shows how long the firm waits. IRR is a rate that managers and bankers understand. PI helps when money is limited. A careful firm may compute several tests, but when they disagree, it follows the NPV.

> A table sets the four tests side by side.

The four tests can be set side by side, so that their questions, rules and weaknesses can be compared. The table below states what each test asks, how it decides, and its main weakness.

:table The four tests compared
| Test | Question it answers | Accept if | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Payback period | How soon is the outlay returned? | Payback is within the cut-off | Ignores time value and later flows |
| Net present value | How much wealth is added, in taka today? | NPV is above zero | Needs a discount rate |
| Internal rate of return | What yearly rate does the project earn? | IRR is above the hurdle rate | Can mislead when sizes differ |
| Profitability index | How much present value for each taka spent? | PI is above 1 | Ignores the size of the gain |

> A fixed habit of testing guards against hasty choices.

A good habit is to compute the NPV first, because it decides. Then compute the others as a check. If every test gives the same answer, as with the oven, the owner can decide with confidence. If they differ, the owner looks for the reason, using the three kinds of disagreement in this section as a guide, and follows the NPV.

## The Worked Case: The Oven and the Van

> The case file records the two options and the provisional discount rate.

:case Case file, illustrative. Sundial Bakery, Dhaka. On 1 July 2026 there are two mutually exclusive options, each with an initial outlay of Tk 3,00,000 and a life of five years. Oven: inflows of Tk 1,00,000 in each year. Van: inflows of Tk 50,000, Tk 80,000, Tk 1,10,000, Tk 1,40,000 and Tk 1,40,000 in years 1 to 5. Provisional discount rate: 10% a year. The inflows are the yearly cash flows found in Chapter VI.

> We state what is given and what the owner must decide.

*Given.* The two options above, and a discount rate of 10%. *Find.* The payback, the NPV, the IRR and the PI of each option, and which option to choose. *Formula.* Payback is the time to recover the outlay. NPV is the present value of the inflows minus the outlay. The IRR makes NPV zero. PI is the present value of inflows divided by the outlay.

### Payback

> The oven pays back in three years, and the van takes a little longer.

The oven's inflow is equal in every year, so its payback is Tk 3,00,000 ÷ Tk 1,00,000 = 3.0 years. The van's inflows are uneven, so we keep a running total. After year 3 the van has returned Tk 50,000 + Tk 80,000 + Tk 1,10,000 = Tk 2,40,000. Tk 60,000 of the outlay is still unpaid. Year 4 brings Tk 1,40,000, so the van needs 60,000 ÷ 1,40,000 = 0.43 of that year. The van's payback is about 3.4 years. The figure below shows both.

:fig The running balance of the oven (solid line) and the van (dashed line). Each project pays back where its line crosses zero. | figs/ch7_payback.svg

### Net present value

> The oven's equal inflows allow the annuity factor, and the van needs a table.

For the oven, the annuity factor for 10% and 5 years is 3.790787. The present value of the inflows is Tk 1,00,000 × 3.790787 = Tk 3,79,079. The NPV is Tk 3,79,079 − Tk 3,00,000 = Tk 79,079. The van's inflows are uneven, so each is discounted by itself.

The van's total present value is Tk 3,76,766 and its NPV is Tk 76,766. The lines in the table are rounded to the nearest taka, so they add to Tk 3,76,767. The total is found before rounding. The oven's NPV of Tk 79,079 is higher than the van's NPV of Tk 76,766 by Tk 2,313. Both are positive, so each project would be accepted if it stood alone.

### The other two tests

> The IRR and the PI both favour the oven, though only by a little.

The oven's IRR is about 19.9% and the van's is about 18.0%. Both are above the hurdle rate of 10%. The oven's PI is 3,79,079 ÷ 3,00,000 = 1.264, and the van's PI is 3,76,766 ÷ 3,00,000 = 1.256. The oven ranks first on every test.

### How firm is the choice?

> The choice depends on the discount rate, so we test several rates.

The discount rate of 10% is provisional, so the owner should ask how firm the choice is. We compute the NPV of both options at several rates.

:table Net present value at several discount rates (Tk)
| Discount rate | Oven | Van |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0% | 2,00,000 | 2,20,000 |
| 5% | 1,32,948 | 1,40,076 |

> The oven leads at every rate from 10% upward, so the choice is firm.

At 0% and 5% the van leads, as the plain total suggested. From 10% upward the oven leads, and its lead widens as the rate rises. At 20% both NPVs are below zero, which agrees with the IRRs of 19.9% and 18.0%. The choice of the oven holds for any rate above 8.6%, and the provisional rate of 10% lies above that point.

> The owner chooses the oven, and the opening puzzle is solved.

*Answer.* The firm chooses the oven. It pays back sooner, it has the higher NPV and IRR and PI, and it does so at every discount rate above 8.6%. The opening puzzle is solved: the van brings in Tk 20,000 more in total, but its larger sums come later, and discounting takes that into account. *Check.* The oven's NPV can also be found from the line-by-line values in the first figure of this chapter: 90,909 + 82,645 + 75,131 + 68,301 + 62,092 = Tk 3,79,078, which agrees with Tk 3,79,079 to within one taka of rounding.

## Summary

> The five tests and the decision rule in a few lines.

Payback is the time a project takes to return its outlay. It is quick, but it ignores time value and ignores what follows the payback date. Net present value is the present value of the inflows minus the outlay, and the accept-reject rule says to accept a project when it is above zero. For mutually exclusive projects the firm chooses the highest NPV, and under limited money it chooses the group with the largest total NPV. The internal rate of return is the rate that makes NPV zero, and it is compared with a hurdle rate. The profitability index is the present value of inflows per taka of outlay. When the tests disagree, the firm follows the NPV.

> You should now be able to say what each test does and which to trust.

You should now be able to say: payback tells how soon, NPV tells how much wealth is added, IRR tells the yearly rate earned, and PI tells the return for each taka spent. You should also be able to say why NPV is trusted when the tests disagree.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake: choosing the project with the shortest payback.

*Mistake:* choosing the project that pays back first. *Correction:* payback ignores the time value of money and every sum after the payback date. A fast project can add less wealth than a slow one, as projects P and Q showed.

> Mistake: discounting the outlay along with the inflows.

*Mistake:* discounting the initial outlay. *Correction:* the outlay is paid today, at time zero, so it is already measured in today's taka. Only the later inflows are divided by (1 + r).

> Mistake: confusing present value with net present value.

*Mistake:* calling the present value of the inflows the NPV. *Correction:* NPV is the present value of the inflows minus the outlay. Forgetting the subtraction makes every project look good.

> Mistake: treating a zero NPV as a failure.

*Mistake:* believing that a project with an NPV of zero is a bad project. *Correction:* it earns exactly the discount rate. It adds nothing beyond that, and it is not a loss.

> Mistake: ranking projects of different size by IRR alone.

*Mistake:* choosing the project with the highest IRR when the projects differ in size. *Correction:* a high rate on a small sum can add less wealth than a lower rate on a larger sum. Compare the NPV.

> Mistake: discounting accounting profit in place of cash flow.

*Mistake:* discounting profit figures. *Correction:* the tests of this chapter use cash flows, as found in Chapter VI. Depreciation is not a cash flow, and it enters the cash flows only through its effect on tax.

> Mistake: using a rate and a period that do not match.

*Mistake:* using a yearly rate with half-yearly cash flows. *Correction:* the rate and the period must match. Convert both to half-years, or both to years, as Chapter IV showed.

## In History

> Irving Fisher set out the idea of valuing future money in 1930.

In 1930 the American economist Irving Fisher published *The Theory of Interest*. In it he explained how the value of a stream of future payments can be found by discounting each payment to the present, and he studied the rate of return that a choice of investment offers. These ideas lie behind the NPV and IRR tests used by firms today. Fisher was working on a question of economics, and not on the budgets of firms, but the connection was soon seen by others.

## Sources

> A short note on where the ideas of this chapter come from.

Irving Fisher, *The Theory of Interest* (1930), is the landmark source for the idea of discounting a stream of payments. The accept-reject rule and the other tests of this chapter are standard topics in textbooks of financial management.
