:chapter XIV | Equity, Retained Earnings and the Capital Market
:mood An owner's money asks for no promise, and so it asks for a share of everything.
:desc This chapter explains the money that owners supply: common shares, preferred shares and profit kept inside the firm. It shows how a share is priced when a new investor joins, how the claims of lenders, preferred owners and common owners are ranked, how a firm raises funds in the capital market, and which institutions in Bangladesh supply long-term finance.

> A friend's money buys less of the firm than the friend expects.

Consider a puzzle. The owner of Sundial Bakery put in Tk 6,00,000 in January. An investor now offers Tk 2,80,000 to join the business. The investor reasons: "Together we hold Tk 8,80,000, and I supply Tk 2,80,000, so I should own 31.8 per cent." The owner replies that 25 per cent is fair. Which of them is right, and what does each person overlook?

> The bakery is worth more than the money first put in, because it has earned and kept profit.

The answer lies in what the bakery has done since January. It has earned profit, and the owner has kept most of that profit inside the business. The bakery is therefore worth more than the Tk 6,00,000 that the owner first paid in. The investor counted the money put in, and the owner counted what the business is now worth. This chapter explains the second way of counting, and the other ways in which owners supply money to a firm.

> The chapter has five parts: common shares, preferred shares, kept profit, the capital market and Bangladeshi institutions.

We study five matters. First, the common share, which is the basic form of ownership. Second, the preferred share, which is a form of ownership with a fixed payment. Third, retained earnings, which is profit that owners leave in the firm. Fourth, the capital market, where firms raise long-term money from many people. Fifth, the institutions in Bangladesh that supply long-term finance.

> The chapter relies on Chapters 9, 11 and 13.

*You will need* the leverage ideas of Chapter 9, the share and bond words of Chapter 11, and the debt ideas of Chapter 13: claim, cost, control, risk, and the tax shield on interest.

## 14.1 Common Stock as Ownership

@! Go slowly here.

> Ownership of a firm can be divided into many small equal parts.

Imagine that three friends buy a field together and agree to divide it into ten equal plots. Each friend owns some plots, and a friend who wants to leave can sell plots to someone else. The field is not cut up; the ownership is. A company works in the same way.

> A share is one equal part of a company's ownership.

@ Share | One of the equal parts into which the ownership of a company is divided.

A **share** means one of the equal parts into which the ownership of a company is divided. If a company has 1,000 shares and you hold 10, you own one hundredth of the company. Chapter 2 named the person who holds shares a shareholder. A company may have one shareholder, as the bakery has today, or many thousands.

> Common stock is the ordinary share, held by the owners who carry the final risk.

@ Common stock | The ordinary shares of a company, held by the owners who are paid last and who control the company.

Chapter 11 valued shares as a kind of security. Here we study them as ownership. **Common stock** means the ordinary shares of a company, held by the owners who are paid last and who control the company. Chapter 11 used the words common stock for a security that can be valued. Now the word names the owner's position in the firm. We keep to the word common stock for these ordinary shares, as in Chapter 11.

> A share has a printed value, called its face value, which is not its worth.

Each share has a **face value**, which is the value printed on the share. The bakery's shares have a face value of Tk 10. Chapter 11 used the same words for the sum that a bond repays at maturity. A share is different: its face value is a label used in the company's records, and it does not say what the share is worth today.^1

> Limited liability means a shareholder can lose only the money paid for the shares.

@ Limited liability | A shareholder can lose no more than the amount paid for the shares.

A shareholder holds a safe position in one respect. **Limited liability** means a shareholder can lose no more than the amount paid for the shares. Suppose the bakery fails and owes lenders Tk 20,00,000, but its assets sell for only Tk 12,00,000. The shareholders lose their shares, but the lenders cannot demand the missing Tk 8,00,000 from the shareholders' own homes or savings. This protection explains why many people are willing to buy shares in firms they do not manage.

> Common owners have three rights: to vote, to share profit and to share what remains.

Common stock gives its holders three main rights. The first is a vote on important matters, such as who will be the directors. The second is a share of any profit that the directors decide to pay out as dividend. The third is a claim on what is left if the firm closes, after every debt has been paid. The vote has force only in proportion to the shares held: an owner of 75 per cent has three times the votes of an owner of 25 per cent.

> The common owner's claim is the last in line, so it is called the residual claim.

@ Residual claim | The right to what remains after every other claim has been paid.

The last two rights have a common feature. They are the **residual claim**, which means the right to what remains after every other claim has been paid. A lender is promised a fixed sum. A common owner is promised nothing, and receives whatever is left. When the firm does well, what is left is large, and the owners gain. When the firm does badly, what is left may be nothing.

> A table compares a lender and a common owner on the four questions of Chapter 13.

The next table applies the four questions of Chapter 13 to a common owner. Read each row for one question, and compare the two columns.

:table A lender and a common owner compared
| Question | Lender | Common owner |
| Claim | A fixed sum on agreed dates | The residual: whatever is left |
| Cost to the firm | Interest, fixed in advance | Dividends, which the directors decide |
| Control | None over daily decisions | A vote in proportion to shares held |
| Risk to the firm | High: fixed charges must be paid | Low: no payment is compulsory |

> Common stock is the safest money for the firm, and the riskiest for the owner.

The table shows a balance between the firm and the owner. For the firm, common stock is the safest money, because no payment is compulsory. For the owner, it is the riskiest money, because the owner is paid last. For this reason, owners expect a higher return than lenders, a point that Chapter 15 will use.

> A new investor must be given the right number of shares for the money paid.

@ Equity per share | Total owners' equity divided by the number of shares.

Now we return to the puzzle. When a new investor joins, the firm must decide how many shares to give for the money. The decision depends on what each existing share is worth. A simple starting point uses the owners' equity: the owners' total claim, which is the money they put in plus profit kept in the business. In words: **equity per share** equals total owners' equity, divided by the number of shares. We use it as a fair starting price, and the two sides may bargain from there.^2

> First worked example: the bakery's share price and the investor's shares.

*Given.* The owner holds 60,000 shares of face value Tk 10, which is Tk 6,00,000. Profit kept in the business is Tk 2,40,000 on 30 September 2026. The investor offers Tk 2,80,000. *Find.* The price of a share, the number of new shares, and the investor's share of the company.

:table The investor's shares, worked in steps
| Step | Working | Result |
| Owners' equity before | 6,00,000 + 2,40,000 | Tk 8,40,000 |
| Equity per share | 8,40,000 / 60,000 | Tk 14 |
| New shares | 2,80,000 / 14 | 20,000 |
| Shares after the issue | 60,000 + 20,000 | 80,000 |
| Investor's part | 20,000 / 80,000 | 25 per cent |

> The investor owns 25 per cent, as the owner said.

*Answer.* The price is Tk 14 a share. The investor receives 20,000 shares and owns 25 per cent of the bakery. *Check.* Owners' equity after the issue is Tk 8,40,000 plus Tk 2,80,000, which is Tk 11,20,000. Divided by 80,000 shares, it is Tk 14 again. Neither side gained or lost, because the new money entered at the price of the old shares.

> The investor's first count failed because the old owner's equity includes kept profit.

The investor counted only the money put in, which gives 2,80,000 divided by 8,80,000, or 31.8 per cent. That count ignores Tk 2,40,000 of profit that belongs to the owner and stays in the firm. The investor must pay for a share of that profit as well. Each of the two persons held part of the truth: the owner knew what the business is worth, and the investor knew the money offered.

> A second example shows what happens when new shares are sold too cheaply.

Now a harder test. Suppose the firm sells the 20,000 new shares at their face value of Tk 10, so that the investor pays Tk 2,00,000. The investor still owns 25 per cent. But owners' equity after the issue is Tk 8,40,000 plus Tk 2,00,000, which is Tk 10,40,000, and 25 per cent of that is Tk 2,60,000. The investor pays Tk 2,00,000 and receives a share worth Tk 2,60,000, a gain of Tk 60,000.

> The owner loses what the investor gains, and this loss is called dilution.

@ Dilution | The fall in the value of existing shares when new shares are sold for less than they are worth.

The gain comes from the old owner. Before the issue, the owner's claim was Tk 8,40,000. After it, the owner holds 75 per cent of Tk 10,40,000, which is Tk 7,80,000, a loss of Tk 60,000. This loss is called **dilution**, which means the fall in the value of existing shares when new shares are sold for less than they are worth. The owner should always ask what price the new shares are sold at, and not only how many.

> Common slips in share arithmetic.

*Common slips.* Students divide the new money by the money already put in, and not by the owners' equity. They use face value as the price. They forget that the number of shares after the issue is the old number plus the new number. After every issue, check that the equity per share is unchanged when the price equals the old equity per share.

> Recap: common stock is ownership with votes and a residual claim; price decides fairness.

*Before moving on.* A share is one equal part of ownership. Common owners vote, share profit and hold the residual claim, and limited liability protects them. The issue price decides whether old owners are treated fairly. You should now be able to say why an investor who pays 31.8 per cent of the total money receives only 25 per cent of the shares.

## 14.2 Preferred Stock

> Some investors want a steady payment and little control.

Not every investor wants the risks of a common owner. A retired teacher with savings may wish for a steady yearly payment, and may not care to vote on the price of buns. A firm may also wish to raise money without adding to its fixed charges or to the number of votes. Both wishes lead to a second kind of share.

> Preferred stock pays a fixed dividend before common owners are paid.

@ Preferred stock | Shares that pay a fixed dividend, which must be paid before any common dividend.

**Preferred stock** means shares that pay a fixed dividend, which must be paid before any common dividend. The dividend is a fixed percentage of the face value. A preferred share of face value Tk 100 with a rate of 10 per cent pays Tk 10 a year. Chapter 3 gave a one-line preview of the preferred dividend; now we study it fully.

> A preferred owner is ranked after lenders and before common owners.

The word preferred names a position in a line. When the firm earns profit, it pays in a fixed order: lenders first, then preferred owners, then common owners. The same order holds if the firm closes and sells its assets. Preferred owners therefore carry less risk than common owners, and more than lenders. The next figure draws this ranking for one year's profit. Read it from the top to the bottom: each box is a claim, and the arrow shows the order in which the claims are met.

:fig The order in which a firm's profit is shared: interest to lenders, then tax, then the preferred dividend, and last the common owners, who may retain what is left. | figures/fig14_order.svg

> The preferred owner usually gives up the vote.

The fixed dividend has a price for the owner. A preferred owner usually has no vote, except in special cases, for example when dividends have not been paid for some time. Preferred owners accept a limited reward and little control. In return they have priority.^3

> A missed preferred dividend is not a default, but it has consequences.

@ Cumulative | A preferred share is cumulative if unpaid dividends are carried forward and paid before any common dividend.

Suppose the firm cannot pay the preferred dividend in a poor year. Unlike unpaid interest, this is not a default, and the preferred owners cannot force the firm to close. But the firm cannot pay any dividend to common owners while the preferred dividend is unpaid. A preferred share is **cumulative** if unpaid dividends are carried forward and must be paid before any common dividend. A share that does not carry them forward is non-cumulative.

> First worked example: how a cumulative dividend takes profit from common owners.

*Given.* Suppose the investor of section 14.1 took preferred shares instead: 2,800 shares of face value Tk 100, which is Tk 2,80,000, at 10 per cent a year. The firm paid no dividend last year. This year Tk 50,000 is available for dividends. *Find.* How the Tk 50,000 is shared if the shares are cumulative, and if they are non-cumulative.

:table Sharing Tk 50,000 between preferred and common owners
| Step | Cumulative | Non-cumulative |
| Yearly preferred dividend: 10% of 2,80,000 | 28,000 | 28,000 |
| Unpaid from last year | 28,000 | Lost |
| Preferred claim this year | 56,000 | 28,000 |
| Paid to preferred owners | 50,000 | 28,000 |
| Paid to common owners | 0 | 22,000 |
| Still unpaid to preferred owners | 6,000 | 0 |

> The cumulative holders take all Tk 50,000; the others take Tk 28,000.

*Answer.* If the shares are cumulative, the preferred owners receive the whole Tk 50,000, the common owners receive nothing, and Tk 6,000 is still owed. If they are non-cumulative, the preferred owners receive Tk 28,000 and the common owners receive Tk 22,000. *Check.* In each column, the two payments add to Tk 50,000. The extra protection costs the common owners Tk 22,000 this year.

> A second example compares a preferred dividend with interest on the same sum.

Now the main difference between preferred stock and debt. Interest is subtracted from profit before tax is calculated, as in Chapter 13. A preferred dividend is paid out of profit after tax. The next table gives the bakery a profit before interest and tax of Tk 1,00,000 and a tax rate of 25 per cent. In the first column the bakery pays Tk 28,000 as interest. In the second it pays Tk 28,000 as preferred dividend. Read down each column.

:table The same Tk 28,000 paid as interest and as a preferred dividend
| Line | Interest of 28,000 | Preferred dividend of 28,000 |
| Profit before interest and tax | 1,00,000 | 1,00,000 |
| Interest | 28,000 | 0 |
| Profit before tax | 72,000 | 1,00,000 |
| Tax at 25 per cent | 18,000 | 25,000 |
| Profit after tax | 54,000 | 75,000 |
| Preferred dividend | 0 | 28,000 |
| Left for common owners | 54,000 | 47,000 |

> The preferred dividend costs Tk 7,000 more, because it has no tax shield.

*Answer.* The common owners keep Tk 54,000 in the first case and Tk 47,000 in the second. The difference is Tk 7,000, which is 25 per cent of Tk 28,000: the tax shield that interest gains and a preferred dividend does not. *Check.* Tax in the second column is Tk 7,000 higher: Tk 25,000 less Tk 18,000. The cost of a fixed payment to the firm is therefore higher for preferred stock than for debt, a point that Chapter 15 will use.

> A table sets the three kinds of money side by side.

The next table brings together the three kinds of money. Read each row for one feature, and compare the three columns.

:table Debt, preferred stock and common stock compared
| Feature | Debt | Preferred stock | Common stock |
| Holder is | A lender | An owner | An owner |
| Payment | Fixed interest | Fixed dividend | Dividend decided by directors |
| Paid | First | Second | Last |
| Missed payment | Default | Carried forward if cumulative | No claim |
| Vote | None | Usually none | Yes |
| Tax shield | Yes | No | No |

> Many small firms never issue preferred stock, because the arrangement is complex.

There are limits to preferred stock. It adds a fixed payment, so it raises the firm's financial risk, as Chapter 9 showed with leverage. It has no tax shield. And its rules vary between companies, so each issue must be read carefully. For these reasons, many firms prefer a mix of debt and common stock.

> Recap: preferred stock is owners' money with a fixed dividend and priority.

*Before moving on.* Preferred stock pays a fixed dividend before common owners, usually with no vote. Cumulative shares carry unpaid dividends forward. The dividend has no tax shield. You should now be able to say why a preferred dividend costs a firm more than interest of the same size.

## 14.3 Retained Earnings

> The simplest source of owners' money is profit left in the firm.

A family business earns Tk 3,20,000 in a year after tax. The family may take all of it for household needs, or leave some in the business to buy a new oven. The second choice is a source of money that needs no bank, no new owner and no form to sign.

> Retained earnings are the profit after tax that is kept in the firm.

@ Retained earnings | The part of profit after tax that is kept in the firm and not paid out as dividend.

**Retained earnings** means the part of profit after tax that is kept in the firm and not paid out as dividend. Retained earnings belong to the common owners, so they are part of owners' equity. When a firm retains profit, the owners agree, in effect, to lend their share of the profit to their own business and take the reward later, as a larger firm.

> The dividend decision divides profit into a part paid and a part kept.

@ Payout ratio | Dividends divided by net profit; the part of profit paid to owners.

The directors decide how to divide the profit between dividend and retained earnings. We measure the division with a ratio. In words: the **payout ratio** equals dividends divided by net profit. The retention ratio equals retained earnings divided by net profit, which is one less the payout ratio.

> Worked example: the bakery's profit in 2026.

*Given.* Net profit for 2026 is Tk 3,20,000: Tk 2,40,000 for the first nine months and Tk 80,000 for the last three. The directors pay a dividend of Tk 0.80 on each of 80,000 shares. For simplicity, all shares receive the same amount. *Find.* The dividend, the payout and retention ratios, the retained earnings, and owners' equity at 31 December.

:table The bakery's profit shared, 2026
| Line | Working | Tk |
| Net profit | 2,40,000 + 80,000 | 3,20,000 |
| Dividend | 0.80 × 80,000 | 64,000 |
| Retained earnings | 3,20,000 - 64,000 | 2,56,000 |
| Payout ratio | 64,000 / 3,20,000 | 20 per cent |
| Retention ratio | 2,56,000 / 3,20,000 | 80 per cent |

> Owners' equity is the owner's money, the investor's money and kept profit, held in three parts.

The bakery began the year with no profit kept, so the kept profit at 31 December is Tk 2,56,000. The case file keeps the owner's money and the investor's money apart. The owner's Tk 6,00,000 buys 60,000 shares. The investor's Tk 2,80,000 buys 20,000 shares: Tk 2,00,000 at face value, and Tk 80,000 more because each share was sold at Tk 14.^4 Owners' equity is the sum of the three parts. The figure below shows them as one bar. Each block is one part, and its length is its size in taka.

:fig Owners' equity of Sundial Bakery at 31 December 2026: the owner's capital, the investor's capital and retained earnings. | figures/fig14_equity.svg

> Owners' equity at 31 December is Tk 11,36,000, or Tk 14.20 a share.

*Answer.* Owners' equity at 31 December is 6,00,000 plus 2,80,000 plus 2,56,000, which is Tk 11,36,000. Divided by 80,000 shares, it is Tk 14.20 a share, which is Tk 0.20 higher than at the issue, because of the profit kept since then. *Check.* 3,20,000 less 64,000 is 2,56,000, and the kept profit in the figure matches.

> Retained earnings are not a pile of cash.

A common error needs attention. Retained earnings are a claim of the owners, not a sum of money in a drawer. The firm has already used the kept profit: it buys flour, fills the shop with stock, and waits for customers to pay. Suppose that at 31 December only Tk 40,000 of the Tk 2,56,000 is held as cash. The rest is in goods, equipment and amounts owed by customers. A firm can have large retained earnings and little cash, as Chapter 12 showed.

> Retained earnings avoid issue costs and new owners, but they are not free.

Retained earnings have advantages. They cost nothing to arrange, they add no new owner who must share control, and they create no fixed payment. They also have limits. They are no larger than the profit the firm has made, and a young firm has little. And they are not free of cost: the owners could have taken the money as dividend and used it elsewhere. Chapter 15 treats this lost use as the cost of retained earnings.

> Owners may differ about the best dividend.

There is also a difference of view. An owner who needs income wants a high dividend. An owner who wishes the firm to grow wants a low one. A firm with a good use for the money, such as a profitable oven, can reasonably keep more. A firm with no good use for it should return it to owners. This is the dividend decision that Chapter 2 named.

> Recap: retained earnings are kept profit; the payout ratio shows the division.

*Before moving on.* Retained earnings are profit after tax kept in the firm. The payout ratio plus the retention ratio is one. Kept profit is a claim of the owners and is not itself cash. You should now be able to find retained earnings from profit and dividend.

## 14.4 Raising Funds from the Capital Market

> The bakery found its investor by asking; larger firms need many investors.

The bakery found its investor through an introduction. A firm that wishes to raise Tk 10,00,00,000 cannot depend on introductions. It needs a way to reach many people with savings, and those people need a way to sell their shares if they later need money. The system that serves both needs is called the capital market.

> The capital market is where long-term money is raised and traded.

@ Capital market | The financial market in which long-term funds are raised and securities are traded.

The **capital market** means the financial market in which long-term funds are raised and securities are traded. It is one part of the financial markets that Chapter 1 described. The market for short-term money, such as an overdraft, is a different part. It has two sides, which we must keep apart.

> The primary market sells new securities, and the secondary market trades existing ones.

@ Primary market | The market in which a firm sells new securities and receives the money.

@ Secondary market | The market in which investors trade securities that already exist.

The **primary market** means the market in which a firm sells new securities and receives the money. The **secondary market** means the market in which investors trade securities that already exist. When the bakery's investor paid Tk 2,80,000 to the bakery, that was a primary sale. If the investor later sells the shares to a friend, the sale is secondary, and the bakery receives nothing. The figure below shows the two flows. Follow the arrows: in the primary market, money goes to the firm; in the secondary market, money moves between investors.

:fig The two sides of the capital market: in the primary market the firm receives the money, and in the secondary market one investor pays another. | figures/fig14_market.svg

> The secondary market matters to the firm, although the firm receives no money from it.

Why should a firm care about the secondary market, when it receives nothing there? The answer is confidence. An investor who knows the shares can be sold later is more willing to buy them now. A firm whose shares trade easily can usually raise new money more cheaply.

> A share issue is the sale of new shares to raise money.

@ Share issue | The sale of new shares by a company to raise money.

A **share issue** means the sale of new shares by a company to raise money. A firm has three common ways to make an issue. The first is a private placement, in which the firm sells to one investor or a few chosen ones, as the bakery did. The second is a public offering, in which the firm sells to the public at large. The third is a rights issue, in which the firm offers new shares first to its existing shareholders.

> Three methods of share issue differ in who buys and how much they cost.

@ Public offering | The sale of new shares to the public at large.

@ Rights issue | An offer of new shares to existing shareholders, in proportion to the shares they hold.

In a **public offering**, the firm publishes a document, called a prospectus, that describes the business, its risks and its accounts. Investors read it and apply for shares. A **rights issue** is an offer of new shares to existing shareholders, in proportion to the shares they hold. The next table sets the three methods side by side. Read each row for one method.

:table Three ways to issue shares
| Method | Who buys | Cost and effort | Fits the bakery when |
| Private placement | One investor or a few chosen ones | Low: a private agreement | The firm needs Tk 2,80,000 from one person |
| Public offering | The public at large | High: prospectus, advisers, approval | The firm is large and wants many owners |
| Rights issue | Existing shareholders | Medium: an offer to each holder | The owners wish to keep their shares in proportion |

> A worked example shows how a rights issue protects each owner's share.

*Given.* Suppose that, after the issue of 14.1, the bakery offers one new share for every four held, at Tk 14. The bakery has 80,000 shares: 60,000 held by the owner, 20,000 by the investor. *Find.* The number of new shares, the money raised, and each holder's cost and part of the company if each takes up the offer in full.

> The offer raises Tk 2,80,000 and leaves each holder's part unchanged.

*Steps.* New shares are 80,000 divided by 4, which is 20,000. Money raised is 20,000 times Tk 14, which is Tk 2,80,000. The owner may buy 60,000 divided by 4, or 15,000 shares, costing Tk 2,10,000. The investor may buy 5,000 shares, costing Tk 70,000. *Answer.* After the offer the owner holds 75,000 of 1,00,000 shares, which is 75 per cent, and the investor holds 25,000, which is 25 per cent. *Check.* The two purchases add to 20,000 shares and Tk 2,80,000. A holder who declines the offer ends with a smaller part of the company.

> Every issue has costs, so the firm receives less than investors pay.

An issue is not free. The firm pays lawyers, auditors, advisers and fees. Suppose the costs of a Tk 2,80,000 issue are 2 per cent. They are Tk 5,600, and the firm receives Tk 2,74,400. The cost of the issue is part of the cost of the money, and it is larger for small issues than for large ones.

> A stock exchange is an organised market where listed shares are traded.

@ Stock exchange | An organised market in which securities are bought and sold under fixed rules.

@ Listing | The admission of a company's shares to trading on a stock exchange.

A **stock exchange** means an organised market in which securities are bought and sold under fixed rules. A company that wishes its shares to be traded there seeks a **listing**, which means the admission of its shares to trading on the exchange. Only listed shares are traded. In Bangladesh the main exchanges are the Dhaka Stock Exchange and the Chittagong Stock Exchange.

> Listing brings money and easy resale, but also duties.

Why would a firm seek a listing? It gains access to many investors, its shares can be sold easily, and its name becomes better known. But it accepts duties. It must publish accounts, follow the exchange's rules, and tell investors promptly about important news. Its owners lose some privacy, and its managers must answer to many shareholders. Chapter 2 named this relation between owners and managers as the agency problem.

> A regulator makes rules for the market and watches that they are kept.

@ Regulator | A public body that makes rules for a market and checks that they are followed.

A market in which ordinary people trade needs a referee. A **regulator** means a public body that makes rules for a market and checks that they are followed. In Bangladesh, the Bangladesh Securities and Exchange Commission, known as BSEC, regulates the securities market, and a company that offers shares to the public must follow its rules. The central bank, Bangladesh Bank, regulates banks and certain other financial institutions. The rules, fees and conditions change, so any student who plans an issue must read the current rules.

> Recap: the primary market raises money, the secondary market trades; issue methods differ.

*Before moving on.* The primary market sells new securities, and the secondary market trades existing ones. A firm may issue shares by private placement, public offering or rights issue. A listing brings access and resale but also duties. You should now be able to say who receives the money when shares are sold on a stock exchange.

## 14.5 Institutions Supplying Long-Term Finance in Bangladesh

> A firm in Bangladesh has several kinds of institution to approach.

A baker who needs a large sum for many years has more than one door to try. Each kind of institution has its own purpose, size and rules. This section describes the main kinds, with a few examples. All facts here are illustrative of the kinds of institution, and the current details must be checked before any use.

> Commercial banks are the most common source of long-term loans for small firms.

The first kind is the commercial bank. Banks lend for the term loans of Chapter 13, and they are the usual choice for a small firm. A bank also holds the firm's accounts, so it can see how the firm's money moves. For the bakery's oven loan, a bank is the natural lender.

> A development finance institution lends for the long term to build industry and infrastructure.

@ Development finance institution | A body that supplies long-term funds to build industry or infrastructure, often with public backing.

A **development finance institution** means a body that supplies long-term funds to build industry or infrastructure, often with public backing. Its aim is not only profit but also growth of the economy. Such institutions lend for longer periods than many commercial banks, and often for projects that private lenders find too large or too slow to repay.

> IDCOL is an example that finances large infrastructure and renewable energy projects.

One example is the Infrastructure Development Company Limited, known as IDCOL. The Government of Bangladesh established it on 14 May 1997, and the Bangladesh Bank licensed it as a non-bank financial institution in January 1998. It finances medium and large projects in infrastructure and renewable energy. These projects are far larger than the bakery's oven, so IDCOL is not a source for the bakery.

> ICB is an investment bank that supports industry and the securities market.

A second example is the Investment Corporation of Bangladesh, known as ICB. It was established on 1 October 1976 as an investment bank, to speed up industrial growth and to build a sound securities market. Investment banks help firms to issue shares and bonds, and may themselves invest in them. A firm planning a public offering usually seeks advisers of this kind.

> Non-bank financial institutions lend and lease without taking deposits from the public.

@ Non-bank financial institution | A financial firm that lends, leases or invests but is not a bank.

A third kind is the **non-bank financial institution**, which means a financial firm that lends, leases or invests but is not a bank. Bangladesh Bank regulates them under the Financial Institutions Act of 1993. Leasing companies and investment companies belong here. ## The Worked Case: The Investor Joins

> The situation: the bakery has one owner, kept profit and a loan.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* *Situation.* On 1 October 2026 the bakery's ownership is divided into 60,000 shares of face value Tk 10, all held by the owner, who paid Tk 6,00,000. Profit kept in the firm is Tk 2,40,000. The bakery owes Tk 7,00,000 to banks, as in Chapter 9. An investor offers Tk 2,80,000.

> The question: how many shares, at what price, and with what effect on risk?

:case *Question.* How many shares should the investor receive, at what price, and how does the new money change the bakery's debt in relation to owners' money?

> The reasoning uses equity per share and the debt ratio.

:case *Reasoning.* Equity per share is Tk 8,40,000 divided by 60,000, which is Tk 14. The investor's Tk 2,80,000 buys 20,000 shares. The bakery's debt is Tk 7,00,000. Before the issue, that is Tk 0.83 of debt for each taka of owners' equity. After the issue, equity is Tk 11,20,000, and the figure is Tk 0.63.

> Conclusion: 20,000 shares at Tk 14; the investor owns 25 per cent; debt weighs less.

:case *Conclusion.* The investor receives 20,000 shares at Tk 14 and owns 25 per cent of the bakery. The new equity lowers the debt carried by each taka of owners' money, so financial leverage, as Chapter 9 defined it, falls. The owner's part falls from 100 per cent to 75 per cent, and the owner shares control and profit.

> The check repeats the arithmetic and tests the price.

:case *Check.* Equity per share after the issue is 11,20,000 divided by 80,000, which is Tk 14, so neither side gained. Debt over equity is 7,00,000 divided by 8,40,000, which is 0.83, and 7,00,000 divided by 11,20,000, which is 0.63. At 31 December, equity is Tk 11,36,000 and equity per share is Tk 14.20.

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas in one paragraph.

A share is one equal part of a company's ownership. Common owners vote, share profit and hold the residual claim, and limited liability protects them. The issue price decides whether old owners are treated fairly: a price below equity per share dilutes them. Preferred stock pays a fixed dividend before common owners are paid, but it has no tax shield, and cumulative preferred shares carry unpaid dividends forward. Retained earnings are profit after tax that the firm keeps; they are a claim of owners, not a sum of cash, and they are not free. The primary market sells new securities, and the secondary market trades existing ones. A firm may issue shares by private placement, public offering or rights issue, and a listing brings access and duties. In Bangladesh, long-term funds come from commercial banks, development finance institutions, investment banks, non-bank financial institutions and the stock exchanges.

## Where People Go Wrong

> A share's face value is not its worth.

*A share of face value Tk 10 is worth Tk 10.* It is not. Face value is a printed label. The worth of a share depends on the firm's equity and its earnings. The bakery's shares of face value Tk 10 are priced at Tk 14.

> Owners' part follows equity, not only money put in.

*An investor who pays 31.8 per cent of the money owns 31.8 per cent of the firm.* Not so. The firm is worth more than the money first put in, because it holds kept profit. The investor owns 25 per cent.

> Preferred stock is not debt.

*A preferred share is a loan.* It is not. A preferred owner is an owner with a fixed dividend and priority. A missed dividend is not a default, and the dividend has no tax shield.

> Kept profit is not cash.

*Retained earnings are money in the bank.* They are a claim of the owners. The firm has already used most of that profit in its assets.

> Trading on the exchange does not bring money to the firm.

*When shares are sold on the stock exchange, the firm receives the money.* It does not. In the secondary market, one investor pays another. The firm receives money only when it sells new shares.

> Owners' money is not free.

*Retained earnings and new shares cost nothing, because there is no interest.* They have a cost: owners expect a return, and Chapter 15 shows how to measure it.

## In History

> A refusal in Calcutta led to a stock exchange in Dhaka.

In 1952, the stock exchange in Calcutta stopped trading in the shares of companies in Pakistan. East Pakistan, whose capital was Dhaka, then had no market of its own for the shares of its firms. A group of eight promoters incorporated the East Pakistan Stock Exchange Association Limited on 28 April 1954, and formal trading began in 1956. The exchange was renamed the Dacca Stock Exchange in 1964, and the spelling later became Dhaka. The lesson for this chapter is plain: a firm that wants to raise owners' money from many people needs a place where those people can later sell, and a country that wants such firms must build that place.
