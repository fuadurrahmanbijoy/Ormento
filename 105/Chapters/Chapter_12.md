:chapter XII | Working Capital and Short-Term Financing
:mood A profitable bakery can still run short of cash on a Thursday.
:desc This chapter explains the money that moves through a firm in its daily work: cash, goods waiting to be sold, and bills not yet paid. It shows how to measure the days that money stays tied up, how to match short needs with short funds, and how to judge whether paying a supplier early is worth the cost.

> A firm can earn profit and still lack the cash to pay one bill.

Consider a puzzle that troubles many young firms. A bakery sells well all month. Its accounts, shown in the layout of Chapter 3, show a profit. Yet on Thursday morning the owner cannot pay the flour supplier. How can a firm that earns profit lack the money to pay one bill?

> The answer is timing: profit and cash arrive on different days.

The answer lies in timing. In Chapter 1 we learned that profit is what remains of sales after costs, while cash flow is money actually moving in or out. Profit counts a sale on the day it is made. Cash counts the sale on the day the customer pays. If a customer takes thirty days to pay, the bakery has already paid for flour, wages and gas before the money returns. The profit exists in the accounts, but the money is still with the customer.

> This chapter studies the daily movement of money through a firm.

This chapter studies that daily movement of money. We ask what a firm must hold to keep working, how long its money stays tied up, where short-term money can be found, and what it costs. We begin with the bakery's position on one day.

> A case-file snapshot of the bakery's day-to-day money.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 10 September 2026 Sundial Bakery holds cash of Tk 18,000. Catering customers owe it Tk 1,20,000 for orders already delivered. It holds flour and other ingredients worth Tk 40,000. It owes the flour supplier Tk 50,000 and owes a further Tk 30,000 for wages and gas bills. The supplier's invoice carries the terms 2/10, net 40, explained in section 12.6. The bakery cannot pay the Tk 50,000 from its cash, although its customers owe it more than twice that sum.

> The chapter relies on percentages and on interest on interest.

*You will need* percentages from Chapter 3 and the idea of interest on interest from Chapter 4. Chapter 2 named working capital in one line. Here we study it fully.

## 12.1 What Working Capital Is

@! Go slowly here.

> A household's monthly routine is a small model of a firm's daily money.

Think first of a household. A family is paid on the first day of the month. It buys food, pays the electricity bill on the tenth day and the school fee on the twentieth. Money in the wallet, food in the kitchen and bills not yet paid all belong to this monthly routine. The family does not plan to keep any of them for years. They are used and replaced, again and again. A firm has the same routine, with larger sums.

> A current asset is something owned that will be used or turned into cash within a year.

@ Current asset | Something the firm owns that it expects to use or turn into cash within one year.

A **current asset** means something the firm owns that it expects to use or turn into cash within one year. Cash in the till is one example. Flour in the store room is another. A bakery has three main kinds of current asset, and we use each one throughout the chapter.

> Receivable: money a customer owes for goods already delivered.

@ Receivable | Money a customer owes the firm for goods or services already delivered.

The first kind is the **receivable**. A receivable means money a customer owes the firm for goods already delivered. When an office orders cakes for a meeting and promises to pay in thirty days, the bakery holds a receivable until the money arrives. The bakery has done its work, but it does not yet hold the cash.

> Inventory: goods held for use or sale.

@ Inventory | Goods that a firm holds for use or for sale.

The second kind is **inventory**. Inventory means goods that a firm holds for use or for sale. For the bakery it is flour, sugar, butter and the buns waiting on the shelf. Some writers say stock for inventory. This book uses inventory, because Chapter 11 gave the word stock another meaning, the shares of a company.^1

> Cash is the third and most useful current asset.

The third kind is cash itself, in the till and in the bank. Cash is the only current asset that can pay a bill today. A receivable must first be collected, and inventory must first be sold and then collected.

> A current liability is a debt due within one year.

@ Current liability | An amount the firm owes that it must pay within one year.

The firm also owes money in the short term. A **current liability** means an amount the firm owes that it must pay within one year. The most common is the **payable**, which means money the firm owes a supplier for goods already received. Wages not yet paid and the gas bill are also current liabilities.

> Working capital is the money tied up in cash, receivables and inventory.

@ Working capital | Money tied up in a firm's daily work, held as cash, receivables and inventory.

@ Capital | In daily life, a city or a large letter. In finance, money put into a business.

We can now name the whole idea. **Working capital** means the money tied up in a firm's current assets: cash, receivables and inventory. It is called working capital because the money works every day. It is bought as flour, turned into buns, sold, and collected again as cash. Then the circle begins once more.

> Net working capital is current assets less current liabilities.

@ Net working capital | Current assets less current liabilities.

Most firms owe money in the short term as well, so one more measure is useful. **Net working capital** means current assets less current liabilities. It shows how much of the firm's daily money belongs to the firm after the short-term debts are paid. The next table shows the bakery on 10 September, with each item explained in turn.

:table The bakery's day-to-day money, 10 September 2026
| Item | Tk |
| Cash | 18,000 |
| Receivables (catering) | 1,20,000 |
| Inventory | 40,000 |
| Current assets | 1,78,000 |
| Payable to the flour supplier | 50,000 |
| Wages and gas bills due | 30,000 |
| Current liabilities | 80,000 |
| Net working capital | 98,000 |

> Net working capital is positive, yet cash is short.

Read the table from the top. The three current assets add to Tk 1,78,000. The two current liabilities add to Tk 80,000. Net working capital is therefore Tk 1,78,000 less Tk 80,000, which is Tk 98,000. The figure is positive, so the bakery owns more in the short term than it owes. Yet the puzzle remains: the bakery cannot pay the Tk 50,000 invoice today.

> Most of the net working capital is not cash.

The puzzle has a plain answer. Of the Tk 98,000, only Tk 18,000 is cash. The rest is money in customers' hands and flour on the shelf. A positive net working capital tells us the firm is sound in the short term. It does not tell us whether the firm can pay a bill this morning. For that, we must also know how quickly the other assets turn into cash.

> Too little working capital risks unpaid bills; too much leaves money idle.

There are limits on both sides. A firm with too little working capital may fail to pay wages or suppliers on time. A firm with too much has money lying idle in unsold goods and slow customers. That money could have bought a second oven. The owner and the supplier see the matter differently. The owner wants little money tied up. The supplier wants a customer who always has cash to pay.

> Recap: working capital is cash, receivables and inventory, less short-term debts.

*Before moving on.* Working capital is the money tied up in cash, receivables and inventory. Net working capital subtracts the short-term debts. You should now be able to say why a firm with positive net working capital can still be unable to pay a bill today.

## 12.2 Financing by Time

> People borrow for different lengths of time for different purposes.

Return to the household. A family borrows a little money from a neighbour for a week to buy medicine. It borrows a large sum from a bank for twenty years to buy a house. Nobody would borrow for twenty years to buy lunch. The length of the loan follows the length of the need. Firms reason in the same way.

> Short, medium and long are fixed by the number of years.

@ Short-term | Lasting up to one year.

@ Medium-term | Lasting more than one year and up to five years.

@ Long-term | Lasting more than five years.

Finance uses three periods. **Short-term** means lasting up to one year. **Medium-term** means lasting more than one year and up to five years. **Long-term** means lasting more than five years. Writers and banks draw these lines in slightly different places, so a reader meeting another book should check its lines.^2 This book keeps the three lines above throughout.

> Each period has its own uses and its own sources of money.

The next table shows what each period is usually used for, and where the money usually comes from. We will study the sources one at a time, the short-term sources in this chapter and the others in Chapters 13 and 14. Read each row from left to right: the use is a need, and the source is the way the need is met.

:table Financing by time
| Period | Typical use | Typical sources |
| Short-term | Flour, wages, goods waiting for customers | Supplier credit, overdraft, short-term bank loan |
| Medium-term | A delivery van, a small oven | Term loan of one to five years |
| Long-term | Premises, large ovens, expansion | Long-term loans, debentures, shares, retained profit |

> The length of the need is the guide to the length of the funding.

The table shows a pattern, and the pattern leads to the next section. Each use has a natural length. A bag of flour is used in days. An oven is used for ten years. The funds should have a length that fits the need.

> Recap: short, medium and long are periods of up to one, five and more than five years.

*Before moving on.* Short-term means up to one year, medium-term up to five years, and long-term more than five years. Each period has its own uses and sources. You should now be able to name one use and one source for each period.

## 12.3 The Matching Principle

@! Go slowly here.

> A short loan for a long need, or a long loan for a short need, causes trouble.

A student wants a motorcycle that will last ten years. A friend offers a loan, but demands full repayment in one month. The student cannot repay, because the motorcycle has earned nothing yet. Now reverse the case. The student borrows for ten years to buy lunch for one week. For nine years and fifty-one weeks the student pays interest on money that was needed for seven days. Both loans are poorly fitted to the need.

> The matching principle: fund short needs with short money, long needs with long money.

@ Matching principle | Fund short-term needs with short-term money and long-term needs with long-term money.

The **matching principle** means fund short-term needs with short-term money, and long-term needs with long-term money. A firm that follows it repays each loan from the money the asset earns, at about the time the asset earns it. A firm that ignores it must find new money to repay old money.

> A firm's working capital need has a permanent part and a temporary part.

Working capital shows the principle well. A firm's need for working capital has two parts. The **permanent part** is the level the firm needs all year, even in a quiet month. The **temporary part** rises in busy weeks and disappears afterwards. The next figure shows the two parts. The horizontal axis shows time through one year, and the vertical axis shows the money needed. The wavy line is the firm's total need. The flat line is the permanent part. The distance between the two lines, in busy weeks, is the temporary part.

:fig The bakery's need for working capital through one year: a flat permanent part and a temporary part that rises in busy weeks. | figures/fig12_matching.svg

> Matching sends the permanent part to long money and the peaks to short money.

By the matching principle, the permanent part should come from long-term or medium-term funds, and the temporary part from short-term funds that the firm repays when the busy weeks end. The figure labels each part with its fitting source.

> A worked example with small numbers shows what a mismatch costs.

Now we test the principle with numbers. Suppose the bakery needs Tk 60,000 more than usual for the two months of a festival season. It borrows Tk 60,000 for five years at 12 per cent a year. The interest for one year is Tk 60,000 multiplied by 0.12, which is Tk 7,200. The bakery needs the money for only two months, so the interest it should pay is two-twelfths of Tk 7,200, which is Tk 1,200. The remaining Tk 6,000 pays for money that sits idle. The loan was too long for the need.

> The reverse mismatch is more dangerous, because the lender can demand repayment.

The reverse mismatch is more dangerous. Suppose the bakery buys a Tk 3,00,000 oven with an overdraft that the bank may call back at short notice. The oven will earn money for years, but the bank may ask for repayment in months. The bakery may then have to sell the oven or borrow again on worse terms. Finding new money to repay old money is called refinancing, and it is a risk whenever the lender has the stronger position.

> Matching is a guide, not a law; firms sometimes accept a mismatch on purpose.

There are exceptions. Short-term money is often cheaper than long-term money, so a firm that wants low interest may accept some mismatch, and some firms do. They accept more risk in return for lower cost. The matching principle does not forbid this. It warns the firm to decide knowingly and to know which risk it carries.

> Recap and a self-test sentence.

*Before moving on.* Fund short needs with short money and long needs with long money. A mismatch costs idle interest in one direction and risks a forced repayment in the other. You should now be able to say, in one sentence, why a firm should not pay for an oven with an overdraft.

## 12.4 The Cash Cycle, Float and Estimating Needs

### The operating cycle and the cash conversion cycle

@! Go slowly here.

> Money leaves the firm as ingredients and returns as customers' cash, after a delay.

Follow one sack of flour. The bakery pays the supplier for it, keeps it in the store for some days, bakes it into cakes, delivers the cakes to an office, and waits for the office to pay. Money left the firm when the supplier was paid, and returns only when the office pays. The time between the two is the time the firm must find money from somewhere else.

> The operating cycle runs from buying ingredients to collecting cash.

@ Operating cycle | The days from receiving ingredients to collecting cash from the customer.

The **operating cycle** means the days from receiving ingredients to collecting cash from the customer. It has two parts: the days the goods wait in inventory, and the days the customer takes to pay. If the bakery holds ingredients for 8 days and customers take 30 days to pay, the operating cycle is 8 plus 30, which is 38 days.

> Supplier credit shortens the time the firm itself must fund.

@ Cash conversion cycle | The days between paying the supplier and collecting from the customer.

The supplier helps the bakery. If the supplier allows 10 days before payment, the bakery does not pay on the day it receives the flour. The **cash conversion cycle** means the days between paying the supplier and collecting from the customer. It is the operating cycle less the days the bakery takes to pay.

> Words first: cycle = inventory days + receivable days - payable days.

In words: cash conversion cycle equals inventory days, plus receivable days, less payable days. In symbols: CCC = ID + RD - PD. Here ID is the days goods wait in inventory, RD is the days customers take to pay, and PD is the days the firm takes to pay its supplier. The following example uses the catering line of the bakery on its own.

> A first worked example with clean numbers.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* Sundial Bakery's catering line sells to offices on credit. Ingredients stay in the store for 8 days. Offices pay 30 days after delivery. The bakery takes the supplier's cash discount and pays on day 10. *Given.* ID is 8 days, RD is 30 days, PD is 10 days. *Find.* the cash conversion cycle.

> The steps are shown in a table, one line of arithmetic each.

:table Cash conversion cycle of the catering line, discount taken
| Step | Working | Result |
| Operating cycle | 8 + 30 | 38 days |
| Less days to pay the supplier | 38 - 10 | 28 days |
| Cash conversion cycle | 28 days | 28 days |

> The bakery funds 28 days by itself; the check is a timeline.

*Answer.* the cash conversion cycle is 28 days. The bakery pays out on day 10 and collects on day 38, so it must find money for 28 days. *Check.* day 38 less day 10 is 28 days. The figure below draws the same cycle as a timeline. Each bar starts on the day shown on the line below it, and its length shows the number of days.

:fig The catering line's cycle: ingredients wait 8 days, customers pay after 30 more, and the supplier is paid on day 10, leaving 28 days to fund. | figures/fig12_cycle.svg

> A second example shows what happens if the bakery pays the supplier late.

Now a second case. The bakery does not take the discount. It pays the supplier on day 40, as the terms allow. Then PD is 40. The cycle is 8 plus 30 less 40, which is minus 2 days. A negative cycle means the customer's cash arrives two days before the bakery pays the supplier. The supplier then funds the whole cycle. This looks attractive, but it has a price, which section 12.6 measures.

### Estimating the money needed

> Estimating the money needed multiplies days by the daily cost.

How much money does the cycle tie up? The answer follows from the days and from the daily cost. Money tied up is the money the bakery has actually spent, so we count it at cost and not at the selling price. In words: money needed equals the daily cost, multiplied by the cash conversion cycle.

> The catering line spends Tk 2,400 a day on ingredients, so 28 days ties up Tk 67,200.

The catering line sells Tk 4,000 a day on credit. Ingredients are 60 per cent of sales, as in Chapter 3, which is Tk 2,400 a day. We count only ingredients, to keep the example small. With the discount taken, the cycle is 28 days. The money tied up is Tk 2,400 multiplied by 28, which is Tk 67,200. Without the discount, the cycle is minus 2 days. The money tied up is Tk 2,400 multiplied by minus 2, which is minus Tk 4,800: the supplier holds the bakery's money, not the reverse.

> Taking the discount raises the money the bakery must fund by Tk 72,000.

*Check.* the difference between the two cases is Tk 67,200 less minus Tk 4,800, which is Tk 72,000. That equals 30 days of ingredients at Tk 2,400 a day. By paying 30 days sooner, the bakery funds 30 days of ingredients itself. A firm also keeps a small cash balance above this figure, for surprises, and the owner should add it.

> The accounts record a receivable at the selling price, which is higher.

There is one more point. The accounts record a receivable at the selling price. Thirty days of sales at Tk 4,000 is Tk 1,20,000, the figure in the bakery's snapshot. The bakery has spent only the cost, Tk 72,000, on those orders. The rest, Tk 48,000, is profit not yet collected. Managers who want a cautious estimate count receivables at the selling price, and they must say so.

### Float

> Float is money paid but not yet usable.

@ Float | Money that has been paid but cannot yet be used by the receiver.

A cheque from a customer does not become usable money on the day it is written. It must be posted or carried, recorded, deposited, and cleared by the banks. The money in this gap is called **float**: money that has been paid but cannot yet be used by the receiver. It is not lost, but while it is in the gap, nobody can spend it.

> Collection float delays the firm's receipts; disbursement float delays its payments.

Float has two directions. **Collection float** is money the firm has been paid, but its bank has not yet credited. It hurts the firm. **Disbursement float** is money the firm has paid by cheque, but its bank has not yet deducted. It helps the firm, because the bank balance stays higher for a few days. Net float is disbursement float less collection float. A positive net float helps; a negative one hurts.

> A worked example finds the average delay and the money held in the gap.

The bakery receives catering cheques of Tk 1,20,000 in one 30-day month. *Given.* Tk 72,000 comes from offices in the city, and each cheque takes 4 days to become usable. Tk 48,000 comes from firms outside the city, and each takes 6 days. *Find.* the average delay and the average collection float.

> Multiply each amount by its days, add, and divide.

The average delay is a weighted average, because the larger amount should count for more. In words: average delay equals the sum of each amount multiplied by its days, divided by the total amount. The average float equals the same sum divided by the number of days in the month.

:table Collection float, one month
| Group | Amount (Tk) | Days | Amount × days |
| City offices | 72,000 | 4 | 2,88,000 |
| Outside the city | 48,000 | 6 | 2,88,000 |
| Total | 1,20,000 |  | 5,76,000 |

> Average delay is 4.8 days; average float is Tk 19,200.

*Steps.* the average delay is 5,76,000 divided by 1,20,000, which is 4.8 days. The average float is 5,76,000 divided by 30, which is Tk 19,200. *Answer.* cheques wait 4.8 days on average, and Tk 19,200 of the bakery's money is in the gap on an average day. *Check.* daily collections are Tk 4,000, and Tk 4,000 multiplied by 4.8 days is Tk 19,200.

> Disbursement float works in the bakery's favour, but by less.

Suppose the bakery pays the supplier Tk 2,400 a day on average by cheque, and each cheque takes 3 days to clear. The disbursement float is Tk 2,400 multiplied by 3, which is Tk 7,200. The net float is Tk 7,200 less Tk 19,200, which is minus Tk 12,000. On an average day, the bank shows Tk 12,000 less than the bakery's own records.

> Reducing float releases money, but the method must cost less than it saves.

A firm can reduce collection float by depositing cheques on the day received, or by asking customers to pay by bank transfer. Suppose one day is cut from the average delay. The bakery releases one day of collections, Tk 4,000. At the 12 per cent bank rate, that money earns or saves Tk 4,000 multiplied by 0.12, which is Tk 480 a year. A method that costs more than Tk 480 a year would not be worth using.

> Common slips in cycle and float arithmetic.

*Common slips.* Students add the payable days instead of subtracting them. They use selling price when the question concerns money spent. They divide by the number of cheques instead of the amount. They forget that a negative cycle is possible and meaningful. Check the sign of each answer before moving on.

> Recap and self-test sentence.

*Before moving on.* The cash conversion cycle is inventory days plus receivable days less payable days. Money needed is daily cost multiplied by the cycle. Float is money in the gap between payment and use. You should now be able to say how a 10-day delay in paying a supplier changes the cash a firm must find.

## 12.5 Short-Term Sources: Trade Credit, Bank and Others

> Short-term money has three families of source.

A firm that needs money for a few weeks has three families of source. It may ask its suppliers for time to pay. It may ask its bank. Or it may use sources that arise from the firm's own routine. This section describes each; section 12.6 measures the cost of the first.

> Trade credit is buying now and paying the supplier later.

@ Trade credit | Buying goods now and paying the supplier at a later agreed date.

The first source is **trade credit**, which means buying goods now and paying the supplier at a later agreed date. Every payable is trade credit. For the bakery, ten days of credit on flour is worth the price of ten days of flour. Trade credit is the largest source of short-term money for most small firms, because suppliers give it as part of the sale.

> An overdraft lets the firm spend beyond its balance, up to a limit.

@ Overdraft | Bank permission to spend more than the balance in the account, up to a stated limit.

The second source is the bank. An **overdraft** means bank permission to spend more than the balance in the account, up to a stated limit. The bank charges interest only on the amount used, and only for the days it is used. A short-term bank loan is different: the bank lends a fixed sum for a fixed period and charges interest on the whole sum.

> Overdraft interest is paid only on the money used.

Compare the two with numbers. The bakery needs Tk 60,000 for 60 days. At 12 per cent a year, an overdraft of Tk 60,000 costs Tk 60,000 multiplied by 0.12 multiplied by 60 and divided by 365, which is Tk 1,184. If the bank instead lends a fixed Tk 1,00,000 for the same 60 days, the interest is Tk 1,973. The larger loan costs more because the bakery pays for money it does not need.

> The third family arises from the firm's own routine.

The third family needs no bank and no supplier. A firm pays wages at the month's end and not daily, so for part of the month it owes its workers money: an accrued expense. A customer may pay in advance for a large order, which gives the firm money before it spends. Some firms also sell their receivables to a finance company for cash now, at a fee. Each of these is short-term money, and each has limits.

> A table sets the main sources side by side.

The next table compares the main sources. Read each row for one source. The cost column tells what the firm pays. The limit column tells what holds the source back.

:table Short-term sources compared
| Source | What it is | Cost | Main limit |
| Trade credit | Time to pay a supplier | Free if paid in time; high if a discount is lost | Supplier may refuse or stop supplying |
| Overdraft | Spending beyond the balance | Interest on the amount used | Bank sets a limit and may ask for repayment at short notice |
| Short-term bank loan | Fixed sum for a fixed period | Interest on the whole sum | Bank may ask for collateral |
| Accrued expenses | Wages and bills not yet due | None | Limited by pay dates |
| Customer advances | Payment before delivery | None | Only if customers agree |

> Cheap sources are limited; costly sources are plentiful.

The pattern in the table is common. The cheapest sources are limited in size, while the more flexible sources cost more. A firm usually uses the cheap sources first and the bank for the rest. From the supplier's seat, trade credit is a cost: the supplier waits for money it could use itself, and gives the credit only because it wants the sale.

> Recap: three families of source, with different costs and limits.

*Before moving on.* Short-term money comes from suppliers, banks and the firm's own routine. An overdraft charges interest only on money used. You should now be able to name two sources that cost nothing and say what limits each.

## 12.6 The Cost of Trade Credit Against a Bank Loan

@! Go slowly here.

> A two per cent discount sounds small, but the period matters.

A supplier offers the bakery a two per cent discount for early payment. Two per cent sounds small. Is it worth taking? The answer depends on a number the offer does not show: the number of days the bakery gives up in return.

> The terms 2/10, net 40 are a short code for a whole offer.

@ Credit terms | The days allowed for payment and any discount offered for early payment.

@ Cash discount | A price cut for paying within a stated short period.

**Credit terms** are the days allowed for payment and any discount offered for early payment. The supplier writes them as a short code. The terms 2/10, net 40 mean this: take a **cash discount** of 2 per cent, a price cut for paying within a stated short period, if you pay within 10 days. Otherwise the full bill is due on day 40.

> Skipping the discount is borrowing from the supplier.

Skipping the discount is borrowing. Suppose the invoice is Tk 50,000. The discount is 2 per cent, which is Tk 1,000. If the bakery pays on day 10, it pays Tk 49,000. If it waits until day 40, it pays Tk 50,000. The extra Tk 1,000 buys 30 more days of use of Tk 49,000. The supplier is lending the bakery Tk 49,000 for 30 days, and charging Tk 1,000 for the loan.

> Words first, then symbols, for the yearly cost.

We turn this into a yearly rate in two steps. First, find the cost for one period: the discount divided by the money actually used. Second, scale it up to a year by multiplying by the number of such periods in a year. In words: annual cost equals discount per taka paid, times 365, divided by the extra days.

> The formula for the yearly cost of giving up a discount.

In symbols: annual cost = d / (100 - d) × 365 / (N - D). Here d is the discount in per cent, N is the day the full bill is due, and D is the last day for the discount. Writing 100 - d in the first fraction reflects the money used: for each Tk 100 of price, the bakery uses only Tk 100 less the discount.^3

> First worked example: the bakery's invoice.

*Given.* invoice Tk 50,000, terms 2/10, net 40, so d is 2, N is 40, D is 10. The bank charges 12 per cent a year. *Find.* the annual cost of skipping the discount, and whether the bakery should take it.

:table Annual cost of skipping the 2/10, net 40 discount
| Step | Working | Result |
| Cost for one period | 2 / 98 | 2.04 per cent |
| Periods in a year | 365 / (40 - 10) | 12.17 |
| Annual cost | 2.04 per cent × 12.17 | 24.8 per cent |

> The cost is 24.8 per cent, twice the bank rate.

*Answer.* skipping the discount costs about 24.8 per cent a year, which is twice the bank rate of 12 per cent. The bakery should take the discount and borrow the money from the bank. *Check.* pay Tk 49,000 for 30 days at 12 per cent, which costs Tk 49,000 multiplied by 0.12 multiplied by 30 and divided by 365, or Tk 483. The discount saves Tk 1,000. The net gain is Tk 1,000 less Tk 483, which is Tk 517 on one invoice.

> The rule: take the discount when the credit costs more than the loan.

The rule follows. If the annual cost of giving up the discount is higher than the cost of other short-term money, take the discount and borrow. If it is lower, skip the discount and use the credit. In the bakery's case, 24.8 per cent is above 12 per cent, so the choice is easy.

> A second example has awkward numbers and a close decision.

Now a second, more awkward example. A second supplier offers 1/15, net 45 on the same Tk 50,000. Here d is 1, N is 45, D is 15. The cost for one period is 1 divided by 99, which is 1.01 per cent. The periods in a year are 365 divided by 30, which is 12.17. The annual cost is 1.01 per cent multiplied by 12.17, which is 12.3 per cent. The bank rate is 12 per cent, so the two are almost equal.

> When rates are close, the gain is too small to matter.

Test the gain. The discount is Tk 500, so the bakery would pay Tk 49,500. Borrowing Tk 49,500 for 30 days at 12 per cent costs Tk 488. The net gain is Tk 500 less Tk 488, which is Tk 12. Such a small gain may not repay the effort of arranging a loan, and a rise in the bank rate would remove it. When the two rates are this close, other matters, such as the bank's reliability, can decide.

> Paying late is an exception, and it carries risks that arithmetic does not show.

There is a further exception. Suppose the bakery pays the first supplier on day 60 instead of day 40. The extra days then number 50, and the annual cost falls to 2 divided by 98 multiplied by 365 divided by 50, which is 14.9 per cent. The cost falls, because the same Tk 1,000 buys more days. Even so, late payment can harm the relationship with the supplier, who may stop giving credit. The arithmetic gives only one part of the decision.

> Common slips in the trade credit formula.

*Common slips.* Students divide the discount by 100 instead of by 100 less the discount. They use the full credit period N and not the extra days N less D. They forget that the discount is a saving to be set against the loan's interest. They mix years of 360 and 365 days.^4 Check each answer by asking: if I borrowed this money from the bank instead, what would it cost?

> Recap: a discount lost is a loan taken from the supplier.

*Before moving on.* Giving up a discount is borrowing from the supplier at a yearly cost of d / (100 - d) × 365 / (N - D). Take the discount when this cost exceeds the bank rate. You should now be able to say why 2/10, net 40 costs far more than 2 per cent.

## The Worked Case: One Invoice, One Decision

> The situation: a Tk 50,000 invoice and Tk 18,000 in cash.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* *Situation.* On 10 September 2026 Sundial Bakery holds a flour invoice of Tk 50,000 with terms 2/10, net 40, dated that day. Cash is Tk 18,000. Customers owe the bakery Tk 1,20,000, but not before the end of the month. The bank would give an overdraft at 12 per cent a year.

> The question: pay early with borrowed money, or wait?

:case *Question.* Should the bakery pay on day 10 with an overdraft, or wait until day 40?

> The reasoning uses the cost formula and the matching principle.

:case *Reasoning.* Paying on day 10 costs Tk 49,000. The cash is Tk 18,000, so the bakery needs an overdraft. Skipping the discount costs 24.8 per cent a year, as section 12.6 showed. The overdraft costs 12 per cent. The bakery needs the money for 30 days, which is a short need, and an overdraft is a short-term source, so the funding fits the need by the matching principle.

> Conclusion: take the discount and borrow.

:case *Conclusion.* Take the discount and pay Tk 49,000 on day 10, using an overdraft. The overdraft interest for 30 days is Tk 483, and the discount is Tk 1,000. The bakery gains Tk 517.

> The check repeats the gain by the second method.

:case *Check.* Cost of the discount lost is 24.8 per cent, against 12 per cent: the difference favours borrowing. Net gain by the money method is Tk 1,000 less Tk 483, which is Tk 517. Both methods agree.

## Summary

> The chapter's main ideas in one paragraph.

Working capital is the money tied up in cash, receivables and inventory, and net working capital subtracts the short-term debts. A firm can have positive net working capital and still lack cash, because most of it is not cash. Financing is short-term, medium-term or long-term, and the matching principle says to fund short needs with short money and long needs with long money. The cash conversion cycle is inventory days plus receivable days less payable days, and money needed is daily cost multiplied by that cycle. Float is money paid but not yet usable, and net float is disbursement float less collection float. Short-term sources are trade credit, bank overdraft and loans, and the firm's own routine. Giving up a discount is borrowing from the supplier at a yearly cost that is often far above the bank rate.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Profit is not cash.

*A profitable firm always has cash.* It does not. Profit counts a sale on the day it is made; cash arrives when the customer pays. A firm can earn profit and fail to pay its bills in the same month.

> Positive net working capital is not the same as cash in hand.

*Positive net working capital means the firm can pay now.* It does not. Net working capital includes receivables and inventory, which are not cash. Only cash pays a bill today.

> A small discount percentage hides a large yearly cost.

*Two per cent is too small to matter.* The discount is earned over a short period. Scaled to a year, 2 per cent for 30 days is 24.8 per cent, which is more than the bank charges.

> Long-term money is not always the safer choice.

*Borrowing for the longest period is always safer.* It is safer against refinancing, but it costs interest on idle money when the need is temporary. Matching, not length alone, is the guide.

> Float is delay, not loss.

*Float is lost money.* Float is money in transit. It is not lost, but while it is in the gap, nobody can use it. The task is to shorten the gap when the saving exceeds the cost.

## In History

> A manufacturer cut the money tied up in inventory by changing how goods arrived.

After the Second World War, Japanese manufacturers had little money to spare for large stocks of parts. At Toyota Motor Corporation, engineers led by Taiichi Ohno developed a system in which parts arrive at the production line shortly before they are used. The idea is widely known as the just-in-time method, and writers connect it with Kiichiro Toyoda, the company's founder. The system lowered inventory, and so it lowered the money tied up in daily work. The lesson for this chapter is plain: when a firm shortens the time goods wait, it releases money for other uses.
