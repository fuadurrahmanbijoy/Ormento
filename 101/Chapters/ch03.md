:chapter III | The Business Environment
:mood Why does a good plan meet a bad day?
:desc Every business works in two worlds: the one inside its walls and the one around it. This chapter shows how to tell them apart, how to name the forces outside, and how to read change early and answer it with care.

> Monday: 100 cups sold and a profit of Tk 300.
Consider two days at one tea stall. On Monday its owner makes 100 cups of tea and sells every cup at Tk 10. Her costs for the day are Tk 700, so her profit is Tk 300. This is the stall from Chapter I. It is a teaching example and is not part of the bakery case.

> Tuesday: rain cuts sales to 80 cups and profit falls to Tk 100.
On Tuesday she works in exactly the same way. She uses the same kettle, the same tea leaves and the same helper, and she charges the same price. But rain falls hard from morning to evening. People stay indoors, and she sells only 80 cups. Her sales are Tk 800. Her costs are still Tk 700. Her profit has fallen to Tk 100.

> Profit fell by two thirds, though nothing inside the stall changed.
Here is the puzzle. Nothing inside the stall changed between the two days. Her skill, her money and her helper were the same. Yet her profit fell by two thirds. What caused the fall? And could she have done anything about it?

> Some forces lie inside a firm and some outside; each needs a different answer.
The answer has two parts. The cause lay outside the stall, so she could not prevent it. But she could have prepared. She might have made fewer cups on a day when the sky looked dark, or offered to deliver hot tea to the offices nearby. Every business lives in this position. Some forces that shape its results lie inside it, where the owner can act. Others lie outside, where the owner cannot give orders but can watch, prepare and respond. This chapter teaches you to tell the two kinds apart and to decide what to do about each.

> The earlier ideas this chapter uses.
*You will need* these ideas from earlier chapters: business, sales, cost, profit and customer (Chapter I); and the aims of a business, capital, interest, wages and location (Chapter II).

## The Inside of the Firm

> A farmer's crop depends on her choices and on the weather.
Think of a farmer's rice field. The crop depends on things the farmer chooses: the seed, the care and the work. It depends as well on things the farmer cannot choose: the rain, the heat and the price in the market. A business is like the field. Some forces that shape it come from its own choices. Others come from the world around it.

> The forces that shape a firm come from inside and from outside.
> Word in use. Environment: in daily life, the air, water and land around us. In business, all forces that affect a firm.
@ Environment | All the forces, inside and outside a firm, that affect what it does and earns.
These forces together are called the **environment** of a business. The environment means everything, inside the firm and outside it, that affects what the firm does and what it earns. In the puzzle, the rain was part of the tea stall's environment. So was the helper's skill. Notice that the word is wider than in daily life. It is not only nature.

> The inside of a firm is the part its owner can shape.
@ Internal environment | The people, money and ways of working inside a firm.
We divide the environment into two parts. The **internal environment** means the people, the money and the ways of working inside a firm. Everything outside the firm forms its external environment, which the next section teaches. This section studies the inside. It has four main parts: the owner, the staff, the money and the culture.

> The owner's aims and skills set the direction of the firm.
Begin with the owner. The owner supplies the aims, the first money and the willingness to accept risk. The owner of the bakery in our case file wants a monthly profit of Tk 60,000 (Chapter II). That aim shapes how hard the shop will work for sales, how much it will spend and how much risk it will accept. A different owner, content with a smaller profit, would run the same shop in a different way.

> Staff bring skill and effort, and also a monthly cost.
Next come the staff. Their number, skill and attitude decide the quality of the work. The bakery plans to employ four staff. If none of them can bake bread that rises well, a good shop will still fail. Staff are also a cost, because wages are paid every month, whether sales are high or low. A firm can improve its staff by training, but training takes time.

> Money limits what a firm can do and how long it can wait.
The third part is money. The bakery owner has Tk 12,00,000 of capital from personal savings (Chapter II). That sum limits the firm's choices. It can pay the rent of Tk 45,000 each month and buy flour and sugar. It cannot open a second shop without more money. A firm with little money has few choices. A firm with more money can survive a bad month. Borrowed money brings interest, which must be paid whether or not the firm is doing well.

> Culture is the unwritten way of working that staff share.
> Word in use. Culture: in daily life, the arts and customs of a country. In business, the shared habits inside one firm.
@ Business culture | The shared habits and beliefs about how work is done inside one firm.
The fourth part is the way people in the firm think and behave. **Business culture** means the shared habits and beliefs about how work is done inside one firm. In one bakery, staff wash their hands and check the oven without being told. That firm's culture values care. In another bakery, staff hurry and hide their mistakes. Culture does not appear on any list of money or machines. Yet it shapes quality, honesty and speed.

> Most inside forces can be changed by decision, but not quickly.
@ Controllable factor | A force the firm can change by its own decision.
See what the four parts share. The owner can change each of them by a decision: hire a skilled baker, train the staff, save more money or insist on clean tables. A force that a firm can change by its own decision is a **controllable factor**. Most parts of the internal environment are controllable. But controllable does not mean quick. A new skill may need months. A larger sum of capital may need years. The owner controls the direction of change, not its speed.

> The same blow lands differently on firms with different insides.
Two bakeries on one street show why the inside matters. Bakery P has Tk 4,00,000 in savings and a team of trained staff. Bakery Q has Tk 20,000 in savings and depends on one baker. In one week the flour supplier fails to deliver, and each bakery must pay Tk 20,000 of rent and wages without any sales. For P, this is 5 per cent of its savings, and it carries on. For Q, it is all of its savings, and it may not be able to buy flour next week. The outside blow was the same. The inside decided the result.

> Many people have a share in how a firm does.
@ Stakeholder | A person or group affected by a business, or able to affect it.
Several people care about how the firm does. The owner wants profit. The staff want wages and safe work. A bank that lent money wants repayment with interest. Customers want good goods at a fair price. Each of these is a **stakeholder**, which means a person or group affected by a business or able to affect it. Some stakeholders are inside the firm, such as the owner and the staff. Others are outside, such as customers and the government. Chapter IV studies what a firm owes to each.

> Recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* The environment is every force that affects a firm. The inside has four parts: owner, staff, money and culture. The owner can change these by decision, though slowly. People who care about the firm are its stakeholders.

## The Outside of the Firm

> Outside forces cannot be changed by one firm's decision.
@ External environment | All the forces outside a firm that affect it.
@ Uncontrollable factor | A force the firm cannot change by its own decision.
Return to the tea stall. The rain was part of its **external environment**, which means all the forces outside a firm that affect it. The owner cannot decide the weather. A force that a firm cannot change by its own decision is an **uncontrollable factor**. Most outside forces are uncontrollable. The task of the firm is not to command them but to understand them.

> Compare the inside and the outside before studying the outside.
Before studying the outside in detail, compare it with the inside. The table sets the two side by side. Read each row as a question: where does it lie, who controls it, how fast does it move, and what should the firm do about it?

:table The inside and the outside of a firm compared
| Point | Internal environment | External environment |
| Where it lies | Inside the firm | Outside the firm |
| Main parts | Owner, staff, money, culture | Six forces: economic, social, political-legal, technological, natural, competitive |
| Control | Mostly controllable | Mostly uncontrollable |
| Speed of change | Slow, and by the firm's own decision | Often fast, and often without warning |
| Whom it touches | The one firm | Many firms at once |
| Main response | Improve and build | Watch and respond |

> Outside forces fall into six groups.
It is usual to sort the outside forces into six groups. The table below names each group, says what it covers and gives an example from a bakery. Read it row by row. Some words in the table are new, and the pages that follow explain them.

:table The six outside forces, with a bakery example
| Force | What it covers | A bakery example |
| Economic | Demand, prices, incomes | Flour costs 8 per cent more |
| Social | Habits, tastes, customs, numbers of people | Customers prefer to order by phone |
| Political-legal | Laws, rules, taxes | A tax is added to the price of every loaf |
| Technological | New tools, machines and methods | An ordering app reaches customers |
| Natural | Weather, climate, land, water | Monsoon rain delays a flour delivery |
| Competitive | Other sellers to the same customers | A new bakery opens 300 metres away |

> The groups overlap; seeing the force matters more than its label.
Read the table with care. The groups overlap. A customer's habit of ordering by phone is a social force, because it is a habit. It is also a technological force, because it depends on a new tool. Do not spend long deciding which label is correct. The skill that matters is to see the force and to ask what it does to the firm.

### The Economic Force

> The economic force covers money conditions around the firm.
@ Economic force | Outside conditions of money and trade, such as demand, prices and incomes.
The **economic force** means the outside conditions of money and trade, such as demand, prices and incomes. It is usually the first force an owner feels, because it reaches the firm's sales and costs directly.

> Demand is how much customers will buy at a price.
> Word in use. Demand: in daily life, to ask firmly for something. In business, the amount customers will buy at a price.
@ Demand | The amount of a good or service that customers are willing and able to buy at a price.
Begin with the customers. **Demand** means the amount of a good or service that customers are willing and able to buy at a given price. When family incomes are good, more families can buy a cake for a birthday. When incomes fall, demand for cake usually falls first. Bread is closer to a need, and cake is closer to a want (Chapter I). So demand is the first thing every firm watches.

> A higher price usually lowers demand, but by how much is uncertain.
Price and demand are linked. When a price rises, customers usually buy less. Suppose the tea stall raises its price from Tk 10 to Tk 12, and sales fall from 100 cups to 90. Sales become 90 × Tk 12, which is Tk 1,080, so the owner gains. But if sales fall to 80 cups, sales are Tk 960, and the owner loses. The result depends on how customers react, and an owner can learn this only by watching them.

> A rise in one input price raises the firm's cost at once.
@ Supplier | A person or business that sells goods or services to another business.
Now consider what the firm buys. A **supplier** is a person or business that sells goods or services to another business. The tea stall buys sugar from a supplier. Suppose sugar is Tk 100 of the stall's daily cost of Tk 700, and the sugar price rises by 10 per cent. Ten per cent of Tk 100 is Tk 10, so the daily cost becomes Tk 710. If she still sells 100 cups at Tk 10, her profit is Tk 1,000 minus Tk 710, which is Tk 290. One price changed, and her profit fell by Tk 10.

> Inflation is a general rise in prices, not one price.
@! Go slowly here.
@ Inflation | A general rise in the level of prices over time, so one taka buys less.
Now a wider idea. Think of the price of a cup of tea at a roadside stall. Some years ago it cost less than it does today. So did bus fares, rice and school books. When prices in general rise over time, we call it **inflation**. Inflation means a general rise in the level of prices, so that one taka buys less than before. Use small numbers. Suppose a basket of goods costs Tk 100 this year and Tk 108 next year. Prices rose by Tk 8 on every Tk 100, so inflation is 8 per cent.

> A rise in one price is not inflation.
Take care with one point. A rise in the price of flour alone is not inflation. It is a rise in one price. Inflation means that prices in general are rising. Even so, inflation matters to a firm. Its costs rise, its staff ask for higher wages, and its customers find that their money buys less. *You should now be able to say:* inflation is a general rise in prices over time, so the same taka buys less.

> Check the idea with one small calculation.
*Pause and check.* A basket of goods cost Tk 200 last year and costs Tk 210 now. What is the rate of inflation? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> The whole economy and the cost of borrowing also matter.
Two more economic conditions deserve a mention. The first is the state of the whole economy. When more people have work and incomes grow, demand for many goods rises. When jobs become scarce, demand falls. The second is the cost of borrowing. If the rate of interest rises, a firm that has borrowed money must pay more each month, and families borrow less to spend.

### The Social Force

> The social force covers the habits and numbers of people.
@ Social force | The habits, tastes, beliefs and numbers of the people around a firm.
The **social force** means the habits, tastes, beliefs and numbers of the people around a firm. People follow custom and fashion. In Bangladesh, many families buy sweets and cakes for Eid and other festivals, so demand for such goods rises in those weeks. The number of young people in a district, the size of families and the level of education all affect what is bought.

> Habits can change quickly, and customs set limits on what may be sold.
A new habit can spread fast. Many young customers in cities now prefer to order food by phone and have it brought to the door. A firm that notices this early can serve them. A firm that does not may lose them. Customs also set limits. The food rules of the customers' religion decide which ingredients a seller may use. A seller who ignores such rules loses trust, whatever the price.

> Changes in numbers and awareness alter what people buy.
Changes in the number and age of people also matter. A district with many young families needs different goods from a district where most people are retired. Awareness changes as well. As more customers ask about hygiene, ingredients and the way goods are made, a seller who answers honestly gains trust. These changes are slow, but they are hard to reverse once they have taken hold.

### The Political-Legal Force

> The political-legal force covers government decisions.
@ Political-legal force | Government decisions, laws and taxes that a firm must follow.
The **political-legal force** means the decisions, laws and taxes of government that a firm must follow. Government is a powerful part of the outside world, because it can make a rule that no single firm can change. Political calm helps a firm plan. Sudden unrest or sudden changes of policy make planning harder.

> A regulation is a government rule that firms must follow.
@ Regulation | A rule made by government that businesses must follow.
A **regulation** is a rule made by government that businesses must follow. A bakery must hold a trade licence (Chapter V) and must follow rules on food safety and cleanliness. These rules protect customers. They also cost the firm time and money. A firm cannot choose which regulations to obey, but it can learn them early and plan for them.

> Government both helps and controls firms.
Government is both a helper and a controller. It builds roads and ports, keeps courts that settle disputes, and may support a trade that it wishes to grow. It also controls: it asks for licences, makes rules and collects taxes. A firm should therefore ask two questions about each government decision. Does it help us? Does it restrict us? One decision may do both. A rule on food safety restricts a bakery, but it also gives the bakery's customers confidence.

> A tax is money paid to government by law.
@ Tax | Money that people and businesses must pay to the government by law.
A **tax** is money that people and businesses must pay to the government by law. Governments use tax money for roads, schools, hospitals and other public services. A business pays several taxes, one of which is charged on its income. The tax that customers see most often is VAT.

> VAT is collected from the buyer for the government.
@! Go slowly here.
@ VAT | Value added tax: a tax added to the price of goods and services, collected by the seller for the government.
Start with an everyday bridge. Look at a restaurant bill. Under the price of the meal you may see a line marked VAT. That line is not the restaurant's profit. It is money that the restaurant collects from you and later pays to the government. **VAT**, short for value added tax, is a tax added to the price of goods and services. The seller collects it from the buyer and pays it to the government.

> A worked example shows how VAT changes the price a customer pays.
Use small numbers. A loaf of bread has a price of Tk 100 before VAT. Suppose the VAT rate is 15 per cent.^1 Fifteen per cent of Tk 100 is Tk 15. The customer pays Tk 100 plus Tk 15, which is Tk 115. The bakery keeps Tk 100 and passes Tk 15 to the government. In this book, a firm's sales are counted before VAT, because the VAT part belongs to the government and not to the firm.

> VAT can lower demand a little; the seller must decide how to show it.
VAT raises the price that the customer sees, so it can lower demand a little. The seller has a choice. It can keep its own price and let VAT show on top, so the customer pays more. Or it can lower its own price so that the total stays near the old figure, and the seller then earns less on each loaf. *You should now be able to say:* VAT is a tax that the seller collects from the buyer for the government, so it is not the seller's own income.

> Check the idea with one small calculation.
*Pause and check.* A book is priced at Tk 200 before VAT. At a VAT rate of 15 per cent, what does the buyer pay? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

### The Technological Force

> Technology changes how goods are made and sold.
@ Technology | The tools, machines and methods used to make or sell goods and services.
@ Technological force | A change in technology, outside the firm, that reaches the firm.
**Technology** means the tools, machines and methods used to make or sell goods and services. A change in technology that reaches a firm from outside is the **technological force**. A new oven may bake faster with less gas. Mobile payment services let customers pay without cash. Delivery apps put a shop's goods on a customer's phone. E-commerce, which Chapter I defined as buying and selling over the internet, is technology at work.

> Technology can lower costs or create new rivals.
Technology cuts both ways. A cheaper machine helps the firm that buys it. But a firm that cannot afford the machine may soon face a rival that can. New tools also need training, and training costs time and money. So the owner should ask two questions about each new tool. What will it save? And what will it cost to learn and to buy?

> Early and late adoption both carry risk.
A firm must also decide when to adopt a new tool. A firm that adopts early may pay a high price and meet faults that others have not yet found. A firm that adopts late may find that its customers have already moved to a rival. There is no fixed rule. The owner weighs the cost of the tool against the number of customers who would use it, and tests it on a small scale first.

### The Natural Force

> The natural force covers weather, climate, land and water.
@ Natural force | The weather, climate, land, water and other conditions of nature that affect a firm.
The **natural force** means the weather, climate, land, water and other conditions of nature that affect a firm. Bangladesh lies in a delta with heavy monsoon rain. Roads flood, trucks and boats arrive late, and goods spoil in damp stores. Heat can spoil cream cakes unless cold storage works. Much of the wheat used for flour in Bangladesh is brought from other countries, so storms at sea or poor harvests abroad can also reach the bakery.^2

> Nature can help a firm as well as harm it.
Nature does not only harm. The mango season helps the juice seller, and cool weather helps the seller of hot tea. Rain that hurt the tea stall in the puzzle would help a person selling umbrellas. The same force can lift one firm and press down another.

### The Competitive Force

> Competitors fight for the same customers.
@ Competitor | Another business that sells similar goods or services to the same customers.
@ Competitive force | The pressure that competitors place on a firm.
A **competitor** is another business that sells similar goods or services to the same customers. The pressure that competitors place on a firm is the **competitive force**. Competitors try to win customers by price, quality, service and convenience. A new bakery 300 metres from our bakery means that local customers can now compare two shops before they buy.

> Competition is wider than the shop next door, and it has a good side.
Competition is wider than the shop next door. Customers always have a choice, and they compare. Competition has a good side too. It keeps prices fair and quality high. It can even bring more customers to a street where many sellers gather. Even so, Chapter II counted few rivals as one factor in choosing a site for the bakery.

> A substitute is a different good that can replace yours.
@ Substitute | A different good that a customer can buy in place of another.
The wider competition comes from substitutes. A **substitute** is a different good that a customer can buy in place of another. A packet of biscuits is a substitute for a slice of cake, and a glass of juice is a substitute for a cup of tea. A firm may have no rival bakery nearby and still lose sales to a substitute. So the owner should ask what need the customer is meeting, and who else can meet it.

> Compare a rival on price, quality, service and convenience.
Suppose a second tea stall opens 50 metres from the stall in the puzzle and charges Tk 8 a cup. That is Tk 2 less, or 20 per cent below her price of Tk 10. Should she cut her price too? Not at once. First she should compare the two stalls on price, quality, service and convenience. If her tea is better and her service faster, many customers may stay at Tk 10. If she cuts her price to Tk 8, her sales on 100 cups fall from Tk 1,000 to Tk 800, a loss of Tk 200. Price is only one of four ways to compete.

### What a Firm Can and Cannot Command

> One small firm cannot order the outside world about, but it can read it.
All six forces share one property: a single small firm cannot command them. But cannot command is not the same as cannot touch. A firm can influence some forces a little. Advertising may shift tastes. A better product gives rivals less room. Joining a trade body (Chapter XII) lets firms speak to government together. The honest rule is this. Outside forces are uncontrollable for one small firm, but they are not unknowable. A firm that knows its environment decides better than one that does not.

> The same force can mean different things to different firms.
Look at the same force from different seats. Rain hurts the tea stall and helps the umbrella seller. A tax on all sellers still affects a small shop more than a large one with more money to spare. So the useful question is not only what is happening. It is what this change means for our firm.

> Forces act in chains, so one event can set off others.
Forces also act in chains. Heavy monsoon rain floods a road, which is a natural force. Flour deliveries arrive late, and the supplier charges more for the delay, which is an economic force. The bakery raises its prices, and some customers move to the new bakery, which is a competitive force. One event on the outside has reached the firm through three forces. An owner who watches only one force at a time may miss the chain.

> Recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* The outside has six forces: economic, social, political-legal, technological, natural and competitive. One firm cannot command them, though it can influence some a little. The same force can help one firm and harm another.

## Reading Change and Responding to It

### Why a Firm Studies Its Environment

> Study gives early sight of chances and dangers.
Why should a busy owner spend time on forces that cannot be commanded? There are good reasons. The first is early sight. An owner who knows what is coming can use an opportunity before rivals do and can reduce a danger before it arrives. The second is better planning. A plan that assumes that prices, rules and customers will never change is likely to fail. The third is realistic aims. In Chapter II, the owner set a profit aim and chose a site. Both choices depend on facts about customers, rent and rivals, and these facts come from the environment.

> Study also keeps a firm within the law and in its customers' trust.
There are further reasons. A firm that knows the law avoids fines and trouble with the authorities. A firm that understands its customers' habits keeps their trust. And a firm that reads change calmly survives longer, because it is not surprised by every storm. Study has a cost, since it takes time and attention. But for most firms the cost is far smaller than the cost of a surprise.

> The environment has four features that make study necessary.
The environment itself has four features that make this study necessary. It has many parts, so no single fact explains it. It changes, sometimes slowly and sometimes within a week. It is uncertain, because nobody knows exactly what will come next. And it is different for each firm, since the same force may help one firm and harm another. Because of these features, the owner can never finish studying. Scanning is a habit, not a task that ends.

### Scanning and Sorting Change

> A driver notices change early and arrives safely.
A bus driver watches the road, the mirror and the clock together. The driver does not control the traffic, yet still arrives safely, because the driver notices change early. A business owner needs the same habit.

> Scanning means watching the outside world on a regular basis.
@ Environmental scanning | Watching the outside forces regularly to notice change early.
**Environmental scanning** means watching the outside forces regularly to notice change early. It needs no special equipment. A baker who listens to customers, reads the price list each week, talks with suppliers and reads government notices is already scanning. Staff who live in the area also hear things the owner does not. The habit matters more than the method.

> Not every change matters equally; three questions sort them.
Not every change deserves action. After noticing a change, ask three questions. How large will the effect on sales or costs be? How soon will it arrive? How sure is it? A change that is small, far away and uncertain can be watched. A change that is large, near and certain needs an answer now.

> A change can be an opportunity or a threat, or both.
@ Opportunity | A change outside the firm that the firm can use to gain.
@ Threat | A change outside the firm that can harm it.
A change outside can help or harm. An **opportunity** is a change outside the firm that the firm can use to gain. A **threat** is a change outside the firm that can harm it. The same change can be both. The habit of ordering by phone is a threat to a shop that ignores it and an opportunity to a shop that adapts.^3

### Choosing a Response

> The danger of a threat depends on the strength of the inside.
The inside and the outside meet at one point: the firm's response. A threat is most dangerous when the inside is weak. If a firm has only one month of cash, a rise in flour prices may be serious. If it has savings and loyal customers, it can absorb the rise. In the same way, an opportunity helps only a firm that has the staff, money and skill to use it. So the owner reads the outside and the inside together.

> Five kinds of response are open to a firm.
Once the owner has read the change, a response is needed. A firm has five broad kinds of response. The table names them in words and gives one bakery example of each. Read each row as an answer to a different situation.

:table Five kinds of response to a change outside the firm
| Response | Meaning | A bakery example |
| Absorb | Accept the change and bear the cost for now | Keep prices steady for a month after a small rise in flour cost |
| Adjust | Change a price, an amount or a method | Raise the price of one cake by Tk 5 |
| Adapt | Change what is sold or how it is sold | Begin to take orders by phone |
| Act early | Prepare before the change arrives | Ask a second supplier for a price |
| Influence | Try to shape the outside force | Join a traders' group to ask for clear rules |

> A response is a choice, and two owners may choose differently.
No response is correct for every case. Absorbing a cost is sensible when the rise is small and short. It is unwise when the rise will last. Adapting costs effort, but it may be the only answer to a lasting change. Two careful owners can choose differently, because their money, staff and aims differ. What matters is that the choice follows a reading of both the outside and the inside.

### The Firm and Its Environment Together

> A firm and its environment shape each other.
The firm also acts on its environment. The bakery gives work to four people, buys from suppliers, pays VAT and draws customers to a street. A new bakery may lower the sales of the one 300 metres away, so it has become part of that firm's environment. The firm and its environment shape each other. Chapter IV asks what duties this gives a firm towards the people it affects.

> A different business meets the same six forces in different strength.
Now try the six forces on a different business. A vegetable grower near Dhaka is a teaching example and is not part of the bakery case. Heavy rain can flood the field, which is a natural force. Wholesale prices fall when many growers harvest in the same week, which is an economic force. Suppose the government raises the price of fertiliser, which would be a political-legal force. A mobile phone that shows market prices is a technological force. Buyers who want vegetables grown with less chemical spray are a social force, and neighbouring growers are a competitive force. For this grower, the natural force is the strongest. She may respond by growing two crops that ripen in different weeks, and by selling directly to nearby shops.

> Recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* Scanning means watching the outside regularly. A change may be an opportunity or a threat, and its danger depends on the strength of the inside. A firm may absorb, adjust, adapt, act early or influence. *You should now be able to say:* a firm cannot command the outside, but it can read it, test its effect on itself and choose a response.

## A Worked Case: The Bakery in March 2026

> The owner lists four changes found in the first days of trading.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 2 March 2026, the second day of trading, the owner of the bakery lists four changes in the area. First, the flour supplier says that flour will cost 8 per cent more. The bakery buys 500 kilograms (kg) a month at Tk 60 per kg. Second, VAT, taken here at 15 per cent, must be added to the price of bread, cakes and biscuits. Third, a rival bakery has opened 300 metres away. Fourth, nearby customers are ordering food by phone and expect to do so. The aim set on 12 January 2026 stays the same: a monthly profit of Tk 60,000, with a rent of Tk 45,000 (Chapter II).

### Question

> The case asks what is controllable, what matters most and what to do.
Which of the four changes can the owner control? Which of them matter most to the monthly profit target? What should the owner do about each?

### Reasoning

> Step 1: all four changes are outside forces.
Step 1 is to sort the changes. All four lie outside the firm, so all four are uncontrollable. The flour price is an economic force. VAT is a political-legal force. The new bakery is a competitive force. The phone-ordering habit is both a social force and a technological force.

> Step 2: measure the flour change with the sales test from Chapter II.
Step 2 is to measure what can be measured, starting with flour. The old cost was 500 kg × Tk 60 per kg, which is Tk 30,000. The rise is 8 per cent of Tk 30,000, which is Tk 2,400. The new cost is Tk 30,000 plus Tk 2,400, which is Tk 32,400. Chapter II gave a first test: sales must cover the rent and the profit target before other costs. We now add flour to the test. Other costs, such as staff, gas and packing, are left out here to keep the test simple. They would raise every total.

:table The sales test before and after the flour price rise
| Item | Before the rise | After the rise |
| Rent | Tk 45,000 | Tk 45,000 |
| Flour | Tk 30,000 | Tk 32,400 |
| Profit target | Tk 60,000 | Tk 60,000 |
| Sales needed | Tk 1,35,000 | Tk 1,37,400 |

> The flour rise lifts the sales needed by Tk 2,400, about 1.8 per cent.
Read the last row. Sales needed rise from Tk 1,35,000 to Tk 1,37,400, an increase of Tk 2,400. As a share of the old figure, Tk 2,400 divided by Tk 1,35,000 is about 0.018, or 1.8 per cent. The flour rise is real, but it is small. The bakery can absorb it for now.

> Step 3: VAT changes the price the customer sees, not the bakery's sales.
Step 3 is VAT. A loaf priced at Tk 100 before VAT costs the customer Tk 115, and the bakery keeps Tk 100. So VAT does not change the sales test, unless customers buy fewer loaves. The owner could try to keep the customer's total at Tk 100. Then the bakery's own price must fall to about Tk 87, because Tk 87 plus 15 per cent of Tk 87, which is Tk 13.05, gives Tk 100.05. That choice costs the bakery Tk 13 on every loaf. It is usually better to show VAT on the price tag and keep the bakery's own price.

> Step 4: the rival and the phone habit need responses from the owner's own side.
Step 4 is the rival and the phone habit. The owner cannot stop a bakery from opening, but can learn what it sells and at what price. The owner chose Dhanmondi because customers matter more than rent (Chapter II), so the bakery should compete on freshness and quality and avoid a price war that its small profit cannot bear. For phone orders, the plan of 5 January already includes orders through an app. The owner should begin with phone calls and a simple messaging page, and should check the fee of any delivery app before agreeing. Staff are the limit here, because only four are planned. One of them must be trained to take orders.

### Conclusion

> The owner absorbs, adjusts, adapts and watches.
The owner should absorb the flour rise this month and ask a second supplier for a price. The owner should show VAT on the price tags and keep the bakery's own price. The owner should compete with the new bakery on freshness and watch its prices each week. The owner should begin phone orders within a month, using one trained member of staff.

### Check

> Test the plan against the profit aim of Tk 60,000.
Test the plan against the aim. If the bakery absorbs the flour rise and nothing else changes, profit falls from Tk 60,000 to Tk 57,600. The shortfall is Tk 2,400, which is 4 per cent of Tk 60,000. That is acceptable for one month but not for many months, which is why the second price quote matters. The check also shows that these are judgements. A different owner, with more savings or fewer staff, might choose differently.

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.
The environment of a business is every force, inside and outside, that affects what it does and earns. The inside has four parts: owner, staff, money and culture. The owner can change these by decision, although not quickly. The outside has six forces: economic, social, political-legal, technological, natural and competitive. One small firm cannot command them, but it can learn about them, and it studies them to see chances and dangers early, to plan well and to keep within the law.

> What to do with change.
A firm scans the outside regularly, sorts each change by size, nearness and certainty, and decides whether it is an opportunity or a threat. It may absorb, adjust, adapt, act early or influence. Its choice depends on the strength of its own inside. The firm and its environment also shape each other.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake: thinking environment means nature only.
*Environment means nature only.* This is the daily meaning of the word. In business, the environment includes every force that affects a firm, such as customers, laws, money and competitors, as well as weather. Nature is only one of the six outside forces.

> Mistake: thinking controllable means quick or easy.
*Controllable means easy.* A force is controllable if the firm can change it by decision. That does not mean the change is quick. Training staff, building a culture of care and raising capital all take time. The owner controls the direction, not the speed.

> Mistake: confusing one price rise with inflation.
*A rise in one price is inflation.* Inflation is a general rise in prices over time. When only flour becomes dearer, a single price has risen. The test is whether prices in general are rising.

> Mistake: thinking VAT is the seller's cost or profit.
*VAT belongs to the shop.* The seller collects VAT from the buyer for the government. It is neither a profit nor, in the usual case, an ordinary cost of the shop. The shop's sales in this book are counted before VAT.

> Mistake: seeing every outside change as a threat, or looking only once.
*Every outside change is a threat, and one look is enough.* The same change can be an opportunity for a firm that is ready to use it. And forces move all the time, so scanning is a regular habit and not a single act.

## In History

> A true story of forces that turned a trade over.
Jute is a plant fibre grown in the delta of Bengal. For centuries, families there grew, spun and wove it by hand. In the nineteenth century, mills in Dundee in Scotland made jute into sacks and cloth, and much of their raw jute came from Bengal. In 1855 the first jute spinning machinery was installed at Rishra, near Calcutta, and mills in Bengal grew until they became serious rivals of Dundee. A technological force and a competitive force had moved the trade.

> A political decision cut the farms off from the mills.
Then politics changed the trade. When India was divided in 1947, the farms that grew most of the jute lay in East Bengal, but all 108 jute mills lay in West Bengal. The new East Pakistan had the fibre and no mills. This was a political-legal force, and no grower could change it. The government of Pakistan encouraged new mills in East Bengal in response. Adamjee Jute Mills, at Narayanganj near Dhaka, began production on 19 December 1951, and it grew into the largest jute mill in the world.

> Later forces changed jute again.
Forces kept moving. In the later twentieth century, man-made materials such as polypropylene replaced jute in many sacks and carpet backings, and Adamjee Jute Mills was closed in 2002. More recently, concern for the natural environment has brought new interest in a fibre that breaks down naturally. The story shows all six forces at work on one trade. It also shows that a firm or a country which reads the forces early has time to respond.
