:chapter VI | The Partnership
:mood Two people, one business: who may sign, and who must pay?
:desc When one owner has too little money or too few skills, a second owner can help. This chapter teaches the partnership: how it begins with an agreement, who the partners are, how they share profit and duty, and how it ends.

> One partner signs a large order alone, and the other receives the bill.
Two friends run a small shop together. One is skilled at baking, the other at selling. One morning the baker orders flour worth Tk 80,000 from a supplier, without telling the seller. The flour arrives, and the supplier sends the bill to the seller. The seller protests: "I never ordered this flour. I never even saw it." Must the seller pay?

> The answer depends on what the two friends became when they joined.
The supplier's answer is short: "You are the baker's partner. The baker acted for both of you." Whether the supplier is right depends on what the law says two people become when they join in business. This chapter explains that relationship, its rights and its dangers. By the end you will be able to answer the supplier, and you will know how our running bakery changes on 1 July 2026.

> This chapter builds on Chapter V.
*You will need.* From Chapter V: sole proprietorship, liability and unlimited liability, and the limits of one owner's capital. From Chapter I: owner and profit. From Chapter II: capital.

## The Meaning and Base of Partnership

> Joining two owners solves problems of money and skill.
Chapter V ended with a problem. One owner's savings and skills are limited. A furniture maker could not buy a Tk 15,00,000 machine on Tk 4,00,000 of savings. The most natural answer is to find a second owner who has money, or skill, or both. The firm then has two owners instead of one, and a new set of questions: who decides, who shares the profit, and who answers for the debts?

@ Partnership | A business owned by two or more people who have agreed to run it together and share its profit.
> The law names such an arrangement a partnership.
The law gives this arrangement a name. A **partnership** means a business owned by two or more people who have agreed to share its profit. Each owner is called a partner, and together they are called the firm.^1 In Bangladesh the rules come from the Partnership Act, 1932. The Act describes a partnership as the relation between people who have agreed to share the profit of a business that all of them, or any of them acting for all, carry on.

> A partnership rests on agreement, not on birth or family tie.
The base of every partnership is an agreement. No one becomes a partner by birth, or by living in the same house. Two brothers who inherit a shop are not partners until they agree to run it and share its profit. The agreement may be spoken, written, or even understood from how the people behave. But a spoken agreement is easy to forget and hard to prove.

@ Partnership deed | A written agreement between partners that sets out how the firm will be run.
> A written agreement prevents quarrels later.
For this reason partners usually write the agreement down. A written agreement of this kind is called a **partnership deed**. A deed normally states the name and place of the firm; the nature of its business; the capital each partner puts in; the ratio in which profit is shared; each partner's duties and any salary; how decisions are taken; and how a partner may leave. Our bakery will sign such a deed on 1 July 2026.

### The Features of a Partnership

> A partnership has two or more owners, one agreement, one business and shared profit.
The first features follow from the meaning. A partnership has at least two owners. It rests on an agreement. It carries on a business, which means an activity for profit. And the owners share the profit. In Bangladesh the law also limits how many partners a firm may have, so a partnership suits small groups.^2

> Partners trust one another with money and with the firm's name.
A partnership also depends on trust. Every partner handles the firm's money and may bind it to contracts. A partner who is careless or dishonest can harm all the others. For this reason partners choose each other with great care, and a new partner may join only if all agree.

> Every partner has unlimited liability, as in Chapter V.
Each partner has unlimited liability. This is the same idea as in Chapter V, now spread over several people. If the firm's things cannot pay its debts, creditors may claim the personal property of the partners. The next subsection shows an added danger: one partner's act can bring this on all.

### Mutual Agency

@! Go slowly here.
> We begin with an ordinary favour between friends.
@ Agent | A person who is allowed to act for another.
This idea is new and abstract, so we take it step by step. Begin at home. Suppose you ask a friend to buy rice for you at the market, and you give your friend Tk 500. The friend buys the rice in your name. You are bound by that purchase, because your friend acted for you. A person who acts for another in this way is an **agent**, and the person for whom the agent acts is the principal.

@ Mutual agency | The rule that each partner is both agent and principal for the others in the firm's business.
> In a partnership, every partner is agent for every other partner.
In a partnership, each partner acts for the others in the firm's business, and the others act for him or her. Each partner is therefore both agent and principal. We call this **mutual agency**. It means that when one partner makes a contract for the firm, in the ordinary course of its business, every partner is bound by it.

> This answers the opening puzzle: the seller must pay, because the baker acted for the firm.
Now we can answer the supplier's claim in the opening puzzle. Buying flour is an ordinary act for a bakery. The baker acted for the firm, and the seller is a partner in the firm. So the firm owes Tk 80,000, and both partners are liable for it. The seller may still have a claim against the baker if the deed forbade such large orders, but the supplier, who did not know of any limit, can ask either partner to pay.

> Limits and exceptions to mutual agency.
There are limits. The rule covers acts done in the ordinary course of the firm's business. A partner who signs a personal loan for a holiday does not bind the firm. A partner who exceeds a limit written in the deed binds the firm to an outsider who did not know of the limit, but that partner may have to repay the others.^3

*Pause and check.* A partner in a shop that sells mobile phones orders a stock of phone cases worth Tk 50,000 for the firm. The other partner did not know. Is the firm bound to pay? *(Answer at the back of the book.)*

> You should now be able to state the rule in one sentence.
*You should now be able to say:* "Each partner can bind the whole firm by acts done in the ordinary course of its business."

A partnership is a business owned by two or more people under an agreement. The partners share profit, may each bind the firm, and each answers for all the firm's debts.

> Recap: agreement, shared profit, trust, unlimited liability, mutual agency.
*Before moving on.* A partnership starts with an agreement, usually written as a deed. Its marks are shared profit, trust, unlimited liability and mutual agency.

## Types of Partnership and Types of Partners

> Partnerships can be sorted by how long they last.
@ Partnership at will | A partnership with no fixed time that any partner may end by notice.
Partnerships differ in how long they last. A **partnership at will** has no fixed time. It continues until one partner gives notice to end it. A partnership for a fixed term lasts for the period the partners agreed, such as five years, and ends when the period ends. A particular partnership is formed for one venture only, such as one building contract, and ends when the venture is finished.^4 Our bakery partnership will be a partnership at will.

> A partnership at will has no fixed life.
We use the word *will* in its everyday sense: the partners continue as long as they wish. The danger is that the firm may end at any time if one partner gives notice. For this reason partners who want certainty may agree a fixed term in the deed.

> Partners can also be sorted by the roles they play.
@ Active partner | A partner who puts in capital and helps to run the firm.
@ Sleeping partner | A partner who puts in capital but does not help to run the firm. Word in use: *sleeping* here means not taking part; the partner is still fully liable.
@ Nominal partner | A person who lends a name to the firm and puts in no capital.
Partners also differ in the role they play. Three kinds are common. An **active partner** puts in capital and also takes part in running the firm. A **sleeping partner** puts in capital but does not take part in running it. A **nominal partner** puts in no capital and takes no part, but lets the firm use his or her name, so outsiders treat that person as a partner.

> The table shows how the three kinds of partner differ.
The table below compares the three kinds. Read each row across: it says whether that kind of partner puts in capital, takes part in running the firm, and is liable to outsiders.

:table Three kinds of partner
| Kind of partner | Puts in capital | Takes part in running | Liable to outsiders |
| Active | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Sleeping | Yes | No | Yes |
| Nominal | No | No | Yes, if outsiders treated the person as a partner |

> Sleeping and nominal partners are still exposed to liability.
Many students think that a partner who does not work is safe. This is a mistake. A sleeping partner is a full partner in law, with unlimited liability, even without taking part. A nominal partner has put in nothing, but outsiders who trusted the name may claim the person's property. Having a name on a signboard is therefore not free of risk.

> Recap: duration gives three kinds of partnership; role gives three kinds of partner.
*Before moving on.* By time: at will, fixed term, particular. By role: active, sleeping, nominal. All three kinds of partner can be asked to pay the firm's debts.

## Rights, Duties and Sharing of Profit

> The deed decides rights and duties; the law fills the gaps.
@ Implied duties | Duties that the law adds where the deed does not mention them.
Partners may agree their rights and duties between themselves. Where the deed says nothing, the law supplies the missing rules. Duties supplied this way are called **implied duties**: duties that the law adds when the deed is silent.

> Partners have rights to take part, to see the books and to share profit.
Partners normally have these rights. A partner may take part in running the firm. A partner may look at the firm's books and copy them. A partner has the right to a share of the profit. A partner who lends the firm money beyond the capital agreed is entitled to interest, which the law fixes at six per cent a year unless the deed says otherwise. A partner may also ask to be repaid expenses properly spent for the firm.

> The main duties are honesty, good accounts and loyalty.
The duties matter even more. A partner must act in good faith toward the others, and must give true accounts and true information. A partner must work for the firm's good and must not make a secret profit from its business. A partner who earns profit by using the firm's name or property must pay that profit into the firm. A partner who competes with the firm in secret breaks the duty of loyalty. And each partner must bear a fair share of the loss.

@ Profit-sharing ratio | The proportion in which partners divide the profit and the loss.
> The partners' shares of profit are set as a ratio.
How is the profit divided? Partners agree a ratio, which we call the **profit-sharing ratio**: the proportion in which they divide profit and loss. The ratio is often equal to the proportion of capital each puts in, but partners may choose any ratio. If the deed is silent, the law supplies equal shares for each partner, and losses are shared in the same way.^5

> Our bakery's capital is Tk 12,00,000 and Tk 8,00,000, a ratio of 3 to 2.
Our bakery's partners put in Tk 12,00,000 and Tk 8,00,000. To find the ratio, divide both by Tk 4,00,000. We get 3 and 2. The partners agree that profit will follow capital, in the ratio 3:2. The shares of a profit then follow three steps. First, add the parts of the ratio: 3 + 2 = 5. Second, divide the profit by 5. Third, multiply the answer by each partner's part.

> A worked example with clean numbers shows the three steps.
*Worked example.* The firm earns a profit of Tk 5,00,000 in a year. Given: profit Tk 5,00,000; ratio 3:2. Find: each partner's share. Steps: parts in all = 3 + 2 = 5. One part = Tk 5,00,000 ÷ 5 = Tk 1,00,000. First partner = 3 × Tk 1,00,000 = Tk 3,00,000. Second partner = 2 × Tk 1,00,000 = Tk 2,00,000. Check: Tk 3,00,000 + Tk 2,00,000 = Tk 5,00,000, which equals the profit.

> Pause and check: share a new profit by a ratio.
*Pause and check.* A firm earns a profit of Tk 3,50,000, shared 3:2. How much does each partner receive? *(Answer at the back of the book.)*

> Deeds may add salary or interest on capital to the sharing.
Deeds often add two items. A partner who works full time may be paid a salary, which is a payment for work and is counted before profit is shared. A partner may also be paid interest on the capital put in, which the law allows only out of profit. Without these terms in the deed, the law gives neither.

> Recap: the deed rules first; implied duties fill gaps; profit follows the ratio.
*Before moving on.* The deed rules first, and the law's implied duties fill any gap. The profit-sharing ratio divides the profit in three steps: add the parts, find one part, multiply.

## Merits, Demerits, Dissolution and Comparison

### Merits and Demerits

> More owners bring more capital, more skill and shared risk.
The merits begin with resources. Two or more owners can bring more capital than one, and more skills: a baker and a seller together are stronger than either alone. The burden of work is shared, and so is the risk of loss. Decisions can be quick, because the group is small, and partners may check one another's mistakes. Forming a partnership is easy: it needs an agreement and a trade licence, as in Chapter V, and no complex permission.

> The demerits are shared liability, disputes and an uncertain life.
The demerits are equally important. Liability is unlimited, and a partner may have to pay debts caused by another's act. Partners may quarrel about money, work or direction, and a quarrel may close the firm. The firm's life is uncertain: it may be changed by a partner's death, exit or insolvency. A partner cannot freely hand his or her place to an outsider; the others must agree. And capital, though larger than one owner's, is still limited to what a few people can give.

### Dissolution

@ Dissolution | The ending of a partnership as it stood. Word in use: in daily life, mixing into a liquid; here, ending a partnership.
> Dissolution means the partnership ends.
A partnership does not last for ever. **Dissolution** means the ending of the partnership between all its partners. The firm's business may then be wound up, or the partners may start again on new terms. The law names several ways in which dissolution can occur.

> Five routes lead to dissolution.
First, the partners may agree to end it. Second, the law may require it, for example if the business becomes unlawful. Third, a contingency may end it, such as the end of a fixed term, the finish of a venture, or the death of a partner, unless the deed says otherwise. Fourth, in a partnership at will, any partner may end it by giving notice. Fifth, a court may order dissolution on grounds such as a partner's serious misconduct or a continued breach of the deed.

> After dissolution, the firm's property pays debts first, then the partners.
When a firm is dissolved and wound up, its property is used in a set order. Debts to outsiders are paid first. Next the firm repays any loans from partners. Then each partner receives back his or her capital. If anything is left, the remainder is shared in the profit-sharing ratio. Any shortfall at the first step falls on the partners' personal property, as in Chapter V.

## Worked Case: The Partner Joins

> The case file records the new partner, the deed and the capital.
:case Case file, illustrative. On 1 July 2026 a partner joins the bakery with Tk 8,00,000. The original owner's capital is Tk 12,00,000, so total capital becomes Tk 20,00,000. The two sign a partnership deed, and profit will be shared 3:2. A second outlet is planned. For tests only, we suppose that the firm earned a profit of Tk 5,00,000 in its first half-year as a partnership, and, in a separate supposition, that the firm failed with things worth Tk 2,00,000 and debts of Tk 6,00,000.^6

> The questions concern the deed, the profit shares and the failure.
*Question.* What should the deed record? How would the half-year profit of Tk 5,00,000 be shared? If the firm failed as supposed, who would pay, and how much would each partner bear in the end?

> The deed must record the main terms.
*Reasoning, step 1: the deed.* The deed should state the firm's name and business, the capital of each partner (Tk 12,00,000 and Tk 8,00,000), the ratio 3:2, each partner's duties, how decisions are taken, that the partnership is at will, and how a partner may leave. The total capital is Tk 12,00,000 + Tk 8,00,000 = Tk 20,00,000, which agrees with the case file.

> The profit is shared in three steps, as in the worked example.
*Step 2: the profit.* Parts in all = 3 + 2 = 5. One part = Tk 5,00,000 ÷ 5 = Tk 1,00,000. The original owner receives 3 × Tk 1,00,000 = Tk 3,00,000, and the new partner receives 2 × Tk 1,00,000 = Tk 2,00,000.

> In a failure, creditors may ask either partner, and the partners then settle between themselves.
*Step 3: the failure.* The shortfall is debts less things: Tk 6,00,000 − Tk 2,00,000 = Tk 4,00,000. Creditors may claim this from either partner's personal property, because each has unlimited liability. Suppose they collect all of it from the original owner. Between the partners, the loss is shared 3:2. One part = Tk 4,00,000 ÷ 5 = Tk 80,000. The original owner's share is 3 × Tk 80,000 = Tk 2,40,000, and the new partner's share is 2 × Tk 80,000 = Tk 1,60,000. The original owner, having paid all, may recover Tk 1,60,000 from the new partner.

> The partnership brings capital and a shared burden, and also a shared risk.
*Conclusion.* The partnership raises capital to Tk 20,00,000 and shares the work and the profit. But it also exposes each partner to the whole of the firm's debts, whatever the shares.

> The check confirms that the parts add back to the totals.
*Check.* Profit: Tk 3,00,000 + Tk 2,00,000 = Tk 5,00,000. Loss: Tk 2,40,000 + Tk 1,60,000 = Tk 4,00,000, which equals the shortfall.

## Closing Pack

### Summary

> The whole chapter in a few lines.
A partnership is a business owned by two or more people who have agreed to share its profit, usually under a written deed. Partners trust one another, share profit by a ratio, and each may bind the firm by acts done in its ordinary course, which is mutual agency. Each has unlimited liability. Partners may be active, sleeping or nominal, and a partnership may be at will, for a fixed term or for one venture. The deed rules first, and implied duties fill gaps. A partnership ends by agreement, law, contingency, notice or court order, and its property pays outside debts first. Compared with the sole proprietorship, it brings more capital and skill, but also shared risk and quarrels.

### Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: thinking a sleeping partner has no liability.
Many students believe that a sleeping partner is safe because he or she takes no part. This is wrong. A sleeping partner has put in capital and is a full partner, with unlimited liability.

> Mistake two: thinking each partner answers only for his or her share of debt.
Another error is to think that a partner with a 2/5 share of profit answers for only 2/5 of the debt. Creditors may ask any partner to pay the whole debt. The partners then settle their shares between themselves.

> Mistake three: thinking family or shared property makes a partnership.
A third error is to think that relatives who share a house or inherited property are partners. A partnership needs an agreement to run a business and share its profit. Kinship alone does not create one.

### In History

> Partnership law in this region was gathered into one Act in 1932.
For a long time the rules on partnership in this region were scattered. Some sat inside the Contract Act of 1872. The Partnership Act was passed on 8 April 1932 and came into force on 1 October 1932, bringing the rules into one statute that applies today in Bangladesh. It states what a partnership is, how partners deal with one another, and how a firm ends. Its long life shows how well one clear law serves people who must trust each other with money.
