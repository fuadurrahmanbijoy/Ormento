:mood When does baking bread become a business?
:desc This chapter gives you the first words of the subject and a picture of the whole. It explains what business is, how it divides into industry, trade and the services that support them, and what a new business must have in order to succeed.

> Two women bake the same bread, but only one is in business.

Early on a Friday morning, in a lane of an old Dhaka neighbourhood, two women are baking bread. They use the same flour and the same kind of oven, and both bake well. The first woman bakes twelve loaves for her own family. The second bakes twelve loaves and carries them to a small shop, where neighbours buy them. By the evening the first woman has a fed family. The second has a fed family and a handful of money.

> The puzzle: why is one woman's baking a business and the other's not?

Only the second woman is in business, and the reason is not the bread. The loaves are the same, and so are the flour, the oven and the skill. Something else has changed. The bread has left the home, it has been given in return for money, and the exchange was meant to leave her better off. But is that enough? Suppose she sold bread only once, to repay a favour. Suppose she gave all of it away. Suppose a clinic charged fees for its beds. Which of these is business, and which is something else?

> What this chapter does, and what it assumes about you.

This chapter answers those questions step by step. It assumes no earlier knowledge of business. Every business word is explained when it first appears, in bold type, and repeated in the narrow column beside the text. The chapter begins with a small set of basic words. It then explains what business is, how its work divides into branches, and what a new business must have to succeed.

> A bakery idea, which the book will follow chapter by chapter.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 5 January 2026 a person who enjoys baking decides to consider opening a bakery in a Dhaka neighbourhood. The bakery would make bread, cakes and biscuits. No shop has been chosen, no money has been gathered and no staff have been found. It is only an idea. The book follows this bakery from its first day through the chapters that follow. Every figure in the case is invented to teach, and none describes a real firm.

## The First Words

> This section teaches fourteen words on which the whole book rests.

Every subject has its own small stock of words, and business is no exception. Some of them you already use every day, but in business they carry a more exact meaning. This section teaches fourteen of them, one group at a time. It starts from ordinary life and arrives at the bakery. Go slowly. Nothing later in the chapter will make sense without these words.

### Needs and Wants

> Some things people cannot do without; others they merely like.

@ Need | Something a person must have to live and work, such as food.
@ Want | Something a person would like to have but can live without.

Think of one ordinary day. A person must eat, drink water and find shelter from the rain. The same person would also enjoy a sweet, a new shirt or a new phone. The first group of things is different in kind from the second. A **need** means something a person must have to live and work, such as food, water and clothing. A **want** means something a person would like to have but can live without, such as a sweet.

> The line between need and want moves with the person and the place.

The line between the two is not fixed. For a student in a city, a mobile phone may be close to a need, because lessons and messages arrive through it. For a farmer in a distant village, the same phone may remain a want. Business does not judge which is which. It supplies both, and each person decides how much to pay for each.

### Goods and Services

> What people need or want arrives either as a thing or as work done.

@ Goods | Word in use. In daily life, a person's belongings. In business, physical things grown or made to be sold.
@ Services | Work done for someone else that cannot be held, such as a haircut.

Walk through a busy market and you will see two kinds of offer. A fish seller hands a fish to a buyer. A barber cuts a buyer's hair. The fish is a physical thing, and the haircut is work done for someone. Business has a name for each kind. **Goods** means physical things that people grow or make and sell, such as rice, bread and cloth. **Services** means work done for someone else that cannot be held in the hand, such as a haircut, a bus journey or a medical check.

> Many offers mix goods and services; the buyer is the customer.

@ Customer | A person or organisation that buys goods or services.

Many offers mix the two. A restaurant sells food, which is goods, and also the work of cooking and serving it, which is a service. Whoever buys either kind is a **customer**. A customer may be a child buying a biscuit, a family buying a month's rice or a large firm buying a hundred bags of flour. Business exists because customers exist, and every later idea in this book returns to that fact.

### Exchange, Money and Markets

@! Go slowly here.
> Before money, trade needed two people whose wants matched exactly.

Imagine a time before money. A farmer has spare rice and wants cloth. She must find a weaver who wants rice at that moment and who will accept the amount she offers. If the weaver wants fish instead, the farmer must search again. Every trade needs two people whose wants match exactly. This is slow, and it often fails.

> Money lets each person sell to one buyer and buy from another.

@ Exchange | Giving one thing and receiving another in return.
@ Money | Anything people accept as payment for goods and services.

Money solves the problem. The farmer sells her rice to any buyer for money, and then uses that money to buy cloth from any weaver. The two wants no longer have to match. Each act of giving one thing and receiving another in return is an **exchange**. **Money** means anything that people accept as payment for goods and services. Today it is mainly notes, coins and balances held in bank accounts, and in Bangladesh the money is the taka.

> A market is a meeting of buyers and sellers, in a place or online.

@ Market | Word in use. In daily life, a place to shop. In business, any meeting of buyers and sellers.
@ Price | The amount of money asked for one unit of goods or services.

Buyers and sellers need some way to meet. A **market** means any place or arrangement where buyers and sellers meet to exchange goods and services. A fish market beside a river is a market in the plain sense. So is a website where thousands of people shop without ever seeing the sellers. A market does not need walls. It needs buyers, sellers and a way to agree on a **price**, which means the amount of money asked for one unit of goods or services. A loaf of bread at Tk 40 has a price of Tk 40.

*Pause and check.* A customer pays Tk 150 to a barber for a haircut. Name the service, the money and the exchange in this story. (Answers are at the back of the book.)

### Sales, Cost and Profit

@! Go slowly here.
> A tea seller shows the idea of profit with small numbers.

@ Sales | The money a business receives from selling goods or services.
@ Cost | Money a business spends to make or sell what it offers.

Begin with a tea stall at a bus stop. Each morning the tea seller spends Tk 700 on milk, tea leaves, sugar and gas.^1 During the day she sells 100 cups of tea at Tk 10 a cup. The money she receives from selling is her **sales**: 100 cups at Tk 10 is Tk 1,000.^2 The money she spent to make the tea is her **cost**, which is Tk 700. Notice that she paid the cost before any customer arrived.

> Profit is what remains of sales after every cost is paid.

@ Profit | The money left from sales after all costs are paid.

The tea seller keeps what is left. Her **profit** means the money left from sales after all costs are paid. In words, profit equals sales minus costs. We follow three steps, because every later chapter uses them. Step one: find the sales, which is 100 × Tk 10 = Tk 1,000. Step two: find all the costs, which is Tk 700. Step three: subtract, so Tk 1,000 − Tk 700 = Tk 300. The profit is Tk 300. As a check, add the profit back to the costs: Tk 300 + Tk 700 = Tk 1,000, which equals the sales.

> Costs stay fixed when sales fall, so profit can shrink or turn into loss.

@ Loss | The amount by which costs are greater than sales.

Now change the numbers. On a quiet day only 80 cups are sold. Sales are 80 × Tk 10 = Tk 800. The costs are still Tk 700, because the milk was bought before the customers came. Profit is Tk 800 − Tk 700 = Tk 100. On a day when few people travel, only 60 cups are sold. Sales are Tk 600, and costs are still Tk 700. Costs are now greater than sales by Tk 100. This result is a **loss**, which means the amount by which costs are greater than sales.

> Two slips to avoid when you calculate profit.

Two slips are common. The first is to call sales "profit". Sales are the money received; profit is only what remains after the costs. The second is to forget a cost, such as the daily payment for the pavement space where the stall stands. A forgotten cost makes the profit look larger than it really is, and the owner then believes she is richer than she is.

> A test sentence for the profit idea.

*You should now be able to say:* "Profit is what is left of sales after all costs are paid, and a loss is the amount by which costs are greater than sales."

### Owners

> Every business belongs to someone, who gains its profit and bears its loss.

@ Owner | A person with the right to a business's profit, who also bears its loss.

Every business belongs to someone. The person who has the right to the profit of a business, and who also bears its loss when there is one, is its **owner**. The tea seller owns her stall, so the Tk 300 profit on a good day is hers, and the Tk 100 loss on a poor day is hers as well. Some businesses have one owner, some have several, and some have thousands. The ways of owning a business are called forms of ownership, and Chapters V to VIII teach them. For now, one owner is enough to hold in mind.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* You now hold fourteen words. People have needs and wants; goods and services meet them; exchange through money, in markets, passes them from seller to customer. The seller's sales minus costs give profit, or loss. The owner takes either result.

## What Business Is

> A fee-charging clinic and an old phone sold once test the idea of business.

Ask first whether a clinic that charges fees is a business. Ask also whether a woman who sells her old phone to a neighbour is in business. Many people answer that it depends. The word requires an exact meaning, and the meaning rests on a few features that must all be present. We state the meaning first and then examine each feature in turn.

> The meaning: providing goods or services to customers in order to earn profit.

@ Business | The activity of providing goods or services to customers in order to earn a profit.

**Business** means the activity of providing goods or services to customers in order to earn a profit. The second woman in the opening puzzle provides goods, which are loaves, to customers, who are her neighbours, and she earns a profit, which is the money left after paying for flour and fuel. The first woman provides bread to her own family and earns nothing. Her baking is household work, and it is not business. The definition holds the six features below.

### The Features of Business

> Feature one: business is an exchange of goods or services for money.

*It is an exchange for money.* Goods or services pass to a customer, and money passes back. Where there is no exchange there is no business, however skilful the work. Cooking at home is not business, and neither is a gift. The exchange can be simple, such as one cup of tea for Tk 10, or large, such as a factory selling a thousand machines.

> Feature two: the aim is profit.

*It aims at profit.* The owner hopes that sales will be greater than costs. This hope is what separates a business from a body that sells things only to cover its costs or to help others. The aim does not guarantee the result. Many businesses make a loss at some time, and some make a loss for years. What matters is that they were started with the hope of profit.

> Feature three: business is regular, not a single act.

*It is regular.* One sale does not make a business. A woman who sells her old phone once is not in business. A woman who buys phones every week and sells them again is. Regular dealing means that the activity repeats and is expected to continue. A business has a name, a place or a method, and customers who expect to return.

> Feature four: risk, and the person who accepts it, the entrepreneur.

@ Risk | The chance that things turn out worse than planned.
@ Entrepreneur | A person who starts a business, brings its parts together and accepts its risk.

*It carries risk.* The tea seller's quiet day showed the problem. She bought her milk and sugar before she knew how many customers would come. **Risk** means the chance that things turn out worse than planned. Every business carries it, because costs are met first and sales come later. The person who starts a business, brings its parts together and accepts its risk is the **entrepreneur**. Risk can be reduced, for example by insurance, which is explained later in this chapter, but it can never be removed.

> Feature five: the business must serve customers who want what it offers.

*It serves customers.* A business survives only if enough customers want what it offers, at a price they accept. A bakery that makes excellent bread nobody wants to buy will close. Looked at from the owner's seat, business is a search for profit. Looked at from the customer's seat, it is a source of goods and services. Both views are true, and each depends on the other.

> Feature six: the activity must be lawful.

*It is lawful.* An activity that the law forbids, such as selling banned drugs, may earn money, but it is a crime and not business as this book uses the word. A lawful business must also follow the rules about licences, taxes and safety, which later chapters introduce one by one.

> Business is not the same as selling for profit alone; other aims matter too.

These six features give a firm test. Take the old phone sold once: it has exchange, but it is not regular and it was not started to earn profit. It fails the test. Take a bakery: it passes every feature. There are also cases between the two, such as a hobby baker who sells at weekends, and for these the features show where the doubt lies. Chapter II shows that a business pursues other aims as well, alongside profit.

### Business, Profession and Employment

> Three ways of earning a living are easily confused.

@ Profession | Work that needs long special training and follows rules set by a professional body.
@ Employment | Work done for an employer, who pays a wage or salary in return.

A doctor, a teacher and a baker all earn money, but they earn it in different ways. A **profession** means work that requires long special training and follows rules set by a professional body, as medicine and law do. A professional charges a fee, which is a payment for a particular piece of work. **Employment** means work done for an employer, who is the person or firm that pays, in return for a wage or salary, which is regular pay fixed in advance. Business, defined above, earns profit.

> A table compares the three; read it one row at a time.

The table compares the three ways of earning money. Read one row at a time, from left to right. The first row asks where the money comes from. The second asks who suffers if things go badly. The third asks what training is expected. The last gives an example.

:table Business, profession and employment compared
| Feature | Business | Profession | Employment |
| Where the money comes from | Profit from selling goods or services | Fees paid by clients | Wage or salary paid by an employer |
| Who bears the risk | The owner | The professional, who depends on clients | Mostly the employer |
| Training expected | Varies; skill in the trade helps | Long, formal training | Depends on the job |
| Example | A bakery owner | A doctor in private practice | A baker paid by a bakery |

> The three overlap; one person may be in all of them.

The three overlap. A doctor who owns a clinic and hires staff practises a profession and also runs a business. A baker may work for wages by day and sell cakes from home at night, and so be an employee and an owner in the same week. The table describes the usual case. It does not forbid a person from belonging to more than one column.

> The clinic puzzle: the difference lies in the aim, not the activity.

Return to the clinic that charges fees. A clinic run to earn a profit for its owner is a business. A clinic run by a charity, which charges low fees and spends any money left over on its patients, serves real needs but does not aim at profit. It sells a service, yet by the features above it is not a business. The difference lies in the aim, not in the activity. The same medical work can sit on either side.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Business provides goods or services to customers to earn profit. It is an exchange, it aims at profit, it is regular, it carries risk, it serves customers and it is lawful. It differs from a profession, which earns fees, and from employment, which earns a wage.

## Industry, Trade and Commerce

> A loaf of bread passes through many hands on its way to the table.

Follow one loaf of bread backwards. It lies on a shop counter. Before that, a baker made it. The baker used flour from a mill, and the mill ground wheat that a farmer grew. A van carried the flour, and a bank handled the payments. Each person did a different job, and each earned money, yet none did the same work. How can so many jobs be sorted so that we understand them? Business sorts them into branches.

> Business has two great branches, industry and commerce.

A branch of business means one of the main kinds of activity into which business divides. There are two great branches. One is industry, which grows, takes or makes goods. The other is commerce, which helps goods reach customers. Both words are defined in full below. Figure 1 shows how each divides further. Look at it before reading on, and come back to it as each part is explained.

:fig The branches of business and their parts | fig_branches.svg

### Industry

> Industry grows, takes or makes goods; it has two main kinds.

@ Industry | Word in use. In daily life, a whole group of firms, such as the garment industry. Here, the branch that grows, takes or makes goods.

In this book, **industry** means the branch of business that grows, takes or makes goods. Notice that the word has another daily use. People speak of the garment industry or the tea industry and mean a whole group of firms. Here the word names a kind of work, not a group of firms. The kind of work divides in two, according to whether the goods are taken from nature or made from materials already taken.

> Primary industry takes from nature; secondary industry makes or builds.

@ Primary industry | Industry that grows or takes things from nature, such as farming.
@ Secondary industry | Industry that turns materials into other goods or builds things.

**Primary industry** means industry that grows or takes things from nature, such as farming, fishing, forestry and mining. **Secondary industry** means industry that turns materials into other goods or builds things, such as baking bread, weaving cloth, assembling bicycles and building houses. In the story of the loaf, the farmer's work is primary and the work of the mill and of the bakery is secondary. Some writers class mining or building differently, but this book keeps to the plain rule: taking from nature is primary, and making or building is secondary.

### Trade

> Trade buys goods and sells them again, without making them.

@ Trade | Buying goods and selling them again to earn a profit.

**Trade** means buying goods and selling them again to earn a profit. The trader does not make the goods. A fish seller at a market buys fish from a fisherman and sells it to households. Trade fills the gap between the maker and the user. Makers prefer to sell in large amounts, while a household wants to buy a little at a time. The word *trade* also means a skilled occupation, such as carpentry, but here it has only the first meaning.

> Trade has two stages: wholesale and retail.

@ Wholesaler | A trader who buys large amounts from makers and sells smaller amounts to shops.
@ Retailer | A trader who sells small amounts of goods to final customers.

Trade often passes through two stages. A **wholesaler** means a trader who buys large amounts from makers and sells smaller amounts to shops. A **retailer** means a trader who sells small amounts of goods to final customers. A bag of flour may travel from a mill to a wholesaler, and from the wholesaler to a grocer, who sells it by the kilogram to a household. When the bakery sells its own bread across the counter, it acts as the retailer of its own goods.

> Trade can be inside a country or between countries.

Trade inside one country is called home trade. Trade between countries is called foreign trade. Foreign trade brings special papers, rules and risks, and it has its own chapters, IX and X. For this chapter remember only that a trader may buy or sell across a border, and that the same idea of buying and selling applies.

### Commerce and the Aids to Trade

> Commerce is trade together with its helping services.

@ Commerce | Trade together with all the services that help trade take place.
@ Aids to trade | Services that help buying and selling take place, such as transport and banking.

**Commerce** means trade together with all the services that help trade take place. Goods seldom move from maker to customer by themselves. They must be carried, stored, paid for, protected and made known. The helping services are called **aids to trade**, which means services that help buying and selling take place.^3 There are six main aids. Each one removes a particular obstacle that would otherwise stop trade, and we take them one by one.

> Transport removes the obstacle of distance.

@ Transport | Moving goods and people from one place to another.

**Transport** means moving goods and people from one place to another. It removes the obstacle of distance. Wheat grown in one district reaches a mill in another, and the flour reaches a bakery in the city. Trucks, trains, boats and vans are all transport. Transport also brings customers to the shop.

> Warehousing removes the obstacle of time.

@ Warehousing | Storing goods safely until they are wanted.

**Warehousing** means storing goods safely until they are wanted. It removes the obstacle of time. A farmer harvests wheat in one season, yet people eat bread in every season. A warehouse protects stock from rain, insects and theft until a buyer is ready.

> Banking removes the obstacle of payment.

@ Bank | An organisation that keeps money safe, lends money and carries payments.

A **bank** means an organisation that keeps money safe, lends money and carries payments from one person to another. Banking removes the obstacle of payment. A customer in one city can pay a seller in another without carrying notes. A bank can also lend money to a business that must buy goods before it has sold any.

> Insurance removes part of the obstacle of risk.

@ Insurance | A contract in which a small payment buys protection against a named loss.

**Insurance** means a contract in which a small payment buys protection against a named loss. It removes part of the obstacle of risk. A baker pays a small amount each year. If fire then destroys the shop, the company that sold the contract pays for the damage, up to the limit agreed. Insurance does not stop the fire; it softens the blow.

> Advertising removes the obstacle of ignorance.

@ Advertising | Telling people about goods and services so that they will want to buy.

**Advertising** means telling people about goods and services so that they will want to buy them. It removes the obstacle of ignorance. A new bakery may be excellent, but customers will not come until they know that it exists. Signboards, leaflets, radio and online posts all advertise.

> Communication removes the obstacle of silence between buyer and seller.

@ Communication | Passing messages between people, by post, telephone or internet.

**Communication** means passing messages between people, whether by post, telephone or internet. It removes the obstacle of silence between buyer and seller. An order, a price and a complaint all travel as messages. A trade that cannot send messages cannot agree on a price.

> The table shows the three kinds of work in the bakery.

The table below gathers the branches and gives an example from the bakery for each. Read each row from left to right: the branch, its job, and the bakery's example.

| Industry (secondary) | Makes goods from materials | Mixing dough and baking bread |
| Trade | Buys and sells goods | Selling loaves over the counter |
| Aids to trade | Help trade take place | A delivery van, a bank account, fire insurance |

### E-commerce

> E-commerce is trade over the internet, not a new branch.

@ E-commerce | Buying and selling goods and services over the internet.

Imagine a customer who at nine in the evening opens an app on her phone and orders a birthday cake for the next morning. She chooses the size, pays through her phone and receives a message to confirm the order. In the morning a rider delivers the cake. This is **e-commerce**, which means buying and selling goods and services over the internet. It is not a third branch beside trade. It is trade carried out in a new way, and it leans on the aids to trade: communication carries the order, banking carries the payment and transport carries the cake.

> E-commerce widens the market and lengthens the hours of trade.

E-commerce has clear gains. The seller reaches customers far beyond the neighbourhood. The shop is open at any hour, because the app does not sleep. A seller may need a smaller shop or none at all, which lowers cost. A customer can compare prices from home. For a small bakery, these gains can mean many more customers than the shop door could ever bring.

> E-commerce has limits that every seller must plan for.

It also has limits. A customer cannot touch, smell or taste the goods before paying. Payment or delivery may go wrong, and then the seller must put it right. The method works only where people have internet access and trust the seller. A bakery whose cakes arrive late or crushed will lose its online customers faster than it won them.

### One Firm, Several Branches

> Classify the activity, not the firm.

A single firm often works in several branches at once. The bakery makes bread, which is industry. It sells the bread, which is trade, and it takes orders through an app, which is a way of trading. It uses a van, a bank and an insurer, which are aids. It would be wrong to ask whether the bakery is industry or trade. The better question is about each activity. We ask three questions in turn. Does the work change materials into new goods? Does it sell goods, or buy goods to sell again? Does it help buying and selling happen? The first yes gives the branch.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* Business divides into industry and commerce. Industry grows, takes or makes goods. Commerce is trade plus the aids to trade: transport, warehousing, banking, insurance, advertising and communication. E-commerce is trade over the internet. Classify each activity, not the whole firm.

## Functions, Success and the Businessperson

> Imagine the bakery on an ordinary Tuesday and list its jobs.

Picture the bakery on an ordinary Tuesday. The bread must be made. Flour must be bought, and loaves must be sold. Someone must find money to pay the suppliers. Staff must be guided, the day's sales written down, and the risks watched. Different people may do these jobs, or one person may do them all, but they must be done.

### What a Business Must Do

> Each main kind of work a business must do is a function.

@ Function | A main kind of work that a business must do.

Each main kind of work that a business must do is a **function**. Business has six. The first is producing, which means making the goods or providing the service. The second is buying and selling, which means obtaining materials and finding customers. These two functions face the customer and the supplier, and without them there is nothing to sell.

> Finance and people are the next two functions.

The third function is finance, which means finding the money the business uses and watching how it is spent. The fourth is managing people, which means choosing staff, guiding their work and paying them. A bakery with good ovens but no money, or with money but no willing staff, will not last.

> Records and risk complete the six functions.

The fifth function is keeping records, which means writing down what comes in and what goes out, so that profit is known and not guessed. The sixth is guarding against risk, which means protecting the business against fire, theft, loss and change. Later chapters, and later subjects, examine each of these six functions in much more depth.

### What a New Business Needs to Succeed

> Ten bakeries stand in one area; why do some close within a year?

Suppose ten bakeries open in one neighbourhood. After a year, eight are still open and two have closed. The bread was not the whole difference. A business succeeds when several conditions hold together, and it can fail when one is missing. We take the conditions in four groups.

> First group: real demand and enough money.

The first condition is real demand: customers who want what is offered at a price they accept. The second is enough money, both to start and to continue until sales cover costs. The money put into a business is called capital, which Chapter II explains. Many new businesses make a loss at first, so the money must last through that period.

> Second group: a suitable place and capable people.

The third condition is a suitable place, such as a shop that customers can reach easily. Chapter II shows how to choose one. The fourth is capable people: an owner who knows the trade and workers who are willing and trained. A good place with weak people, or good people in a bad place, produces a weak business.

> Third group: knowing the rules and keeping records.

The fifth condition is knowing the rules. A business needs licences and must pay taxes, and Chapter V shows the first of these, the trade licence. The sixth is careful records. An owner who knows costs exactly can set prices that bring a profit. An owner who guesses may sell for less than the goods cost.

> Fourth group: the ability to adapt.

The seventh condition is the ability to change. Customers change their habits, rivals appear and rules are altered. A business that cannot adapt may lose its customers without noticing. Chapter III teaches how to watch such changes. No business meets all seven conditions perfectly, but a strong one meets most of them most of the time.

### The Businessperson

> A businessperson owns or runs a business; some qualities help.

@ Businessperson | A person who owns or runs a business.

A **businessperson** means a person who owns or runs a business. The entrepreneur is the businessperson at the moment of starting. Many people ask what such a person needs to be like. No list is complete, but a few qualities appear again and again in successful businesspeople, and each can be learned.

> Vision, judgement and willingness to bear risk.

The first quality is vision, which means seeing what customers will want before they ask for it. Linked to it is judgement, which means weighing the facts and choosing sensibly when the answer is not certain. The third is willingness to bear risk. A person who fears every loss will never begin, and a person who ignores every loss will not last. Good judgement stands between the two.

> Hard work, honesty and trust.

The fourth quality is hard work with persistence. A new business seldom pays quickly, and the owner must continue through the slow months. The fifth is honesty, which has a plain business reason. A baker who uses poor flour once may lose customers for years, because trust breaks quickly and returns slowly. A reputation for honest weights and fair prices brings customers back.

> Leadership, communication and willingness to learn.

The sixth quality is leadership, which means guiding others so that they work well together. It depends on communication, which means making plain what is wanted and listening to what staff and customers say. The seventh is willingness to learn, because markets change. No person is born with all of these qualities. They are built by practice, and a beginner can start building them today.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* A business must perform six functions: producing, buying and selling, finance, managing people, keeping records and guarding against risk. It succeeds when demand, money, place, people, knowledge of the rules, records and the ability to adapt hold together. The businessperson needs vision, judgement, willingness to bear risk, persistence, honesty, leadership and a readiness to learn.

## Worked Case: Sorting the Bakery's Activities

> The situation: seven jobs planned for the bakery.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* *Situation.* Later on 5 January 2026 the person lists seven jobs that the planned bakery would do. Job 1: mix dough and bake loaves. Job 2: sell loaves over the counter to passers-by. Job 3: take cake orders through a phone app. Job 4: hire a van to deliver cakes. Job 5: pay each day's sales money into a bank account. Job 6: buy insurance against fire in the shop. Job 7: print leaflets announcing the opening.

> The question: which branch does each job belong to?

*Question.* Into which branch of business does each of the seven jobs fall: industry, trade or aids to trade?

> The reasoning uses the three questions in order.

*Reasoning.* We ask three questions of each job, in order. First: does it change materials into new goods? If so, it is industry. Second: does it sell goods, or buy goods to sell again? If so, it is trade. Third: does it help buying and selling happen, by carrying, storing, paying, protecting or informing? If so, it is an aid to trade. Job 1 changes flour and water into bread, so it is industry. Job 2 sells goods to customers, so it is trade. Job 3 also sells goods, but over the internet, so it is trade carried out as e-commerce. Jobs 4 to 7 neither make nor sell anything. Job 4 carries goods, which is transport. Job 5 handles money, which is banking. Job 6 protects against loss, which is insurance. Job 7 tells people that the bakery exists, which is advertising.

> The conclusion appears in a table.

*Conclusion.* The table gives the branch of each job and the reason.

:table Branch of each job in the bakery's plan
| Job | Branch | Reason |
| 1. Mix dough and bake loaves | Industry (secondary) | Changes materials into goods |
| 2. Sell loaves over the counter | Trade | Sells goods to customers |
| 3. Take cake orders by phone app | Trade (e-commerce) | Sells goods over the internet |
| 4. Hire a van for delivery | Aid: transport | Carries goods to customers |
| 5. Pay sales money into a bank | Aid: banking | Handles money safely |
| 6. Insure the shop against fire | Aid: insurance | Protects against loss |
| 7. Print opening leaflets | Aid: advertising | Informs customers |

> A check by counting and by removal.

*Check.* Count the jobs: one is industry, two are trade and four are aids, which makes seven, so no job is missing and none is counted twice. Next test the answer by removal. Take away jobs 4 to 7, and the bakery can still bake and sell, though delivery is harder, money is less safe and fewer customers know the shop. Take away job 1, and there is no bread to sell. The aids support the work, but industry and trade are its centre.

## Summary

> The chapter in brief.

Business is the activity of providing goods or services to customers to earn a profit. Profit is what remains of sales after all costs are paid, and it is the owner's reward for bearing risk. Business is an exchange that is regular, aims at profit, carries risk, serves customers and is lawful. It differs from a profession, which earns fees, and from employment, which earns a wage. Business divides into industry, which grows, takes or makes goods, and commerce, which is trade together with the aids to trade. The aids are transport, warehousing, banking, insurance, advertising and communication. E-commerce is trade carried out over the internet. A business performs six functions and needs several conditions to succeed, and its owner benefits from vision, judgement, honesty and persistence.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: calling sales "profit".

*"Sales and profit mean the same."* They do not. Sales are all the money received from selling. Profit is only the part that remains after every cost has been paid. A shop can have large sales and a small profit, or even a loss.

> Mistake two: believing business means only factories or shops.

*"Business means a factory or a shop."* Business is a kind of activity, not a kind of building. A farmer selling rice, a rider delivering parcels and a website selling books are all in business if they meet the six features.

> Mistake three: using "commerce" to mean only trade.

*"Commerce is the same as trade."* Trade is only buying and selling. Commerce includes trade and the aids to trade, such as transport and banking.

> Mistake four: thinking one sale makes a business.

*"Anyone who sells something is in business."* A single sale, such as selling an old phone, lacks regularity and the aim of profit. Business means repeated dealing with an aim to earn.

> Mistake five: classifying a whole firm as one branch.

*"A firm belongs to one branch."* A firm usually works in several. Classify each activity. The bakery bakes, which is industry, sells, which is trade, and uses a van and a bank, which are aids.

## In History: The Muslin of Dhaka

> The old muslin trade shows every branch of business working together.

Long ago, the region around Dhaka was known far beyond Bengal for muslin, a cotton cloth so fine that rulers and merchants in distant lands prized it. The cloth was the product of a whole chain of work. Farmers grew a fine local cotton, which was primary industry. Spinners and weavers, working by hand, turned the cotton into cloth, which was secondary industry. Merchants gathered the cloth and sold it at home and abroad, which was trade. Boatmen carried it along the rivers, which was transport, and others handled payment and credit, which was banking.

> The chain weakened when cheaper machine-made cloth arrived.

In the nineteenth century, cheap cloth made by machines in Britain reached the markets of Bengal in large amounts. Hand-woven muslin took great skill and long labour, and it could not match the new low price. The craft shrank sharply. When the market for the finished cloth weakened, every part of the chain suffered together: the farmers, the weavers, the boatmen and the merchants.

> The lesson: branches depend on one another and on customers.

The lesson is twofold. First, the branches of business depend on one another. Industry has no use without trade, and trade has no goods without industry. Second, all of them depend on customers, and customers change their choices. A business must watch such change, and Chapter III shows how.
