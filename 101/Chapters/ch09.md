:chapter IX | Trade Between Nations
:mood Why does a country that can grow wheat buy it from abroad?
:desc Goods and services cross borders every day. This chapter explains why nations trade, what they gain and risk, how a country counts its trade, and how governments and world bodies guide it.

> A country can do many things, yet it still trades.

Look at the label inside a shirt. It may say that the shirt was made in Bangladesh, from cotton grown in another land, to be worn in a third. Look next at a loaf of bread: part of its flour may have grown in fields thousands of kilometres away. Yet Bangladesh has fertile land and could grow more wheat. Why does it not grow all it needs? And why do other countries buy our shirts when they could sew their own? These two questions lead to the idea of this chapter: that nations, like neighbours, gain by exchanging what each does well.

> This chapter needs the ideas of money, the environment and the forms of business.

*You will need:* the meaning of goods, services and money (Chapter I), the outside forces on a firm (Chapter III), and the idea of a company (Chapter VII). Chapter X shows how a single sale is carried out across a border.

## Home Trade, Foreign Trade, and Why Nations Trade

@ Home trade | The buying and selling of goods and services within one country.

> Trade inside a country is home trade; trade across borders is foreign trade.

In Chapter I you learned that trade means buying and selling. We now sort trade by place. **Home trade** is the buying and selling of goods and services within one country. A baker in Dhaka who sells a cake to a customer in Chattogram is engaged in home trade. **Foreign trade** is the buying and selling of goods and services between different countries.^1 A Bangladeshi firm that sends shirts to a shop in Germany is engaged in foreign trade.

^1: Foreign trade is also called "international trade". This book uses "foreign trade", the form used in Bangladesh.

@ Export | A good or service sold to a buyer in another country.

> One sale across the border is an export for the seller and an import for the buyer.

Every foreign sale has two sides. An **export** is a good or service sold to a buyer in another country. An **import** is a good or service bought from a seller in another country. The same sale is an export for the seller's country and an import for the buyer's country. Trade in goods, such as shirts and flour, is sometimes called visible trade, because one can see the goods cross the border. Trade in services, such as shipping, banking and tourism, is called invisible trade.

> Foreign trade differs from home trade in seven ways.

Why treat foreign trade separately? Because it brings problems that home trade does not. The table sets the two side by side; read each row for one point of difference.

:table Home trade compared with foreign trade
| Point | Home trade | Foreign trade |
| Parties | Buyer and seller in one country | Buyer and seller in different countries |
| Money | One currency | Different currencies, so an exchange rate is needed |
| Law and rules | One legal system | Two or more systems, with customs rules at each border |
| Movement of goods | Short distances, often by road | Long distances, often by ship or air |
| Papers | Few | Many (see Chapter X) |
| Risk | Lower | Higher: delay, currency change, political trouble |
| Payment | Simple, through local banks | Needs banks in both countries and safer methods of payment |

> To see why nations trade, start with two neighbours.

Now the central question: why do nations trade? Begin with two neighbours, Rina and Salim. In one day Rina can bake 12 cakes or sew 6 shirts. In one day Salim can bake 4 cakes or sew 4 shirts. Rina is better at both jobs. Does Rina gain anything from trading with Salim?

> Look at what each gives up: Rina gives up more cakes per shirt than Salim does.

Look at what each gives up. When Rina sews one shirt, she gives up 2 cakes, because 12 cakes and 6 shirts fill the same day. When Salim sews one shirt, he gives up only 1 cake. So Salim's shirts are *cheaper*, in cakes, than Rina's shirts. Suppose each divides the day in half and makes some of each. Rina makes 6 cakes and 3 shirts; Salim makes 2 cakes and 2 shirts. Together they have 8 cakes and 5 shirts.

> Now let each focus on what costs them less.

Now let Salim sew only shirts: 4 shirts. Rina must still supply the fifth shirt, which takes one-sixth of her day, so she sews 1 shirt and bakes cakes in the remaining five-sixths of her day: 10 cakes. Together they now have 10 cakes and 5 shirts. The shirts are the same; there are 2 more cakes. The extra cakes come only from making things in the right place.

> Trade then shares the gain between them.

Let them exchange: Salim gives 2 shirts to Rina and receives 3 cakes. Rina now holds 7 cakes and 3 shirts; Salim holds 3 cakes and 2 shirts. Compare with the start. Rina has 1 more cake and the same shirts. Salim has 1 more cake and the same shirts. Both are better off, and the weaker neighbour has gained as much as the stronger one.

@ Comparative advantage | The ability to make a good while giving up less of other goods than another producer would.

> The rule: gain comes from comparing what is given up, not from being best.

The rule behind the example is this. A producer has a **comparative advantage** in a good when making it costs that producer less in other goods given up than it costs another producer. Salim has a comparative advantage in shirts; Rina has one in cakes. Each should concentrate on the thing they give up least to make, which is called **specialisation**, and then trade. Notice that Rina is better at both jobs, and still gains. What matters is the comparison of what is given up, and not who is better.

> Countries do the same, as Bangladesh's shirts and others' wheat show.

Replace Rina and Salim by two countries, and cakes and shirts by wheat and clothing. A country with much land and few workers gives up little by growing wheat. A country with many workers and little farmland gives up little by making clothes. Each does best by specialising and trading. Countries also trade for other reasons: climate and minerals differ, so some goods cannot be made everywhere; technology and skills differ; and a larger market allows larger and cheaper production. Customers also enjoy variety.

> The idea has limits: not everyone gains, and new industries may need time.

The idea has limits, and honesty requires us to state them. Workers in an industry that cannot compete with imports may lose their jobs, even when the nation as a whole gains. A young industry may need protection for some years before it can compete. And a country that depends on a single export is exposed when its price falls. These points explain why governments guide trade, as we shall see in the last section.

## Importance, Benefits and Risks of Foreign Trade

> Foreign trade brings goods, markets, jobs and new methods.

The first benefit is access: a country can buy what it cannot produce, such as oil in a country without wells, or machines it has not learned to build. The second is choice and price: foreign competition keeps prices down and quality up. The third is markets: a firm that sells abroad has more customers than home buyers alone can provide, so it can grow larger. The fourth is employment: export industries give work to many people, and Bangladesh's clothing industry is an example. The fifth is learning: firms meet new methods, designs and technology. The sixth is earnings of foreign money, which pays for imports.

@ Exchange rate | The price of one country's money in terms of another country's money.

> Foreign money is needed, and its price is called the exchange rate.

Money brings us to a new idea. A buyer abroad pays in the seller's currency, or in a third currency such as the US dollar, so one country's money must be turned into another's. The price at which this is done is the **exchange rate**. Suppose the rate is Tk 120 for one US dollar. An American book priced at US$10 then costs Tk 1,200. If the taka weakens to Tk 126 for a dollar, the same book costs Tk 1,260.^2 Imports have become dearer, and Bangladeshi exports have become cheaper for foreign buyers. A fall in the taka's value therefore helps exporters and hurts importers.

^2: The rates in this book are invented to show the idea and are not the rates of any date.

> Trade has risks, which a wise firm and a wise government must watch.

Trade also carries risk. A country that buys its fuel or food abroad is exposed when supply stops or prices rise. Cheap imports may close local firms. Price swings in world markets can cut export earnings overnight. Exchange rates may move against a firm between the day it signs a contract and the day it pays. And disputes between governments may close markets. Firms reduce these risks by dealing with several buyers and suppliers; governments reduce them with policies that we study in the last section.

> Recap: benefits and risks of foreign trade.

*Before moving on:* foreign trade gives goods, markets and jobs but carries risk. You should now be able to say how a weaker taka affects an importer.

## Balance of Trade and Balance of Payments

@ Balance of trade | The value of a country's exports of goods minus the value of its imports of goods.
@ Balance | Word in use. In daily life, a weighing scale or what remains. In trade, the difference between two totals.

> A country counts its trade in goods by subtracting imports from exports.

A country needs to know how it stands in trade, just as a firm must know its sales and costs. The first measure is the **balance of trade**: the value of a country's exports of goods minus the value of its imports of goods, over a period such as a year. If exports are larger, the result is a *trade surplus*. If imports are larger, the result is a *trade deficit*. Imagine a country that exports goods worth US$4,000 million and imports US$5,200 million. Its balance of trade is 4,000 − 5,200 = −1,200, a deficit of US$1,200 million.

> A trade deficit is not always bad, nor a surplus always good.

Many students think a deficit is simply bad. That is not always so. A country that imports machines for new factories runs a deficit now and may export more later. A country with a persistent deficit must still find the money to pay, from earnings elsewhere or from borrowing. So a deficit is a warning to examine, and a surplus is not always a prize, since a country may export goods that its own people need.

@ Balance of payments | A record of all money that enters and leaves a country in a period, for trade, services, income and investment.

> The wider picture counts all money in and out, not goods alone.

Goods are not the whole of a country's dealings. It also sells and buys services such as shipping and banking. Workers abroad send money home, which is called *remittances*, and this is a large source of income for Bangladesh. And money flows in and out for investment and loans. The **balance of payments** is a record of all money that enters and leaves a country in a period. It has two main parts. The *current account* records trade in goods and services, income, and remittances. The *capital and financial account* records money moved for investment and loans.

@! Go slowly here.

> A simple example shows how the parts add up.

Take the country above and add figures, all in US$ million. Goods: exports 4,000, imports 5,200, net −1,200. Services: exports 500, imports 900, net −400. Remittances and other income received: net +900. The current account is −1,200 − 400 + 900 = −700. Suppose investment and loans bring a net inflow of +500 on the capital and financial account. The overall balance is −700 + 500 = −200. The country has paid out US$200 million more than it received. It meets the gap from its reserves, which are the foreign money held by its central bank, or by borrowing.

:table Balance of trade compared with balance of payments
| Point | Balance of trade | Balance of payments |
| What it records | Goods only | Goods, services, income, remittances, investment and loans |
| Result | Trade surplus or deficit | Overall surplus or deficit |
| Parts | Exports and imports of goods | Current account and capital and financial account |
| Size of picture | A part | The whole |

> A country can narrow a deficit by raising exports or cutting imports.

How can a country mend a deficit? It may raise exports by helping its firms, cut imports by taxes or limits, let its exchange rate adjust, attract investment, or borrow. Each choice has costs. Cutting imports raises prices at home; borrowing must be repaid. The measures that governments use are the subject of the next section.

## How Governments and World Bodies Guide Trade

> Governments guide trade to protect firms, earn revenue and balance accounts.

Governments do not leave trade alone, for four reasons. They wish to protect local firms from sudden foreign competition, to earn revenue, to correct a deficit, and to protect the public from unsafe goods. They also wish to help their own exporters. The tools fall into two groups: those that restrict imports, and those that encourage exports.

@ Tariff | A tax placed on imported goods.

> A tariff makes imports dearer; a quota limits how many may enter.

The best-known tool is the **tariff**, a tax placed on imported goods, also called a customs duty. The second is the **quota**, a limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported in a period. Other tools include import bans, licences, and standards for safety and quality. Compare two ways of cutting imports of biscuits. A tariff makes each pack dearer but allows any amount. A quota allows only a fixed number of packs but leaves the price to the market.

> A worked tariff example shows who gains and who loses.

Take imported biscuits that reach the port at Tk 90 a pack, while local biscuits sell at Tk 95. The government adds a tariff of 10 per cent. The duty is 10 per cent of Tk 90, which is Tk 9. The imported pack now costs Tk 99, so the local pack is the cheaper. The local firm gains sales; the government gains Tk 9 on each pack still imported; and the customer pays more, or has less choice. We see that a tariff helps some people and costs others.

> Governments also reward exports with help of various kinds.

To encourage exports, a government may give a subsidy (see Chapter VIII), which is cash support, to exporters, or allow them to import materials without duty if the finished goods are sent abroad. It may also set up zones with special facilities, supply information on foreign markets, and help firms to attend trade fairs.^3 Chapter X describes how an exporter works with these supports, and Chapter XII describes the zones and the bodies that promote exports.

^3: Cash support, duty refunds and bonded warehouses are among the schemes used in Bangladesh. The details change often; check current rules before relying on them.

@ WTO | World Trade Organization, the world body that sets rules for trade between its member countries.

> A world body writes rules so that trade between members is fair.

One country's tariff may harm another country's exporters, and each may then reply with its own. If every country did so, trade would shrink and all would lose. To prevent this, many countries have joined the **WTO**, the World Trade Organization, in which members agree on rules for trade.^4 It provides a place for countries to negotiate lower barriers, and a way to settle disputes between members. Countries may also sign agreements between two or more of themselves, to lower tariffs on each other's goods.

^4: The WTO began work on 1 January 1995 and took over from an older agreement, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) of 1947. Its headquarters are in Geneva.

> Argument remains over free trade and protection, and both sides have a case.

People disagree about how far governments should limit trade. Those who favour free trade say that it brings lower prices, better goods and growth, as the Rina and Salim example showed. Those who favour protection say that young industries need time, that jobs matter, and that no nation should depend on others for essentials. A thoughtful reader will see merit in each, and will notice that most governments use a mixture.

> Recap: tariffs, quotas, subsidies and the WTO.

*Before moving on:* governments use tariffs, quotas and subsidies to guide trade, and the WTO sets common rules. You should now be able to say who gains and who loses from a tariff.

## The Worked Case: Flour and the Exchange Rate

> The bakery's flour figures for November 2026.

:case Case file, illustrative. November 2026. Of the 500 kg of flour the bakery buys each month (Tk 32,400 in all, Chapter III), 300 kg is bought from a local mill at Tk 64.80 a kg. The other 200 kg comes through an importer who quotes in US dollars: US$0.54 a kg at an exchange rate of Tk 120 to the dollar. Taka falls to Tk 126 to the dollar. The importer's margin is ignored. All figures are invented to teach.

> The case asks what a weaker taka does to the bakery's flour bill.

*Situation.* The bakery's imported flour is priced in dollars. *Question.* What happens to the monthly flour bill when the taka falls from Tk 120 to Tk 126 to the dollar?

> We work out the cost before and after the change.

*Reasoning.* Before the fall: imported flour costs US$0.54 × Tk 120 = Tk 64.80 a kg, so 200 kg costs Tk 12,960. Local flour is 300 × Tk 64.80 = Tk 19,440. The total is Tk 32,400, which agrees with Chapter III. After the fall: US$0.54 × Tk 126 = Tk 68.04 a kg, and 200 kg costs Tk 13,608. The local price is unchanged, so the bill is Tk 19,440 + Tk 13,608 = Tk 33,048. The rise is Tk 33,048 − Tk 32,400 = Tk 648, or 2.0 per cent.

> The bakery's profit falls by the same amount unless it acts.

*Conclusion.* If the owners absorb the rise, monthly profit falls by Tk 648, from Tk 53,800 (Chapter IV) to Tk 53,152, which is Tk 6,848 short of the Tk 60,000 target. They might buy more flour from the local mill, whose price does not follow the dollar. *Check.* Tk 648 divided by Tk 32,400 is 0.02. And Tk 126 is 5 per cent more than Tk 120, so the imported part, Tk 12,960, should rise by 5 per cent: Tk 648. The two routes agree.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in a few lines.

Foreign trade is trade between countries; an export for one is an import for the other. Nations trade because each gives up less by specialising in what it can make at lower cost and exchanging the rest, a rule called comparative advantage. Trade brings goods, markets and jobs and carries risk, including changes in the exchange rate. The balance of trade counts goods only; the balance of payments counts all money in and out. Governments use tariffs, quotas and subsidies to guide trade, and the WTO sets rules among its members.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five confusions, each stated and corrected.

Some say a country must be best at a thing to trade in it. It must only give up less to make it. Others say that a trade deficit means the country is losing, but a deficit may reflect investment in future output. A third error is to treat the balance of trade and the balance of payments as the same; the first counts goods only. Another is to say that a weaker taka is always bad: it helps exporters and hurts importers. The last is to say that a tariff helps everyone. It helps local producers and the government but raises prices for buyers.

## In History

> In 1817 an English economist showed why even a weaker producer gains by trade.

In 1817 the English economist David Ricardo, who had made his fortune as a stockbroker, published *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation*. In it he used the example of England and Portugal, which traded cloth and wine. Even if Portugal could make both more cheaply, he argued, each country gained by making the good it gave up least to make, and exchanging. At that time many English landowners defended taxes on imported grain, and Ricardo's argument became a weapon in the case for freer trade.
