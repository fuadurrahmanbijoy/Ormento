:chapter VIII | Cooperative Societies and State Enterprises
:mood Owned by its members, or owned by the nation: who gains when profit is not the aim?
:desc Some businesses are owned by the people who use them, and some by the state on behalf of the public. This chapter teaches cooperative societies and state enterprises, then sets all five forms of ownership side by side.

> Small milk sellers earn little alone, but may earn more together.
Imagine a hundred small farmers who each keep two or three cows. Each has a little milk to sell each day. A trader visits each farm, names a low price, and takes the milk. No farmer can refuse, because the milk will spoil by evening. Alone, each farmer is weak. What if all hundred joined, sold the milk together, and kept for themselves what the trader had been keeping?

> A second puzzle: who should own the services that every person needs?
A second puzzle comes from the other side. Some services, such as roads, power or railways, are needed by everyone, yet may not earn a profit. Should such a business be left to private owners who must earn a profit, or should the government own it on behalf of the public? This chapter studies two forms of ownership that answer these puzzles: the cooperative society and the state enterprise.

## The Cooperative Society

### Meaning and Aims

@ Cooperative society | A business owned and run by its members, to serve their common needs rather than to earn profit for outside owners.
@ Member | A person who belongs to a cooperative society, owns a part of it and takes part in its decisions.
> A cooperative is a business that belongs to the people who use it.
In a company, ownership follows money. In a cooperative, ownership follows need. A **cooperative society** means a business owned and run by its members to serve their common needs. A **member** is a person who belongs to the society, owns a part of it and has a say in its decisions. The hundred farmers above, selling their milk together, would form a cooperative.

> The aim is service to members, not profit for outside owners.
The main aim is service. The farmers want a fair price. Buyers who form a consumers' cooperative want fair prices and good goods. A group of workers who form a credit cooperative want cheap loans. A cooperative may earn money, but it is not run to enrich outside owners. The money earned is used for the members' benefit.

> In Bangladesh the law is the Co-operative Societies Act, 2001, and the Constitution recognises cooperative ownership.
In Bangladesh the main law is the Co-operative Societies Act, 2001.^1 The Constitution of Bangladesh also names cooperative ownership, together with state ownership, among the principles of ownership.

### The Principles of Cooperation

@ Principle of cooperation | A basic rule that cooperatives follow, such as open membership and one member, one vote.
> Cooperatives follow a set of shared rules, called principles.
Cooperatives all over the world follow shared rules, called **principles of cooperation**. The International Cooperative Alliance sets out seven.^2 In plain words they are these. First, membership is open to all who can use the service and accept the duties, and no one is forced to join. Second, control is democratic: each member has one vote, whatever the money put in. Third, members put in capital fairly and receive only limited interest on it. Fourth, the society is independent. Fifth, it teaches its members. Sixth, cooperatives help one another. Seventh, they care for the community.

> One member, one vote is the sharpest difference from a company.
The second principle marks the sharpest difference from a company. In the company of Chapter VII, a holder of 1,20,000 shares has more votes than a holder of 80,000. In a cooperative, a member who has put in Tk 500 and a member who has put in Tk 5,000 each have one vote. Power follows people, not money.

> Recap: a cooperative serves its members, who share control equally.
*Before moving on.* A cooperative society is a business owned by its members to serve their needs. It follows principles, the sharpest of which is one member, one vote.

## Types and Working of Cooperatives in Bangladesh

> Cooperatives are grouped by level and by purpose.
Cooperatives can be grouped in two ways. By level, the Act recognises three kinds: primary, central and national societies. A primary society is made of individuals, such as villagers. A central society is made of primary societies, and a national society of central ones. By purpose, there are many kinds, including savings and credit, farmers', dairy, fishery, weavers', housing, transport and consumers' cooperatives. The rules list many types of cooperative.^3

> A cooperative is formed by members and registered with the government department.
A cooperative is formed by members who agree on its rules, called by-laws, and who apply for registration to the government department that supervises cooperatives. Once registered, it is a legal body and can own property and make contracts. The members meet in a general meeting and elect a managing committee to run the society day to day.

@ Surplus | The money a cooperative has left after paying all its costs.
> What is left after costs is called surplus and is shared by use.
What happens to money left over? In a company it is profit, divided by shares. In a cooperative it is called **surplus**: the money left after all costs are paid. After setting aside a reserve for hard times, the society usually returns the rest to members in proportion to how much they used the society, such as the milk each one supplied.

> A worked example shows the sharing of surplus by use.
*Worked example.* A dairy cooperative has a surplus of Tk 2,40,000. Its members together supplied 1,60,000 litres of milk. One member supplied 4,000 litres. Find that member's share. Steps: that member supplied 4,000 ÷ 1,60,000 = 1/40 of the milk. Share = 1/40 × Tk 2,40,000 = Tk 6,000. Check: Tk 2,40,000 ÷ 1,60,000 litres = Tk 1.50 a litre, and 4,000 × Tk 1.50 = Tk 6,000.

> Pause and check: share a new surplus by use.
*Pause and check.* A consumers' cooperative has a surplus of Tk 90,000 and members bought goods worth Tk 30,00,000 in all. One member bought goods worth Tk 20,000. What is that member's share? *(Answer at the back of the book.)*

### The Cooperative in Marketing Farm Goods

> Cooperatives help farmers collect, store, carry and sell their goods together.
Cooperatives play a special part in selling farm goods. A single farmer has little to sell and little power to bargain. Joined together, farmers can collect their produce, grade it, store it and carry it to market in larger loads, which lowers the cost for each. They can sell at a better price, and they need not depend on one trader. Cooperatives can also give farmers market news and credit. In the milk example, the cooperative takes the milk from many small farmers, cools and sells it, and returns the surplus.

> The limits: weak management and members who lack money.
There are limits. Many cooperatives suffer from weak management, little capital, and members who do not take part. A society that depends on a few leaders may fail when they leave. Cooperation works only when members trust one another and take part.

> Recap: types by level and purpose, surplus shared by use, and a role in marketing.
*Before moving on.* Cooperatives have three levels and many purposes. Surplus is shared by use. They help farmers by selling together.

## The State Enterprise

@ State enterprise | A business owned by the government and run for the public's benefit.
> A state enterprise is owned by the government for the public.
A **state enterprise** means a business owned by the government and run for the public benefit. The owner is the state, acting for the whole people, and not any private person. Banglapedia and other sources call the same idea a public sector enterprise or state-owned enterprise.

### Forms of State Enterprise

@ Public corporation | A state enterprise set up by a special law, with its own board and money.
> A government may own a business in three main ways.
A government may own a business in three main ways. First, as a department of a ministry, funded and run by the government itself. Second, as a **public corporation**: an enterprise set up by a special law, with its own board and its own accounts, though owned by the state. Third, as a government company, which is an ordinary company under the Companies Act in which the government holds most of the shares. In Bangladesh, bodies such as the Bangladesh Jute Mills Corporation and the Bangladesh Textile Mills Corporation are public corporations.

### Suitable Fields

> The state enterprises fit services that the public must have.
State ownership usually suits four kinds of field. The first is a service that must reach everyone even where profit is small, such as water or power. The second is a field needing very large capital that private firms cannot supply. The third is a field of public safety or security, such as defence. The fourth is a natural monopoly, where one supplier is more efficient than many, and public ownership guards against high prices.

> Recap: three forms, and fields that suit state ownership.
*Before moving on.* A state enterprise is owned by the government. It may be a department, a public corporation or a government company, and it suits services that the public must have.

## Importance, Need and Problems of State Enterprise

### Importance and Need

@ Nationalisation | The taking of a private business by the government, which then owns it.
> Governments own businesses to serve public aims.
State enterprise serves several public aims. It can supply services to all people at fair prices, create jobs, develop poor regions, and build industries that private firms will not. After independence it was also a way to take over businesses left by their owners. On 26 March 1972 the government issued an order to take over many industrial enterprises, an act of **nationalisation**: the taking of a private business by the government. Eleven sector corporations were set up to manage them.

> The need arises when private firms cannot or will not supply the service.
The need for state enterprise arises when private owners cannot or will not supply a service. Private firms seek profit, so they may avoid remote areas, poor customers or high risk. A government may step in to fill the gap. State ownership also lets a government plan the economy and keep key industries in the nation's hands.

### Problems, Loss and Its Causes

> Many state enterprises suffer losses and weak management.
State enterprises also face problems. Many make losses. Decisions may be slow, because officials must ask higher authority. Jobs may be filled for political reasons, so there are too many staff. Managers may have little reason to cut costs, since the state will cover any loss. Machines may be old, and control weak.

> A worked example shows how a low price can create loss.
Prices set for social reasons can cause losses. *Worked example.* A state-owned bread supplier makes 10,000 loaves a day. Each costs Tk 95 to make, but it sells each at Tk 85 to help poor families. Find the loss in 30 days. Steps: loss per loaf = Tk 95 − Tk 85 = Tk 10. Loss per day = 10,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 1,00,000. Loss in 30 days = 30 × Tk 1,00,000 = Tk 30,00,000. Check: 10,000 × 30 = 3,00,000 loaves, and 3,00,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 30,00,000.

> Losses are paid by taxpayers, so less is left for other needs.
Who pays this loss? The government covers it from taxes and loans. Money spent on covering the loss cannot be spent on schools or hospitals. This is the cost of a low price. It may be a fair cost if the aim is to help the poor, but the loss should be seen and judged, not hidden.

> Remedies: more freedom, clear targets, and sometimes privatisation.
@ Privatisation | The sale or transfer of a state enterprise to private owners.
Several remedies are tried. A state enterprise may be given more freedom to manage, and judged by clear targets. A government may also choose **privatisation**: the sale or transfer of a state enterprise to private owners. Bangladesh adopted a policy of moving some enterprises into private hands from 1975, by sale or by converting them into public limited companies. Progress has been slow, in part because employees fear job losses.

> Recap: state enterprise serves public aims, but low prices, weak control and political pressure can cause losses.
*Before moving on.* State enterprise exists for public aims and where private firms will not serve. Its problems include loss, slow decisions and political pressure. Remedies include freedom, targets and privatisation.

## Worked Case: Milk and Bread Prices

> The case file records the milk purchase and the price comparison.
:case Case file, illustrative. In September 2026 the bakery begins to buy milk from a dairy cooperative, at Tk 80 a litre for 400 litres a month. A private trader had quoted Tk 84 a litre. The bakery also compares its own price for a loaf, Tk 100 (as in Chapter III), with the price list of a state-owned bread supplier, Tk 85 a loaf. All figures are illustrative.

> The questions concern the saving, the surplus and the state price.
*Question.* How much does the bakery save each month by buying from the cooperative? Why can the cooperative sell at a fair price, and what is its position as an owner? Why might the state-owned supplier sell below the bakery's price?

> The monthly saving is found by subtraction.
*Reasoning, step 1: the saving.* Trader's price Tk 84, cooperative's price Tk 80, so the saving is Tk 4 a litre. For 400 litres: 400 × Tk 4 = Tk 1,600 a month.

> The cooperative can serve members, not outside owners.
*Step 2: the cooperative.* A cooperative need not pay profit to outside owners. After costs, its surplus goes to its member farmers in proportion to the milk they supplied, as in the worked example. The bakery is a customer, not a member, so it gains through the price only.

> The state supplier may price by public aim, not by cost.
*Step 3: the state supplier.* A state enterprise may fix a low price for a social aim, such as helping poor families. The bakery's price is Tk 100 and the state supplier's is Tk 85, a difference of Tk 15 or 15 per cent of Tk 100. The bakery must cover its own costs and a profit, so it cannot copy the lower price. It must compete on freshness and service, as Chapter III decided.

> The two forms differ in aim, and so in price.
*Conclusion.* The cooperative serves its members' needs and the state enterprise serves a public aim. Neither is run only for profit, and that affects the prices each can charge.

> The check confirms the arithmetic.
*Check.* 400 × Tk 84 = Tk 33,600 and 400 × Tk 80 = Tk 32,000. The difference is Tk 1,600, which agrees with step 1.

### Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: thinking a cooperative makes no money at all.
Many students think a cooperative earns no money. It may earn a surplus. The difference is who receives it: the members, in proportion to use, and not outside owners in proportion to shares.

> Mistake two: thinking the state enterprise and public company are the same.
Another error is to treat a public limited company and a state enterprise as the same. A public company is one that may invite the public to buy shares; the owners may be private. A state enterprise is owned by the government.

> Mistake three: thinking a state enterprise must always run at a loss.
A third error is to think that state enterprises must lose money. Some earn a profit. The point is that they are run for a public aim, and a loss may arise when the price is set below cost.

### In History

> The Rochdale pioneers opened a members' shop in 1844.
In 1844, in the town of Rochdale in northern England, a group of working people opened a shop that they owned together. Shops of that time often sold poor or short-weight goods. The members agreed that each would have one vote, that goods would be honest, and that the surplus would be shared in proportion to what each bought. Their shop lasted, and others copied its rules. The principles of cooperation used today descend from the practice of these pioneers.^4
