:mood A stranger across the sea: who sends first, the goods or the money?
:desc Chapter IX explained why nations trade. This chapter shows how one sale crosses a border: the steps an exporter and an importer follow, the papers that carry the trade, and the barriers that Bangladeshi exporters meet and how they may be lowered.

> Seller and buyer have never met, and neither will move first.

A baker in Dhaka receives an order from a shop owner in Dubai whom she has never met. She will not ship the biscuits until she is paid, for if the money never arrives she cannot get the goods back. The shop owner will not pay until the biscuits arrive, for if they never come he cannot get his money back. Each is honest, and each is afraid. The goods must travel by sea, and the money must travel by bank. Who moves first? The answer is a set of steps and papers, built over centuries so that strangers can trade. This chapter teaches them.

> This chapter needs the ideas of foreign trade, exchange rates and tariffs, and of banks and transport as aids to trade.

*You will need:* export, import, exchange rate and tariff (Chapter IX), and the aids to trade, such as banks, transport and insurance (Chapter I).

## Export Procedure

@ Exporter | A person or firm that sells goods to a buyer in another country.

> The exporter is the seller; the importer is the buyer abroad.

Two words name the two sides. An **exporter** is a person or firm that sells goods to a buyer in another country. An **importer** is a person or firm that buys goods from a seller in another country. Our baker is the exporter; the shop owner in Dubai is the importer. The goods themselves go by ship or aircraft, and the firm that owns the vessel carries them. The person who hands the goods to the carrier is called the **shipper**, and the person named to receive them is the **consignee**.^1 Often the exporter is the shipper and the importer is the consignee, but not always.

^1: A shipper may be a forwarding agent acting for the exporter, and a consignee may be a bank until the importer has paid.

> An export follows a fixed order of steps from first contact to final payment.

An export follows a fixed order. The table lists the steps, and the paragraphs after it explain the less obvious ones. Read down the table once, then read the notes.

:table Steps of an export
| Step | What happens | Who acts |
| 1 | The exporter registers with the government to export and finds a buyer | Exporter |
| 2 | Buyer and exporter agree the goods, price and terms; the exporter sends a price quotation | Both |
| 3 | The buyer arranges a letter of credit through a bank | Importer and bank |
| 4 | The exporter checks the letter of credit and makes or collects the goods | Exporter |
| 5 | Goods are inspected, packed and marked | Exporter |
| 6 | Space is booked with a carrier; insurance is arranged | Exporter or agent |
| 7 | Customs checks the papers and clears the goods | Customs |
| 8 | Goods are loaded; the carrier issues a bill of lading | Carrier |
| 9 | The exporter sends the papers to the bank and receives payment | Exporter and bank |

> Registration comes first, because a firm may not export without it.

Step 1 is registration. Most countries allow only registered firms to export, so that the government can keep records and offer help.^2 Registered, the exporter looks for buyers through trade fairs, through the promotion bodies named in Chapter XII, through websites, or through agents abroad. Step 2 fixes the *terms of sale*: what is sold, how many, the price, who pays for carriage and insurance, and how the buyer will pay.

^2: In Bangladesh an exporter registers with the office that controls imports and exports, and also holds a valid trade licence (Chapter V). Check the present rules and forms.

> Before shipping, the exporter checks the letter of credit and prepares the goods.

Step 4 matters more than it looks. The exporter reads the letter of credit line by line, because the bank will pay only if the exporter follows its terms exactly. If anything is wrong, such as a wrong date or a missing document, the exporter must ask the buyer to correct it *before* shipping. Steps 5 and 6 are practical: goods are checked for quality, packed so that they survive the journey, and marked with the buyer's name and port, and cargo insurance is bought to cover loss at sea.

@ Customs clearance | Permission from the customs service for goods to leave or enter a country, after papers are checked and duty is paid.

> Customs must approve goods before they leave the country.

Step 7 is **customs clearance**: permission from the customs service for goods to leave or enter a country, after the papers have been checked and any duty paid. We tend to think of customs only at the point where goods *enter* a country, but exports are checked too, to confirm that the goods match the papers and that nothing banned is leaving. In step 9 the exporter hands the papers to a bank. The bank forwards them to the importer's bank and, when they are in order, the exporter is paid.

@! Pause and check.

> Test the order of the steps.

Put these in the right order: shipment loaded; letter of credit received; goods packed; payment received; papers sent to the bank. The answer is at the back of the book.

> Recap: the steps of an export.

*Before moving on:* an export runs from registration and a quotation, through a letter of credit, packing and clearance, to shipment and payment. You should now be able to say why the exporter reads the letter of credit before making the goods.

## Import Procedure

> An import mirrors an export, with the buyer in charge.

An import is the same trade seen from the other side. The importer holds the money and orders the goods, so the importer's steps are mirror images of the exporter's. The table follows the same layout. Read it beside the export table to see the pairs.

:table Steps of an import
| Step | What happens | Who acts |
| 1 | The importer registers and decides what to buy | Importer |
| 2 | The seller sends a price quotation; the importer places an order | Both |
| 3 | The importer asks a bank to open a letter of credit in the seller's favour | Importer and bank |
| 4 | The seller ships the goods and sends the papers through banks | Exporter and banks |
| 5 | The importer's bank gives the papers when the importer pays or agrees to pay | Bank |
| 6 | The importer files a customs declaration, and customs works out the duty | Importer and customs |
| 7 | The importer pays duty and taxes | Importer |
| 8 | Customs releases the goods; the importer collects them from the port | Customs and importer |

@ Bill of entry | The importer's declaration to customs listing the goods, their value and their origin.

> The importer's declaration to customs lets the duty be worked out.

Steps 6 and 7 concern the government. The importer files a declaration, called the **bill of entry**, which lists the goods, their value and their origin.^3 Customs checks the declaration against the papers, may inspect the goods, and then charges the tariff you studied in Chapter IX, together with other taxes such as VAT. Only after payment will customs release the goods. Delay here costs money, since goods left at a port may incur storage charges each day.

^3: The name "bill of entry" is used in Bangladesh and in many other countries. The forms and the way of filing change with the customs system; check the current practice.

> A worked example shows how duty adds to the cost of an import.

Here is a small example, with an invented duty rate. *Given.* Goods are invoiced at Tk 2,00,000; freight and insurance are Tk 20,000; the duty rate is 5 per cent of the total value at the port. *Find.* The duty and the total cost to the importer. *Formula.* Duty equals the rate times the value at the port; total cost equals that value plus duty. *Steps.* Value at the port = Tk 2,00,000 + Tk 20,000 = Tk 2,20,000. Duty = 5 per cent × Tk 2,20,000 = Tk 11,000. Total = Tk 2,20,000 + Tk 11,000 = Tk 2,31,000. *Answer.* The duty is Tk 11,000 and the importer's cost Tk 2,31,000. *Check.* Five per cent of Tk 2,20,000 is one-twentieth, and Tk 2,20,000 ÷ 20 = Tk 11,000.

@! Pause and check.

> Test the method on new figures.

Goods are invoiced at Tk 80,000; freight and insurance are Tk 10,000; the duty rate is 10 per cent. Find the duty and the total cost. The answer is at the back of the book.

> Recap: the steps of an import.

*Before moving on:* an importer orders, opens a letter of credit, files a bill of entry, pays duty, and collects the goods. You should now be able to say which party opens the letter of credit.

## Documents of Foreign Trade

> Every border sale rests on papers, and each paper has one clear job.

The baker and the shop owner cannot see each other, so they trust papers. Each paper has a job: it describes the goods, proves they were loaded, shows who owns them, or promises payment. The table names the main documents and their purpose. After the table, we study the four that students find hardest: the invoice, the bill of lading, the charter party and the letter of credit.

:table Main documents of foreign trade
| Document | Who prepares it | What it does |
| Invoice | Seller | Lists goods, quantity, price and total owed |
| Packing list | Seller | Shows what is in each package |
| Certificate of origin | A chamber or authority | States the country where the goods were made |
| Bill of lading | Carrier | Receipt for goods, contract of carriage, and proof of ownership |
| Insurance document | Insurer | Proves the cargo is covered against loss |
| Letter of credit | Importer's bank | A bank's promise to pay the seller if the papers match |

@ Invoice | A seller's document listing the goods sold, their price and the total the buyer must pay.

> The invoice is the seller's bill for the goods.

An **invoice** is a seller's document listing the goods sold, their price and the total the buyer must pay. It serves as a bill for the importer, as proof of value for customs, and as a basis for the bank's payment. Before the sale, the seller may send a *proforma invoice*, which is an offer showing what the price would be, used by the buyer to apply for a letter of credit. A **packing list** states what is in each carton, so that customs and the buyer can check the goods against it.

@ Bill of lading | A carrier's document that receipts goods for shipment and states the terms of carriage.
@ Bill | Word in use. In daily life, a request for payment. In shipping, a written record of goods handed to a carrier.

> The bill of lading does three jobs at once.

The **bill of lading** is a carrier's document that gives a receipt for goods loaded and states the terms on which they are carried. It does three jobs. It is a receipt, since it proves that the carrier took the goods on board in good order. It is the contract of carriage, since it records the terms of the journey. And it is a *document of title*: whoever holds the original document may claim the goods at the end of the voyage. That third job makes it valuable, which is why banks keep it until payment is made.

@ Charter party | A contract by which a person hires a whole ship, or a large part of one, for a voyage or a period.

> A charter party hires the whole ship; a bill of lading covers one load.

A **charter party** is a contract by which a person hires a whole ship, or a large part of it, for a voyage or a period. A small exporter with a few cartons does not hire a ship; the cartons travel with others on a ship the carrier already runs, and the carrier issues a bill of lading. A firm shipping a full cargo of grain may charter the entire vessel. The two documents therefore differ in size and purpose, as the table shows.

:table Bill of lading compared with charter party
| Point | Bill of lading | Charter party |
| What it is | Receipt and contract for one consignment | Contract for hire of a ship |
| Who uses it | Any shipper, large or small | Firms with large cargoes |
| What is hired | Space for the goods | The ship, or much of it |
| Proof of ownership | Yes | No |

@ Letter of credit | A bank's written promise, at the buyer's request, to pay the seller if the seller presents the papers it names.

> We solve the opening puzzle with a bank as go-between.

@! Go slowly here.

Now we can answer the puzzle of the opening page. Think first of an everyday case. You wish to buy a bicycle from a stranger, but you are afraid to hand over money before you see it, and the seller fears to hand over the bicycle before the money. So you both go to a trusted friend. You leave the money with the friend, and the friend gives it to the seller when the bicycle is handed over. The friend is the go-between. In foreign trade the go-between is a bank, and its promise is the **letter of credit**: a bank's written promise, made at the buyer's request, to pay the seller if the seller presents the papers it names.

> The letter of credit has four parties and one rule: the papers must match.

Follow the steps. The importer, the *applicant*, asks his bank to issue a letter of credit naming the exporter as the *beneficiary* and listing the papers required. The bank, the *issuing bank*, sends the letter to a bank near the exporter, the *advising bank*. The exporter ships the goods and takes the required papers to that bank. If the papers match the letter exactly, the bank pays. The importer's bank then collects from the importer. The exporter is safe because a bank promises payment; the importer is safe because the bank pays only when the papers show the goods were loaded. The rule is that the papers must match.^4

^4: Banks around the world follow common rules for these credits, published by the International Chamber of Commerce, called the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits.

> Banks check papers, not goods, so quality must be settled elsewhere.

An important limit follows. The bank looks at *papers*, not at biscuits. If the papers match, the bank pays, even if the goods turn out to be poor. A buyer worried about quality should therefore require an inspection certificate among the papers, and should deal with a trusted seller. The letter of credit solves the problem of who moves first. It does not guarantee that the goods are what the buyer hoped.

> Recap: the four main documents.

*Before moving on:* the invoice is the bill, the bill of lading is a receipt and a proof of ownership, the charter party hires a ship, and the letter of credit is a bank's promise. You should now be able to say why a bank can safely pay against papers.

## Barriers to Export and How to Lower Them

> Even with the steps known, exporters from Bangladesh meet barriers.

Knowing the steps does not make exporting easy. Exporters from Bangladesh, like those in many developing countries, meet barriers of three kinds. Some lie inside the firm or the country: weak roads and ports, delays in clearance, uncertain supply of power, high cost of loans, and a short supply of trained staff. Some lie in the product: a country that sells mostly one kind of good, as Bangladesh largely does with clothing, is exposed when buyers change their taste or prices fall.^5 And some lie in the buyer's country: tariffs and quotas, strict rules on safety and quality, and buyers' demands about working conditions.

^5: Ready-made garments supply the large majority of Bangladesh's export earnings. Check the current share before quoting a figure.

> Each barrier has a likely cure, and the table sets them side by side.

The table lists some barriers, their effect, and a way to lower each. It is a starting point: the right cure for a given firm depends on its product and its market.

:table Barriers to export and ways to lower them
| Barrier | Effect | A way to lower it |
| Slow port and customs | Delays and storage costs | Faster clearance, simpler forms, better port equipment |
| Few kinds of export | Risk if one market weakens | Develop new products and new markets |
| Poor quality or standards | Goods rejected by buyers | Training, testing and quality certificates |
| Costly or scarce credit | Small firms cannot start | Low-cost loans for exporters |
| Tariffs and quotas abroad | Goods dearer or limited | Trade agreements and talks through the WTO |
| Little knowledge of markets | Wrong goods, wrong buyers | Market studies, trade fairs, trade missions |

@ Export promotion | Actions by government and others that help firms to sell more abroad.

> Export promotion is the set of actions that help firms sell abroad.

**Export promotion** means the actions by government and others that help firms to sell more goods abroad. They include the supports named in Chapter IX, such as cash support and duty refunds. They include trade fairs, where a firm shows its goods to foreign buyers; trade missions, which take groups of firms to visit a market; training in quality and design; and special zones with good facilities. Government bodies and trade bodies carry out much of this, and Chapter XII describes them.

> A sound strategy widens both the range of goods and the range of markets.

A sound strategy rests on two ideas. First, widen the range of goods, so that the country does not rest on one product. Second, widen the range of markets, so that one buyer's trouble does not become the country's trouble. Both need a steady supply of good-quality goods, trained people, and honest dealing in every consignment. A firm that delivers on time and as promised earns a good name, and a good name brings repeat orders, which is the best promotion of all.

@! Pause and check.

> Test the cost of delay.

Goods wait 6 days at the port, with storage at Tk 3,000 a day. The export is worth Tk 3,00,000. What per cent of the value is lost to storage? The answer is at the back of the book.

> Recap: barriers and promotion.

*Before moving on:* barriers lie at home, in the product and in the buyer's country, and promotion aims to lower them. You should now be able to say why a country that sells one product is at risk.

## The Worked Case: The First Export

> The bakery's first export, March 2027.

:case Case file, illustrative. March 2027. The bakery, now a private limited company (Chapter VII), accepts an order from a shop in the United Arab Emirates for 2,000 packs of biscuits at Tk 90 a pack, packed in 100 cartons of 20 packs. It costs Tk 62 to make and pack each pack, and Tk 14,000 to carry and insure the whole consignment, which the bakery pays. The buyer opens a letter of credit. All figures are invented.

> The case asks us to put the sale in order and test whether it pays.

*Situation.* The bakery will make its first export. *Question.* In what order does the sale move, which papers are needed, and what does the bakery earn before other costs?

> We follow the steps in order and name the papers.

*Reasoning.* The bakery registers as an exporter, agrees the terms with the buyer, and receives the letter of credit. It checks every line against the order. It makes and packs the 2,000 packs into 100 cartons, books carriage and insurance, and has customs clear the cartons. The carrier loads them and issues a bill of lading. The bakery sends the bank the invoice (2,000 × Tk 90 = Tk 1,80,000), the packing list, a certificate of origin, the bill of lading and the insurance document, as the letter of credit requires. When the papers match, the bank pays Tk 1,80,000. The cost of making is 2,000 × Tk 62 = Tk 1,24,000.

> The earnings figure follows from sales less making cost and carriage.

*Conclusion.* Earnings before other costs equal Tk 1,80,000 − Tk 1,24,000 − Tk 14,000 = Tk 42,000. The pilot pays, if other costs are small. The bakery should also watch the exchange rate, as in Chapter IX, since the buyer pays in foreign money. *Check.* The earnings per pack are Tk 90 − Tk 62 − Tk 7 (the carriage of Tk 14,000 spread over 2,000 packs) = Tk 21. Then 2,000 × Tk 21 = Tk 42,000, which agrees.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in a few lines.

An export runs from registration and agreement of terms, through a letter of credit, packing, insurance and customs clearance, to shipment and payment. An import mirrors it, with the importer opening the letter of credit and paying duty on a bill of entry. Documents carry the trade: the invoice bills, the bill of lading receipts the goods and proves ownership, the charter party hires a ship, and the letter of credit promises payment. Exporters from Bangladesh meet barriers at home, in the product and abroad, and export promotion tries to lower them.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five confusions, each stated and corrected.

Some say that a letter of credit guarantees good quality. It guarantees payment against matching papers, and nothing more. Others confuse the bill of lading with the charter party: the first receipts a load, the second hires a ship. A third error is to think that customs concerns only imports, when exports are cleared too. Another is to think that the exporter is always the shipper, when an agent may ship the goods. The last is to treat any small mismatch in papers as unimportant; the bank may refuse to pay.

## In History

> Merchants once travelled with their goods; written receipts let them stay at home.

In the late Middle Ages, merchants in the Mediterranean often sailed with their goods, or sent an agent, to guard them and to sell them. As trade grew, the need arose for a written receipt that the captain gave for goods loaded, and for a record of where they were to be delivered. These records became the bill of lading. Once the receipt could be passed from one person to another, goods could be bought and sold while still at sea, and a merchant no longer needed to sail with his cargo.
