:chapter VII | The Joint Stock Company
:mood Can a business be a person, and can its owners lose only what they paid?
:desc A company is a business that the law treats as a person of its own, owned through shares and run by directors. This chapter explains what a company is, how private and public companies differ, how one is formed, and how shares and capital work.

> A buyer of a small part of a failed business is asked to pay its whole debt.
Suppose a stranger buys a small part of a business for Tk 10,000. A year later the business fails owing Tk 50,00,000. The creditors ask the stranger to pay. Under the forms of Chapters V and VI, that stranger, as a partner, could be forced to pay all of it. Few people would take such a risk for Tk 10,000. Yet thousands of people do buy small parts of businesses every day, and sleep well.

> A second puzzle: what happens to the firm when its owner dies?
A second puzzle sits beside the first. When a sole owner dies, the firm in law ends. When a partner dies, the partnership may end. Yet some businesses have lasted for centuries, passing through hundreds of owners. How can the business stay alive while its owners come and go? One legal idea answers both puzzles. This chapter teaches it.

> This chapter builds on Chapters V and VI.
*You will need.* From Chapter V: liability, unlimited liability and capital. From Chapter VI: partnership, the profit-sharing ratio and the failure test. The bakery's figures from Chapter VI are used in the worked case.

## The Idea of a Company

### A Person Made by Law

@! Go slowly here.
> Start with a school: it can own things in its own name.
This idea is new and abstract, so we take it one step at a time. Think of a school. The school has its own name and its own bank account. It can sign a contract to buy desks. It can be sued in court. Yet the school is not any one teacher, or the headmaster, or any one student. If the headmaster retires, the school continues. In everyday life we already treat some groups as if they were persons.

@ Company | A business that the law treats as a separate person, owned by its shareholders.
@ Legal person | Something the law treats as a person: it can own property, sign contracts and be sued, apart from its members.
> The law gives a company a personality of its own.
The law does the same for a business. A **company** means a business that the law treats as a person of its own, apart from the people who own it. We call such a thing a **legal person**. A company can own property, borrow money, sign contracts and be sued, all in its own name. In Bangladesh these rules come from the Companies Act, 1994.

> The owners are now outside the firm in law; its debts are its own.
This changes who owes what. When the company borrows, the company owes the debt, not its owners. The things the company owns belong to the company, not to any owner personally. In Chapters V and VI the firm and its owners were one person, so debts reached the owners. Here they are two persons.

> You should now be able to say what a company is.
*You should now be able to say:* "A company is a person made by law, with its own property and debts, separate from the people who own it."

### Limited Liability

> The first result of separateness: the owners' risk has a ceiling.
@ Limited liability | The rule that owners are liable for a company's debts only up to the amount they agreed to pay for their shares.
Now the first puzzle can be answered. The company's debts belong to the company. So creditors may take the company's things, but they cannot take the personal property of its owners. The owners' risk stops at the money they agreed to pay for their parts of the company. We call this **limited liability**: the rule that an owner is liable for the company's debts only up to the amount agreed for his or her share.

> A small example: the stranger with Tk 10,000.
Take the stranger of the opening puzzle. The stranger paid Tk 10,000 in full for a part of a company. The company fails owing Tk 50,00,000. The stranger has already paid the full Tk 10,000, so nothing more is due. The most the stranger can lose is the Tk 10,000. The creditors, not the stranger, bear the rest of the loss.

> Pause and check: apply the ceiling to a new case.
*Pause and check.* A person holds parts of a company that cost Tk 25,000 and are fully paid. The company fails owing Tk 20,00,000 more than it owns. What is the most this person can lose? *(Answer at the back of the book.)*

> Limited liability has exceptions that matter in practice.
There are exceptions. A bank lending to a small company may ask its owners to promise personally to repay. That promise is a separate contract, and it can reach the owner's property. Also, an owner who has not yet paid the full amount agreed for his or her part must still pay that amount. The rule limits liability; it does not remove risk.

### Shares, Perpetual Life and Control

> A company divides its ownership into equal small parts.
@ Share | One of the equal parts into which a company's ownership is divided. Word in use: in daily life, a part given to each person; in business, a unit of ownership. (Some books say *stock*.)
A company divides its ownership into many equal parts. Each part is called a **share**: a unit of ownership in a company. Someone who owns shares is a shareholder. A person with 1,000 shares owns 1,000 of the small parts and has the rights that go with them. Section 4 teaches shares in detail.

> A company continues while its owners change.
A company has a continuing life. Because it is a person of its own, it does not end when a shareholder dies or sells. The shares simply pass to new owners. This is the answer to the second puzzle. Lawyers call it perpetual succession, which means that the company has no natural end except by law.

> A company is run by directors chosen by the shareholders.
Control is separate from ownership. A company may have hundreds of shareholders, so they choose a small group to run it. This group is the board of directors, taught in Section 5. Ownership belongs to the many; management belongs to the few.

:box IN SHORT
A company is a business that the law treats as a person of its own. Its owners hold shares, their liability is limited, the company continues when owners change, and directors run it.

> Recap: separate person, limited liability, shares, continuing life, directors.
*Before moving on.* A company is a legal person. Its owners hold shares, with limited liability. It continues beyond its owners and is run by directors.

## Private and Public Companies Compared

> The law divides companies into two kinds.
@ Private limited company | A company with at most fifty members that restricts the sale of its shares and may not ask the public to buy them.
@ Public limited company | A company that may ask the public to buy its shares and has at least seven members.
The Companies Act divides companies into two kinds. A **private limited company** is a company that restricts the transfer of its shares, limits its members to fifty, and may not invite the public to buy its shares.^1 A **public limited company** is any company that is not private; it may invite the public to buy shares.

> The table sets the two kinds side by side.
The table below compares the two. Read each row across: the feature, what it is for a private company, and what it is for a public company.

:table Private and public limited companies
| Feature | Private limited company | Public limited company |
| Number of members | At least two, at most fifty, not counting employees | At least seven, with no upper limit |
| Sale of shares | Restricted by the articles | Free, subject to the rules |
| Invitation to the public | Not allowed | Allowed, through a prospectus |
| Directors | At least two | At least three |
| Starting business | Certificate of incorporation is enough | Certificate of commencement also needed |

> Choose by size, capital and the wish for outside investors.
Which should a firm choose? A small family firm or a pair of friends usually chooses a private company. It keeps ownership close and needs few members. A firm that needs large capital, from many people it does not know, chooses a public company. A private company may later become public if it has seven or more members and changes its articles, as our bakery plans to do in Chapter XII.

> Recap: private is small and closed; public is large and open.
*Before moving on.* Private companies are closed: two to fifty members, restricted shares, no public invitation. Public companies are open to public investors, with at least seven members.

## How a Company Is Formed

> Forming a company requires two key documents and registration.
Forming a company is more formal than starting a sole proprietorship. A few people, who may be called promoters, plan the company, choose its name and agree its capital. They then prepare two documents and send them to the Registrar of Joint Stock Companies and Firms.

@ Memorandum of association | The company's charter: its name, office, objects, liability and capital.
> The memorandum states what the company is and may do.
The first is the **memorandum of association**. It is the company's charter. It states the company's name, the place of its registered office, the objects for which it is formed, the limit on its members' liability, and the amount of capital. The company may not go beyond the objects in the memorandum.

@ Articles of association | The company's internal rules on shares, meetings and directors.
> The articles set the internal rules of management.
The second document is the **articles of association**. These are the company's internal rules. They cover how shares are issued and moved, how meetings are held, and how directors are chosen and paid. A private company must include in its articles the three restrictions that make it private.

@ Certificate of incorporation | The Registrar's certificate that a company has been registered and now exists.
> Registration gives the company its legal birth.
If the papers are in order and the fees are paid, the Registrar issues a **certificate of incorporation**. The certificate is proof that the company has been registered. From the date on it, the company exists as a legal person. A private company may begin business at once.

@ Prospectus | A public invitation to buy a company's shares, giving facts about the company.
> A public company must invite the public through a prospectus.
A public company that wishes to ask the public for money must issue a **prospectus**: a public invitation to buy shares. It tells readers what the company plans to do, who its directors are, how much capital it needs, how it will use the money, and what the risks are. The aim is to give buyers enough facts to judge.

@ Certificate of commencement | The Registrar's second certificate that a public company may begin business.
> A public company needs a second certificate before it may start.
A public company needs one more step. After incorporation it must satisfy the Registrar that the conditions of the law are met, and the Registrar then issues a **certificate of commencement** of business.^2 Only then may it start. In short, incorporation is the birth of the company, and commencement is its permission to begin trade.

> Recap: promoters, memorandum, articles, incorporation, then prospectus and commencement for public companies.
*Before moving on.* Formation needs a memorandum, articles and registration. A public company also issues a prospectus and obtains a certificate of commencement.

## Shares and Capital

### Kinds of Shares

> A share has a face value and brings a right to dividend.
@ Par value | The amount printed on a share, also called its face value.
@ Dividend | The part of a company's profit that is paid to shareholders.
Each share carries a printed value, the **par value**. The bakery's shares will have a par value of Tk 10. A company that has earned profit may pay part of it to its shareholders. This payment is the **dividend**. A company is not forced to pay a dividend each year; the directors decide how much to pay and how much to keep.

> Two main kinds of share: ordinary and preference.
@ Ordinary share | A share whose holder has a vote and receives whatever profit remains.
@ Preference share | A share whose holder is paid a fixed dividend before ordinary shareholders.
Shares are of two main kinds. **Ordinary shares** give the holder a vote at meetings and a share of what remains of the profit after all other claims. The dividend varies: it may be large in a good year and nothing in a poor one. **Preference shares** give the holder a dividend at a fixed rate, paid before any ordinary dividend. In return the holder usually has no vote. Preference suits a person who wants steadier income and less risk.

> Dividend per share is the total dividend divided by the number of shares.
Dividend per share is found by dividing the total dividend by the number of shares. *Worked example.* A company has 2,00,000 shares and declares a total dividend of Tk 4,00,000. Given: dividend Tk 4,00,000; shares 2,00,000. Find: dividend per share. Steps: Tk 4,00,000 ÷ 2,00,000 = Tk 2. Answer: Tk 2 per share, which is 20 per cent of a Tk 10 par value. Check: Tk 2 × 2,00,000 shares = Tk 4,00,000.

### Kinds of Capital

> A company's capital has three names, and each means something different.
@ Authorised capital | The largest amount of share capital a company may issue, as set in its memorandum.
@ Issued capital | The value of the shares that the company has actually issued.
@ Paid-up capital | The amount of the issued capital that shareholders have paid so far.
A company's capital is described in three ways. **Authorised capital** is the largest amount of share capital the company may issue, as stated in its memorandum. **Issued capital** is the amount of shares actually issued to shareholders. **Paid-up capital** is the amount shareholders have actually paid so far. The three need not be equal. A company may authorise much and issue little.

> The table shows the three capitals using our bakery.
Our bakery will become a private limited company on 1 January 2027, and the table shows its capital. The authorised capital is 5,00,000 shares of Tk 10, which is Tk 50,00,000. The partners are issued 2,00,000 shares at par and pay in full.

:table The bakery's capital on 1 January 2027
| Kind of capital | Shares | Amount |
| Authorised | 5,00,000 | Tk 50,00,000 |
| Issued | 2,00,000 | Tk 20,00,000 |
| Paid-up | 2,00,000 | Tk 20,00,000 |

> The unissued room can raise more capital later.
Note that Tk 30,00,000 of the authorised capital is not yet issued. This is room to grow. If the bakery needs more capital for a second outlet, the directors can issue more shares up to the limit, without changing the memorandum.

## Merits, Demerits and Management

### Management and Control

@ Board of directors | The group of people, chosen by shareholders, that manages a company.
> Shareholders choose a board, which runs the company.
The shareholders meet at least once a year and elect a **board of directors**: the group that manages the company. The board decides policy, appoints senior managers, and reports to the shareholders. Small companies often have the same people as shareholders and directors. Large companies keep the two groups apart.

> Control follows shares: more shares means more votes.
Control follows shares. Each ordinary share usually carries one vote, so those who hold more than half the votes can pass ordinary decisions. In our bakery, the original owner holds 1,20,000 of 2,00,000 shares, which is 60 per cent, and so controls an ordinary vote. The new partner holds 40 per cent.

### Merits and Demerits

> The merits are limited liability, large capital and a continuing life.
The merits are clear. Liability is limited, so people dare to invest. Many investors can pool large capital. The life of the company continues beyond its owners. Shares of a public company can be sold to others. And the board can hire skilled managers. These strengths explain why most large firms in the world are companies.

> The demerits are cost, rules, and the gap between owners and managers.
The demerits are real too. Forming and running a company costs more and needs more paperwork. Public companies must publish reports and follow many rules. Owners and managers may have different aims, and managers may serve their own interest. A shareholder with a small holding has little power. And creditors may be wary of lending to a company with few things of its own.

> Recap: directors manage; shares give votes; limited liability is the key merit.
*Before moving on.* Shareholders elect the board of directors. Votes follow shares. The main merit is limited liability; the main demerits are cost and the gap between owners and managers.

## Worked Case: The Bakery Becomes a Company

> The case file records the new capital and the shares.
:case Case file, illustrative. On 1 January 2027 the bakery becomes a private limited company. Authorised capital is Tk 50,00,000, divided into 5,00,000 shares of Tk 10. Two lakh shares are issued at par to the partners: 1,20,000 and 80,000. Total issued capital is Tk 20,00,000. For tests only, we suppose that the company later declared a total dividend of Tk 4,00,000, and, in a separate supposition, that it failed with things worth Tk 2,00,000 and debts of Tk 6,00,000.^3

> The questions concern shares, dividend and the failure.
*Question.* Do the shares agree with the capital of Chapter VI? What would each holder receive as dividend? If the company failed as supposed, who bears the shortfall?

> The shares agree with the partners' capital.
*Reasoning, step 1: the shares.* 1,20,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 12,00,000. 80,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 8,00,000. Together 2,00,000 shares = Tk 20,00,000, the same as the partners' capital in Chapter VI. The ratio 1,20,000 : 80,000 is 3:2, so ownership is unchanged.

> The dividend per share is Tk 2, and each holder receives it on each share.
*Step 2: the dividend.* Dividend per share = Tk 4,00,000 ÷ 2,00,000 = Tk 2. The first holder receives 1,20,000 × Tk 2 = Tk 2,40,000. The second holder receives 80,000 × Tk 2 = Tk 1,60,000. The sum is Tk 4,00,000.

> In the failure, creditors bear the shortfall, not the shareholders.
*Step 3: the failure.* The shortfall is Tk 6,00,000 − Tk 2,00,000 = Tk 4,00,000. The shares were issued fully paid, so shareholders owe nothing more. The creditors cannot claim the shareholders' personal property, so they lose Tk 4,00,000. In Chapter VI the same failure fell on the partners' own property.

> Limited liability protects the owners and shifts risk to creditors.
*Conclusion.* The company protects its owners from the debts, and shifts the risk to creditors. For this reason lenders to small companies often ask for a personal promise from the owners.

> The check adds the parts to the totals.
*Check.* Shares: Tk 12,00,000 + Tk 8,00,000 = Tk 20,00,000. Dividend: Tk 2,40,000 + Tk 1,60,000 = Tk 4,00,000. Both agree with the case file.

### Summary

> The whole chapter in a few lines.
A company is a business that the law treats as a person of its own. Its owners hold shares, have limited liability, and cannot lose more than they agreed to pay. The company continues when owners change and is run by a board of directors. Private companies are small and closed; public companies may invite the public to buy shares. A company is formed with a memorandum and articles, and registered by a certificate of incorporation; a public company also needs a prospectus and a certificate of commencement. Shares are ordinary or preference. Capital is authorised, issued or paid up.

### Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: thinking limited liability means no loss.
Many students write that limited liability means the owner cannot lose. This is wrong. The owner may lose the whole amount paid for the shares. What is limited is the loss beyond that amount.

> Mistake two: confusing authorised capital with money in hand.
Another error is to treat authorised capital as money the company has. It is only the limit of what may be issued. A company with Tk 50,00,000 authorised and Tk 20,00,000 issued has raised, at most, Tk 20,00,000 from shares.

> Mistake three: thinking public means owned by the government.
A third error is to think that a public company is owned by the state. It is not. *Public* means that the company may invite the public to buy its shares. Companies owned by the government are another matter, taught in Chapter VIII.

### In History

> The Dutch East India Company of 1602 sold shares to the public.
In 1602 a group of Dutch merchants combined their separate trading ventures into one body, the United East India Company, chartered by the Dutch government. Instead of a voyage-by-voyage venture, the company had capital that lasted from year to year, and ordinary people could buy shares in it. The shares could be sold on to others. This gave the company a continuing life that no single merchant's firm had, and showed that many savers could fund a large enterprise together. Modern companies grew from this idea.^4
