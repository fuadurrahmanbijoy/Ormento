:mood Why does a business exist, what does it use, and where should it stand?
:desc This chapter explains the results that a business works to reach, the four kinds of resource it uses to reach them, and the choice of a place in which to work. It ends by putting the three together in one plan.

> Three friends give three different reasons for opening one bakery.

Three friends stand in front of the same empty shop in Dhaka and each says what a bakery there would be for. The first says, "To earn a good living." The second says, "To give the neighbourhood clean bread at a fair price." The third says, "To give work to people who cannot find any." All three speak sincerely, and all three are right. Yet if one bakery tries to do all three things at once, its owner will meet hard choices. Cheaper bread lowers the profit. More workers raise the cost.

> Before any baking, two more questions wait: what to use, and where to stand.

Before anyone bakes a loaf, two more questions wait. What must the bakery have: ovens, flour, a shop, workers, money? And where should it stand: in this lane, or in the next district? The first question is about the results a business wants and the resources it uses. The second is about place. This chapter answers both, and then joins the answers into one plan.

> What this chapter assumes from Chapter I.

You will need four ideas from Chapter I: business, profit, risk and entrepreneur. They are explained in Sections 1 and 2 of that chapter. Every new word in this chapter is explained when it first appears, as before.

> The bakery sets its first targets.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 12 January 2026 the person sets targets for the bakery. The target is a monthly profit of Tk 60,000. The plan is to rent a shop of 600 sq ft at Tk 45,000 a month,^1 to hire four staff and to start with Tk 12,00,000 of the person's own savings.^2 This money for the business is called capital, which Section 2 explains. Three possible sites have been compared, and Dhanmondi is chosen. The chapter shows how that choice was made.

## Aims of Business

> A business works toward results, which are called aims.

@ Aim | A result that a business works to reach.

Every business works toward some results, whether its owner has written them down or not. The three friends in front of the shop were stating results. We call such a result an **aim**, which means a result that a business works to reach. Another word for it is *objective*, but this book uses *aim* throughout. A bakery may have the aim of earning Tk 60,000 a month. It may also have the aim of paying every worker on the first day of the month.

> Aims fall into four groups: economic, social, human and national.

Aims come in many forms, but they fall into four main groups. Economic aims concern money. Social aims concern the customers and the community around the business. Human aims concern the people who work in it. National aims concern the country as a whole. We take the groups one at a time, and a table afterwards puts them side by side.

### Economic Aims

> Economic aims are profit, survival and growth.

An aim is *economic* when it concerns the money a business earns and the size it reaches. The first economic aim is profit, which Chapter I explained as the money left from sales after all costs are paid. The second is survival, which means earning enough to stay open from one year to the next. The third is growth, which means becoming larger: more customers, more goods or more shops. A business can reach one of these and miss another. A bakery can survive for years at a small size and a small profit, and never grow.

> Profit comes first in time, because survival and growth depend on it.

Profit comes first in order, because survival and growth depend on it. A business that makes a loss every month uses up its money and closes. A business that makes only a thin profit has little to spend on a second shop. This is why the economic aim of profit appears in the very meaning of business. It does not follow that profit is the only aim, and the next groups show why.

### Social Aims

> Social aims are about customers and the community.

A business sells to people and works among people, so it has aims toward them. These are *social* aims. They include selling goods that are safe and of the quality promised, charging fair prices, giving honest weights and treating neighbours with respect. A bakery that sells stale bread as fresh may earn more this week, but it breaks a social aim and, in time, loses its customers. Chapter IV studies these duties in full. For now, hold in mind that a business belongs to a community, and that the community can reward or punish it.

### Human Aims

> Human aims are about the people who work in the business.

The people who work in a business are not only a cost. They have needs of their own, and a business can set aims for them. These are *human* aims. They include fair pay, safe working conditions, a chance to learn new skills and respectful treatment. A baker who works at a hot oven with a safe floor and a fair wage will usually work better and stay longer. A human aim therefore does good to the worker first, and often to the owner afterwards.

### National Aims

> National aims serve the country, through jobs, taxes and trade.

A large number of businesses together shape a country, so a business may also set aims for the nation. These are *national* aims. They include creating jobs, paying taxes on time, making goods at home that would otherwise be bought from abroad, and earning money for the country by selling goods abroad. A single small bakery contributes a little to each of these, by paying wages and taxes. Chapters IX and X explain the trade between nations that lies behind the last two.

The table below compares the four groups of aims. Read one row at a time. Each row names a group, says what it seeks, says who gains most and gives an example from the bakery.

### When Aims Conflict

> Aims can pull against one another, even when each is good.

Each aim looks reasonable on its own. Trouble arrives when two of them pull in opposite directions. Suppose the bakery can buy a cheaper grade of flour that makes slightly poorer bread. The economic aim says to buy it, because costs fall and profit rises. The social aim says not to, because customers deserve the quality promised. Or suppose the owner can raise every worker's wage. The human aim says yes, but the economic aim says no, because costs rise and profit falls.

> The same conflict looks different from each viewpoint.

The conflict looks different from each seat. The owner sees profit falling. The customer sees bread getting worse. The worker sees pay staying low. None of them is wrong. A business owner must therefore decide how much of each aim to give up for the sake of the others. Many owners find that the choice is smaller in the long run than it seems today, because honest goods and fair wages bring loyal customers and loyal staff, and these protect profit. Yet in the short run the conflict is real, and a good owner admits it. Chapter IV returns to this question.

## Factors of Production

> A single loaf contains the work of land, people, tools and an owner.

Look at one loaf. It was made from flour, and the flour came from wheat that grew in the ground. A baker kneaded the dough with her hands. An oven baked it, and the oven was bought with money saved for the purpose. Behind all of this stood a person who decided to bake, found the shop, hired the baker and accepted the risk of a loss. A loaf of bread is a small bundle of four different contributions. Economists give each contribution a name, and the names have lasted for more than two hundred years.

> The four contributions are called factors of production.

@ Factor of production | A resource used to make goods or provide services.

A **factor of production** means a resource that a business uses to make goods or to provide services. There are four: land, labour, capital and enterprise.^3 Each is paid for being used, and each has its own name for its payment. This section takes the four one at a time. Go slowly, because the rest of the book keeps returning to these ideas.

### Land

@! Go slowly here.
> Land in business includes every gift of nature a business uses.

@ Land | Word in use. In daily life, ground. In business, all gifts of nature that a business uses: ground, water, minerals and forests.

Begin with something familiar. A farmer's field gives wheat, a river gives fish and a quarry gives stone. None of these was made by a person. Business gives such gifts of nature one name: **land**, which means all the gifts of nature that a business uses, including ground, water, minerals and forests. Notice that the word has a wider sense here than in daily life. A fishing business uses river water as land, even though no ground is involved. A bakery uses a site to stand on.

> The payment for land is called rent.

@ Rent | Payment for the use of land or buildings.

The owner of land lets a business use it and receives a payment. This payment is **rent**, which means payment for the use of land or buildings. The bakery's shop is the nearest example. It stands on ground and it is a building, and the person pays Tk 45,000 a month for it. Strictly, a building was made by people and is therefore capital, the third factor. When it is let out, however, its payment is still called rent, and this book treats the site and the building together under land and rent.

### Labour

@! Go slowly here.
> Labour is human work of body or mind, and its payment is wages.

@ Labour | Human work, of body or mind, used to make goods or provide services.
@ Wages | Payment for labour.

The second factor is people. **Labour** means human work, of body or mind, used to make goods or provide services. The baker who kneads dough uses her body. The person who plans recipes and sets prices uses her mind. Both are labour. The payment for labour is **wages**, which means payment for labour, whether called a wage, a salary or by another name.

> Labour is not only the work of employees; an owner may supply it too.

A business pays wages to those who work for it, but labour is not limited to employees. An owner who bakes beside her staff is also supplying labour, and so she supplies more than one factor. This is common in small businesses. It means that one person can appear in two rows of the same table.

### Capital

@! Go slowly here.
> Capital is money and the things money buys for use in the business.

@ Capital | Word in use. In daily life, the chief city of a country, or a large letter. In business, money and the things it buys for use in the business.

The third factor is **capital**, which means money and the things bought with money that a business uses to produce, such as ovens, tools and a stock of flour. Take care with the word. In daily life, *capital* may mean the chief city of a country, as Dhaka is of Bangladesh. In business it means something quite different: the money put into the business and the equipment that money has bought. The bakery's capital will be its Tk 12,00,000 of savings and, once they are bought, its ovens and trays.

> The payment for the use of borrowed capital is interest.

@ Interest | Word in use. In daily life, curiosity or a hobby. In business, payment for the use of borrowed money.

Capital can be the owner's own, or it can be borrowed from a bank or another lender. A lender gives up the use of her money for a time and asks a payment in return. This payment is **interest**, which means payment for the use of borrowed money. In daily life the word has another meaning, such as a person's interest in music. Here it is a payment, usually stated as a share of the sum borrowed for each year. If the bakery borrowed Tk 1,00,000 and agreed to pay Tk 10,000 a year for the loan, the Tk 10,000 would be interest.

### Enterprise

> Enterprise is the work of bringing the factors together and bearing the risk.

@ Enterprise | The work of bringing the other factors together and bearing the risk.

The fourth factor is **enterprise**, which means the work of bringing the other factors together and bearing the risk. Chapter I called the person who does this work the entrepreneur. The entrepreneur finds the shop, hires the baker, buys the oven and accepts that sales may not cover the cost. The other three factors are paid whether the business succeeds or not, in most cases. The entrepreneur is different, because her reward depends on what is left.

> The reward of enterprise is profit, which is not fixed in advance.

The reward of enterprise is profit, which Chapter I defined as the money left from sales after all costs are paid. Look again at what that means. Rent, wages and interest are usually agreed before the business starts. Profit is not agreed. It is whatever remains, and it can be large, small or even negative, which is a loss. This is why enterprise is the riskiest of the four factors, and why the entrepreneur is rewarded with the uncertain part.

### The Four Factors and Their Rewards

> A table gathers the factors, their meanings and their rewards.

The table gathers the four factors with their rewards. Read each row from left to right: the factor, what it means, its reward and an example from the bakery.

:table Factors of production and their rewards
| Factor | Meaning | Reward | Example in the bakery |
| Land | Gifts of nature, site, buildings | Rent | The rented shop at Tk 45,000 a month |
| Labour | Human work of body or mind | Wages | Four staff who bake and sell |
| Capital | Money and equipment bought with it | Interest | Tk 12,00,000 of savings, then ovens and trays |
| Enterprise | Bringing factors together; bearing risk | Profit | The person who starts the bakery |

> Reading the table: sort real facts into the four rows.

The table is a tool, and the best way to learn a tool is to use it. The bakery's facts on 12 January fall into the four rows. The rented shop is land, and its reward is rent. The four staff are labour, and their reward is wages. The savings are capital. The person who sets the targets and takes the risk supplies enterprise, and her reward is the profit, if any. If part of the savings were borrowed from a bank, the bank would earn interest and the owner would receive what remained.

> The factors are combined in a mix, and the mix can change.

A business combines the four factors in some proportion, which is called its mix. The mix can change. A bakery may knead dough by hand, which uses much labour and little capital, or by machine, which uses more capital and less labour. Which is better depends on the price of each factor. Where wages are low and machines are costly, hand work may pay. Where wages rise, a machine may become the cheaper choice. The owner therefore watches the prices of all four factors, and not the profit alone.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* A factor of production is a resource used to make goods or provide services. There are four: land, labour, capital and enterprise. Their rewards are rent, wages, interest and profit. Profit is the only one not fixed in advance.

## The Location of a Business

> A baker and a sugar maker would not choose the same kind of place.

Would a bread baker and a sugar maker choose the same kind of place? Fresh bread goes stale within a day, so a baker wants her shop near the people who will eat it. Sugar is made from cane, and cane is heavy and spoils soon after cutting, so a sugar maker wants his mill near the fields. The two make different choices for good reasons, and the reasons are what this section teaches.

> Location means the place where a business is set up.

@ Location | The place where a business is set up.
@ Plant | The buildings, land and machines where goods are made. Not a living plant.

The place where a business is set up is its **location**. For a factory, the place of the factory itself is called the **plant**, which means the buildings, land and machines where goods are made. Do not confuse it with a living plant, which has the same spelling. A shop has a location too, though it has no plant, because it makes nothing. Choosing a location is important because it is hard to change. A bad shop can be repainted, but a bad place stays bad, and moving costs money.

### Factors in the Choice of Location

> Nearness to customers matters most for goods that spoil or for shops.

Several things help a business choose a place. The first is nearness to customers. A shop that sells to people who pass on foot must stand where people pass. A bakery that sells goods that go stale quickly gains most from this. Customers will not travel far for a loaf.

> Nearness to materials matters when materials are heavy or spoil.

The second is nearness to materials. A business that uses heavy or fast-spoiling materials saves cost by standing near them. A rice mill near the paddy fields pays little to carry the paddy. A mill far away pays a great deal. By contrast, a business that uses light, long-lasting materials may stand anywhere.

> Labour supply and transport matter to all businesses in different ways.

The third is the supply of labour. A business needs workers with the right skills, and it needs them nearby, since few people will travel far each day for a modest wage. The fourth is transport. Roads, railways and rivers carry materials in and goods out. A business with poor transport pays more for every journey and delivers more slowly.

> Power, water and the cost of land also shape the choice.

The fifth is power and water. A factory needs a steady supply of electricity, gas or water, and a site without them may cost more to serve than it saves in rent. The sixth is the cost of land, which appears as the rent or the price of the site. A central site costs more, because many businesses want it, and a distant site costs less.

> Rules and help from the government can also decide.

The seventh is the set of rules and help from the government. Some places forbid certain kinds of business, to protect homes or the environment. Other places offer special help, such as lower taxes, to attract business. Chapter XII describes the special zones that the government of Bangladesh has set aside for export factories. The eighth is room to grow. A site that cannot hold a second oven will limit the business later.

### Choosing Among Sites: The Weighted Comparison

> No site is best on every factor, so a method is needed to compare them.

In practice no site is best on every factor. One is close to customers but costly. Another is cheap but far from workers. The owner must choose, and a careless owner is likely to follow the factor most easily seen. A method helps. The method used here is the weighted comparison, which asks the owner to say how much each factor matters and then to score each site on each factor.

> The method in words: weight each factor, score each site, add.

In words, the method has three steps. First, give each factor a weight, which is a number showing how much that factor matters. The weights of all factors add up to 10, so the most important factor takes the largest share. Second, score each site on each factor from 1 to 5, where 5 is best. Third, multiply each score by its weight and add the results. The site with the highest total is the best by this method. In symbols, the weighted score of a site is the sum, over all factors, of weight times score.

> A first, clean example: a tailor chooses between two sites.

We now follow the method with small, clean numbers. *Given:* a tailor compares two sites, X and Y. She uses three factors with these weights: customers nearby 5, rent 3 and ease of access 2. The weights add up to 10. *Find:* which site has the higher weighted score. *Scores:* Site X scores 4 on customers, 3 on rent and 3 on access. Site Y scores 2, 5 and 4. For rent, a low rent earns a high score.

:table A tailor compares two sites
| Factor | Weight | Site X score | Site X weighted | Site Y score | Site Y weighted |
| Customers nearby | 5 | 4 | 20 | 2 | 10 |
| Rent | 3 | 3 | 9 | 5 | 15 |
| Ease of access | 2 | 3 | 6 | 4 | 8 |
| Total | 10 | | 35 | | 33 |

> Reading the table, and the answer to the tailor's problem.

*Steps and answer.* For Site X: 5 × 4 = 20, then 3 × 3 = 9, then 2 × 3 = 6, and 20 + 9 + 6 = 35. For Site Y: 5 × 2 = 10, then 3 × 5 = 15, then 2 × 4 = 8, and 10 + 15 + 8 = 33. Site X, with 35, scores higher than Site Y, with 33. *Check.* The weights add up to 10, and the greatest possible total is 10 × 5 = 50. Both totals are below 50, as they must be, and Site X leads because the tailor values customers most.

> A new setting: a rice mill, where materials matter most.

Now change the setting. A rice mill has different weights, because paddy is heavy and costs much to carry. Suppose the weights are paddy nearby 4, transport to buyers 3, power supply 2 and customers nearby 1. Site P, in the farming district, scores 5, 3, 2 and 2. Site Q, in the city, scores 2, 4, 5 and 4. Site P gives 20 + 9 + 4 + 2 = 35 and Site Q gives 8 + 12 + 10 + 4 = 34. The farm site wins narrowly, because paddy has the heaviest weight. The same method gave a city answer for the tailor and a farm answer for the mill, because the weights differ.

> The method is a help to judgement, not a replacement for it.

The method has limits. The scores are judgements, not measurements. Two careful people may score the same site differently. The weights are also judgements, and a different set of weights can give a different winner, as the worked case at the end of this chapter shows. The method does not decide for the owner. It makes the owner's reasons plain, so that they can be examined and argued about before the money is spent.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* The location is the place where a business is set up. The main factors are nearness to customers and to materials, labour supply, transport, power and water, cost of land, government rules and room to grow. The weighted comparison scores each site on each factor, multiplies by the weight and adds.

## Putting Resources, Place and Aims Together

> The three parts of this chapter must fit one another.

The three parts of this chapter must fit together. The aims say what the business is trying to do. The factors say what it must have. The location says where it will stand. A plan is sound only when the three agree. If the aim is a profit of Tk 60,000 a month and the shop's rent is Tk 45,000, we should ask at once whether the shop can earn enough to pay for both.

> A first test: sales must cover rent and the target profit together.

@! Go slowly here.
We can make a first test with the figures from 12 January. Profit is sales minus costs, so sales must equal the costs plus the profit. The only cost fixed so far is the rent of Tk 45,000 a month. Add the target profit of Tk 60,000, and we reach Tk 1,05,000. The bakery must therefore sell at least Tk 1,05,000 of goods each month, and that is before it pays for flour, fuel and the four staff. Those costs will push the figure higher. This is not a forecast. It is only a quick test that shows the target is not trivial.

> The aims change the test: higher wages or lower prices raise the sales needed.

The other aims change the test. Suppose the human aim is to pay each worker Tk 2,000 more each month. With four staff this adds Tk 8,000 to the costs, and the sales needed rise by Tk 8,000 for the same profit. Suppose the social aim is to keep bread cheap. Then each loaf brings less money, and more loaves must be sold to reach the same sales. Each aim therefore has a price, and the plan must say who pays it. Sometimes the owner pays by accepting less profit. Sometimes the customers pay by buying at a slightly higher price.

> The owner reconciles aims by stating them, pricing them and ranking them.

To reconcile aims, an owner can follow three habits. First, state each aim clearly in a sentence, so that no one is guessing. Second, put a price on each, by asking what it costs per month. Third, rank them, so that when two conflict the owner knows which gives way. The ranking is not permanent. It can change as the business grows and earns more. A new business often puts survival first and adds social and human aims as the profit allows.

> A short recap of the section.

*Before moving on.* A sound plan fits its aims, its factors and its location together. A quick test is that sales must at least cover the costs plus the target profit. Each extra aim has a price, and the owner must decide who pays it.

## Worked Case: Choosing the Bakery's Site

> The situation: three sites, four factors and three sets of figures.

:case *Case file, illustrative.* *Situation.* In the days before 12 January 2026 the person compares three sites for the bakery, each of 600 sq ft. Site A, in Dhanmondi, rents at Tk 45,000 a month. Site B rents at Tk 30,000 and Site C at Tk 35,000. The person judges four factors: customers nearby, rent, access for supplies and deliveries, and few rival bakeries. The weights are 4, 3, 2 and 1, which add up to 10. Scores run from 1 to 5, and the scores are the person's own judgement.^4

> The question: which site has the highest weighted score?

*Question.* Which of the three sites has the highest weighted score, and how sure can the person be of the answer?

> The reasoning multiplies each score by its weight and adds.

*Reasoning.* The person scores each site on each factor. Site A scores 5 for customers, 2 for rent, 4 for access and 3 for rivals. Site B scores 3, 5, 3 and 3. Site C scores 3, 4, 2 and 4. For rent, a lower rent earns a higher score, and for rivals, fewer rivals earn a higher score. Each score is multiplied by its weight, and the products are added down each column of the table. In the table each cell shows the score and, in brackets, the weighted figure.

:table Weighted comparison of three bakery sites
| Factor | Weight | Site A | Site B | Site C |
| Customers nearby | 4 | 5 (20) | 3 (12) | 3 (12) |
| Rent | 3 | 2 (6) | 5 (15) | 4 (12) |
| Access | 2 | 4 (8) | 3 (6) | 2 (4) |
| Few rivals | 1 | 3 (3) | 3 (3) | 4 (4) |
| Total | 10 | 37 | 36 | 32 |

> The conclusion: Site A leads, but only by one point.

*Conclusion.* Site A scores 20 + 6 + 8 + 3 = 37. Site B scores 12 + 15 + 6 + 3 = 36. Site C scores 12 + 12 + 4 + 4 = 32. Site A, in Dhanmondi, has the highest total and is chosen. Its lead over Site B is only one point, so the person should not be very sure.

> The check: weights, maximum and a test of how sensitive the result is.

*Check.* First, the weights add up: 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10, so the greatest possible total is 10 × 5 = 50, and all three totals lie below it. Second, test how sensitive the result is. Suppose the person cared more about rent and less about customers, with weights of 3, 4, 2 and 1. Site A would then score 15 + 8 + 8 + 3 = 34, Site B would score 9 + 20 + 6 + 3 = 38 and Site C 9 + 16 + 4 + 4 = 33. Site B would win. The choice of Site A therefore depends on the person's belief that customers matter more than rent, which is reasonable, since a profit target of Tk 60,000 needs large sales. The person records this reason and goes ahead with Dhanmondi.

## Summary

> The chapter in brief.

An aim is a result a business works to reach, and aims fall into four groups: economic, social, human and national. Aims can conflict, and each has a price. A business uses four factors of production: land, paid rent; labour, paid wages; capital, paid interest; and enterprise, paid profit. Profit is the only reward not fixed in advance. The location is the place where a business is set up, and the plant is the buildings and machines where goods are made. The main factors in choosing a location are customers, materials, labour, transport, power and water, cost of land, government rules and room to grow. The weighted comparison scores each site, multiplies by the weights and adds. A sound plan fits aims, factors and location together.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: believing profit is the only aim.

*"A business has one aim, which is profit."* Profit matters most in order of time, but a business also has social, human and national aims. A firm that follows profit and ignores the rest often loses its customers and its staff.

> Mistake two: treating capital as cash only.

*"Capital means cash."* Capital is money and also the things money buys for use in the business, such as ovens, tools and stock. Cash is only one part of it.

> Mistake three: calling every payment for land by one name.

*"Rent, wages and interest are all the same."* They are three different payments for three different factors. Rent is paid for land, wages for labour and interest for borrowed capital.

> Mistake four: thinking the cheapest site is the best.

*"The site with the lowest rent is the best."* Rent is only one factor. A cheap site far from customers may cost more in lost sales than it saves in rent.

> Mistake five: treating profit as fixed pay.

*"Profit is paid like a wage."* Wages are agreed in advance. Profit is whatever remains after all costs, and it may be large, small or negative.

## In History: The Factors Named

> Early writers divided the production of a nation among three classes.

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, writers who studied how nations grow rich asked a simple question: who shares in what a country produces? Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations*, published in 1776, divided the income of a nation among landowners, workers and the owners of stock, which meant the money and goods used to produce more. David Ricardo, who wrote later, used the same three divisions to ask how a nation's income is shared. They are the land, labour and capital of this chapter, with rent, wages and profit as their rewards.

> Later economists added the organiser as a fourth factor.

Later economists, among them Alfred Marshall, gave a place to the organiser who brings the other factors together, and this led to the fourth factor in this chapter, enterprise. The idea has proved useful for more than two centuries because it turns a loose question, "who deserves what?", into a clear table with four rows. When we ask how a bakery shares its sales among the owner of the shop, the baker, the lender and the entrepreneur, we are using a way of thinking that these writers began.

^1: The sign sq ft means square feet. A shop of 600 sq ft measures about 56 square metres.

^2: Bangladesh groups large numbers as 12,00,000, which is twelve lakh. This book uses that grouping for taka. In the grouping used in many other countries the same sum is written 1,200,000.

^3: Some writers count only three factors and include enterprise within labour. This book counts four, because the entrepreneur does a different job: bearing the risk.

^4: The scores are judgements, not measurements. Two careful people may score a site differently. What matters is that the reasons are written down.
