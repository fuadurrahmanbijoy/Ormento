:chapter XII | Markets and Institutions that Support Business
:mood Where do strangers meet to share the ownership of a firm?
:desc A business does not stand alone. It raises money in markets, follows rules set by regulators, speaks to government through trade bodies, and is helped to sell abroad by special zones and agencies. This chapter describes the share market and the main institutions that support business in Bangladesh.

> Money for growth must come from people who have never seen the business.

The bakery owners want to open a second outlet. It will cost about Tk 10,00,000, which is more than the partners can find from their own savings and more than a bank will lend on easy terms. They think of selling small slices of the company to many people. But who will buy a slice of a bakery they have never visited? And if a buyer later needs the money back, to whom can she sell her slice? The puzzle has two halves: how a company finds new owners, and how those owners can later leave. Markets and institutions exist to answer both.

## The Share Market and the Stock Exchange

@ Share market | The market in which shares are issued and then bought and sold.

> The share market has two parts: new shares, and shares already issued.

Think first of a bicycle. A factory sells a new bicycle to a customer. Later the customer sells the used bicycle to a neighbour. The factory is paid only the first time; the second sale is between two customers. Shares behave in the same way. The **share market** is the market in which shares are issued and then bought and sold. It has two parts. The *primary market* is where a company sells new shares and receives the money. The *secondary market* is where people who already own shares sell them to others, and the company receives nothing.

@ Stock exchange | An organised market, run under rules, where listed shares are bought and sold through licensed brokers.

> A stock exchange is an organised place with rules for trading shares.

You might sell a used bicycle by shouting across a street, but shares need more care, since a buyer must be sure the seller owns the shares and that payment will be made. A **stock exchange** is an organised market, run under rules, where listed shares are bought and sold through licensed brokers. A **broker** is a licensed person or firm that buys and sells shares for clients and earns a fee. The investor does not walk onto the exchange; she gives an order to a broker, and the broker carries it out.

@ Listing | The admission of a company's shares to trading on a stock exchange, after it meets the exchange's rules.

> Only companies that pass the exchange's tests may have their shares traded.

A company cannot simply announce that its shares are for sale on the exchange. It must apply, show its accounts and its plans, and meet conditions on size, record and honesty. If it passes, its shares are admitted to trading, which is called **listing**. A listed company is also bound to publish its accounts and important news. The conditions protect buyers, who can trust that a listed company has been checked.

> The main stock exchange in Bangladesh is the Dhaka Stock Exchange.

@ DSE | Dhaka Stock Exchange, the main stock exchange of Bangladesh, in Dhaka.

The main stock exchange in Bangladesh is the **Dhaka Stock Exchange (DSE)**. A second exchange operates in Chattogram.^1 The DSE is itself a company, and it works under the Companies Act and the securities law of the country. Its functions are easy to list. It provides a place where shares can be bought and sold at any time, so that owners can turn shares into money. It sets prices through the meeting of buyers and sellers. It enforces rules of honest dealing on its members. It requires listed companies to publish information. It helps companies to raise money by giving buyers confidence. And its price index gives a rough picture of how investors feel about the economy.

^1: The Chittagong Stock Exchange began in 1995.

@! Go slowly here.

> The price of a share on the exchange is not its par value.

Recall from Chapter VII that a share has a par value, such as Tk 10. On the exchange the price changes with demand and supply. If many buyers want a share and few sellers will sell, the price rises; if the opposite, it falls. A Tk 10 share may sell for Tk 40 if investors expect the company to earn well. Here is a small example. *Given.* An investor buys 500 shares at Tk 40 and later sells them at Tk 46. *Find.* The gain. *Steps.* Gain per share = Tk 46 − Tk 40 = Tk 6. Total gain = 500 × Tk 6 = Tk 3,000. *Answer.* The gain is Tk 3,000, which is 15 per cent of the Tk 20,000 paid. *Check.* Tk 3,000 ÷ Tk 20,000 = 0.15.

> A share's journey shows who is paid at each step.

Follow one share. The bakery company sells it for Tk 10 to Rahima in the primary market. The bakery receives Tk 10. Two years later, Rahima sells it through a broker on the exchange to Karim for Tk 14. Rahima gains Tk 4. The bakery receives nothing from this second sale, though Karim is now its owner and will receive any dividend. The journey shows why a company welcomes a busy secondary market: buyers in the primary market are more willing to subscribe when they know they can later sell.

## Issuing Shares to the Public and the Regulator

@ IPO | Initial public offering: the first sale of a company's shares to the public.

> The first sale of shares to the public is called an IPO.

When a company sells its shares to the public for the first time, the sale is an **IPO**, short for *initial public offering*. The steps follow the formation steps of Chapter VII, with extra care. The board decides how many shares to sell and at what price. It prepares a *prospectus*, the public invitation described in Chapter VII, giving the facts about the company, its plans and its risks. It then applies for approval, and the approving body checks the papers.^2 After approval the public may apply for shares. If applications exceed shares offered, the shares are shared out by a fair method. The shares are then listed on the exchange.

^2: Companies are often helped in this by a merchant bank acting as issue manager. The rules for pricing, for the share of the offer kept for small investors, and for the method of sharing out change from time to time; check the current rules.

> A worked example shows the sharing out when applications exceed shares.

An example follows. *Given.* A company offers 1,00,000 shares at Tk 10 each. Applications arrive for 2,50,000 shares. *Find.* The money raised, and the share of each application met if shares are sold in proportion. *Steps.* Money raised = 1,00,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 10,00,000. Share met = 1,00,000 ÷ 2,50,000 = 0.4. *Answer.* The company raises Tk 10,00,000, and each applicant receives 40 per cent of the shares applied for. *Check.* 40 per cent of 2,50,000 is 1,00,000.^3

^3: In practice shares are sometimes given by lot, not by proportion. The principle, that demand exceeding supply forces a choice, is the same.

@ Regulator | A public body with the power to make and enforce rules for a market.
@ BSEC | Bangladesh Securities and Exchange Commission, the government body that regulates the share market.

> A market this important needs a referee, and the referee is the regulator.

Why is a referee needed? Because a buyer of shares cannot see inside the company. A dishonest seller might hide losses, or a trader might spread false news to push the price up. A **regulator** is a public body with the power to make and enforce rules for a market. In Bangladesh the regulator of the share market is the **Bangladesh Securities and Exchange Commission (BSEC)**, set up by law in 1993.

> BSEC approves issues, supervises exchanges and brokers, and punishes fraud.

BSEC has four chief tasks. It approves each public issue of shares, after checking the prospectus. It supervises the stock exchanges and the brokers who work on them. It makes rules against unfair practices, such as using secret information to trade, and it may punish those who break them. And it works to protect small investors, who cannot protect themselves. Remember the main point: the exchange is the *place* of trading; BSEC is the *rule-maker* and referee over it.

> Recap: IPO and the regulator.

*Before moving on:* an IPO is the first public sale of shares, approved by BSEC after a prospectus is checked. You should now be able to say why a company cannot sell shares to the public without approval.

## Chambers of Commerce and Trade Bodies

> Business people form associations to speak with one voice.

One bakery has a small voice. If a new rule threatens bakers, one owner may not be heard, but a body of a thousand firms will be. For this reason business people form associations. A **chamber of commerce** is an association of business firms of an area or kind, formed to protect and advance their common interests. A **trade association** is an association of firms in one line of business, such as garment makers, formed for the same purpose. Examples are the Dhaka Chamber of Commerce and Industry and the Metropolitan Chamber of Commerce and Industry, both of which are old and respected, and the association of garment makers and exporters.^4

^4: The Metropolitan Chamber traces its beginnings to 1904, as the Narayanganj Chamber of Commerce. Names and rules of these bodies change; check each body's current name.

> Chambers speak to government, give services, and help trade.

What do such bodies do? They put the views of members to government, and are often asked to sit on official committees that discuss trade, tax and ports. They provide information on markets and rules. They give services to members, such as the certificates of origin you met in Chapter X, and settle disputes among members. They organise fairs and training. And they link members with business bodies abroad. These tasks matter most to small firms, which could not do them alone.

@ FBCCI | Federation of Bangladesh Chambers of Commerce and Industry, the top body of Bangladeshi chambers and trade associations.

> One apex body brings the chambers and associations together.

Above the many chambers and associations stands the **Federation of Bangladesh Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FBCCI)**. It was set up in 1973, and its members are chambers and trade associations. Because it speaks for the whole private sector, the government consults it on large questions of trade and industry. It also keeps links with business bodies in other countries and takes part in fairs. We call it an *apex* body, because it stands at the top of a structure of smaller bodies.

> Recap: chambers, associations and the FBCCI.

*Before moving on:* chambers and trade associations give business firms a voice, services and links, and the FBCCI stands at their head. You should now be able to say why a small bakery would join a chamber.

## Export Zones, Export Promotion and Other Institutions

@ EPZ | Export processing zone: an area with special facilities for firms that make goods mainly for export.

> An export zone offers firms special conditions to attract them.

An **EPZ**, or *export processing zone*, is an area with special facilities for firms that make goods mainly for export. Firms inside may bring in machines and materials without duty, may enjoy tax relief for some years, and have roads, power and services ready, with the papers handled in one place. The aim is to attract investors, create jobs and earn foreign money. Bangladesh passed a law for zones in 1980, set up an authority to run them in 1981, and opened its first zone at Chattogram in 1983.^5 More have followed in other places.

^5: The authority is the Bangladesh Export Processing Zones Authority. Dates differ slightly between sources, so check them before citing.

@ EPB | Export Promotion Bureau, a government body that helps Bangladeshi firms to find markets and to sell abroad.

> The Export Promotion Bureau helps firms find buyers and markets.

The **Export Promotion Bureau (EPB)** is a government body that helps Bangladeshi firms find markets and sell abroad. It gathers and gives out information on foreign markets, helps firms to attend trade fairs, keeps records of exporters, and helps to carry out export policy. In Chapter X you saw that an exporter must find a buyer; the EPB is one of the bodies that helps.

> Other bodies share the work of supporting business.

Several other bodies play a part. The central bank, Bangladesh Bank, controls the money supply, supervises banks, and has rules for foreign exchange and for the letters of credit you studied in Chapter X. The National Board of Revenue collects taxes and runs customs. A government investment agency helps firms, including foreign ones, to start. And the Export Processing Zones Authority runs the zones. The table sets out the main bodies and what each is for.

## The Worked Case: Becoming Public

> The bakery's plan to become public, March 2029.

:case Case file, illustrative. March 2029. The bakery, a private limited company (Chapter VII), has 2,00,000 shares of Tk 10 issued: 1,20,000 held by the first owner and 80,000 by the second partner. Authorised capital is 5,00,000 shares (Tk 50,00,000). The owners plan to convert to a public limited company and offer 1,00,000 new shares of Tk 10 to the public, to pay for a second outlet. BSEC approval is needed. All figures are invented.

> The case asks how much the bakery raises and who then owns it.

*Situation.* The owners wish to raise money from the public. *Question.* How much will they raise, can the company issue so many shares, and who will control it after the issue?

> We check the limit, the money raised and the new ownership.

*Reasoning.* Issued shares would become 2,00,000 + 1,00,000 = 3,00,000. This is below the authorised 5,00,000, so no change of authorised capital is needed. Money raised is 1,00,000 × Tk 10 = Tk 10,00,000, equal to the cost of the outlet. The first owner would hold 1,20,000 of 3,00,000 shares = 40 per cent, the second partner 80,000 of 3,00,000 = 26.7 per cent, and the public 1,00,000 of 3,00,000 = 33.3 per cent. Together the two partners hold 66.7 per cent.

> The owners keep control, but with new duties to the public.

*Conclusion.* The plan raises the money and leaves the two partners with a clear majority, so they keep control. They gain new duties: a prospectus, BSEC approval, listing on the exchange, and public accounts. *Check.* 40 + 26.7 + 33.3 = 100 per cent, and 3,00,000 shares at Tk 10 equals Tk 30,00,000 of issued capital.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in a few lines.

The share market has a primary part, where companies sell new shares, and a secondary part, where owners trade them. The stock exchange organises the second under rules, through brokers, and lists only companies that qualify. An IPO is a company's first public sale of shares; BSEC, the regulator, approves it. Chambers and trade associations give business a voice, with the FBCCI at the head. Export processing zones, the Export Promotion Bureau and other bodies help firms to invest and sell abroad.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five confusions, each stated and corrected.

Some think that the company receives money whenever its shares are traded. It receives money only in the primary sale. Others think that BSEC is the stock exchange; the exchange is the market, and BSEC is the referee. A third error is to take par value as the price on the exchange, though the two differ. Another is to think that only large firms need a chamber; small firms need one most. The last is to treat an export processing zone as a place to sell at home: its firms make mainly for export.

## In History

> A stock exchange was founded in 1954 because another had closed to Pakistani shares.

In 1952 the stock exchange in Calcutta stopped dealing in the shares of Pakistani firms. The government then decided that East Pakistan needed an exchange of its own. On 28 April 1954, eight promoters incorporated the East Pakistan Stock Exchange Association, which was later renamed the Dhaka Stock Exchange. Formal trading began in 1956. Trading stopped during the war of 1971 and began again in 1976, when only nine companies were listed. The exchange has grown since, with the regulator BSEC added in 1993.
