:chapter XI | Multinational Corporations
:mood A firm with one home and many workplaces: friend or master?
:desc Some of the largest firms in the world work in dozens of countries at once. This chapter explains what a multinational corporation is, how it is organised and staffed, why it expands abroad, and what its presence may bring to a developing country such as Bangladesh.

> A bakery owner is offered a famous foreign brand, and must ask why anyone offers it.

A foreign bakery brand, known in many countries, approaches our baker with an offer: use our name, our recipes and our shop design, and pay us a fee. The baker is pleased and uneasy. Why would a firm from another country wish to be in her street at all? It could simply sell its goods across the border, as Chapter IX described. And if it comes, who gains more, the baker, her customers, the nation, or the foreign firm? These questions lead to one of the most debated subjects in business: the multinational corporation.

> This chapter needs the idea of a company and the idea of trade between nations.

*You will need:* the company and its shareholders (Chapter VII), and foreign trade, exports and imports (Chapters IX and X).

## What a Multinational Corporation Is

@ Multinational corporation | A large firm that owns and runs business operations in several countries.

> A multinational corporation runs business in several countries from one centre.

A **multinational corporation** is a firm that owns and runs business operations in several countries. A firm that only exports goods is not one, for it does not run operations abroad. A firm that builds a factory or opens shops in other countries is one. Such firms are usually very large. They hold much capital, use advanced technology, own well-known brands, and employ many thousands of people. They often take the legal form of a company, as in Chapter VII.

@ Home country | The country where a multinational corporation has its headquarters and is owned.

> The home country is where the firm is based; the host country is where it works abroad.

Two words name the countries. The **home country** is the country where a multinational corporation has its headquarters and is owned. The **host country** is a country where it operates but is not based.^1 Take a firm with headquarters in Country Z that runs a factory in Bangladesh. Z is the home country and Bangladesh is the host country. The firm in Z is the *parent company*; the business it runs in Bangladesh is its *subsidiary*, which is a company controlled by another, here by the parent.

^1: "Host" is an everyday word for a person who receives guests. In this chapter it means the country that receives the firm's operations.

@ Foreign direct investment | Money that a firm of one country puts into owning or controlling a business in another country.

> Direct investment means putting money into a business abroad, to own or control it.

How does the parent enter the host country? One way is **foreign direct investment**: money that a firm of one country puts into owning or controlling a business in another country. Building a factory is an example; so is buying most of the shares of a local firm. The parent may own the subsidiary wholly, or share ownership with a local partner in a *joint venture*. Both are direct investment, because the parent controls the business. Contrast it with a loan, which brings money but not control.

@ Franchise | The right to use a firm's brand, products and methods in return for fees.

> A franchise lets a local firm use a foreign brand without foreign ownership.

A less costly way to enter a country is the **franchise**: the right to use a firm's brand, products and methods in return for fees. The foreign brand gives the right; the local owner, called the *franchisee*, runs the shop with its own money and pays the *franchisor*, usually a lump sum at the start and a share of sales each month. The local owner is not a subsidiary; she owns her shop. A *licence* is similar and allows a local firm to make a product by the owner's methods.

@ Technology transfer | The passing of machines, methods and skills from one firm or country to another.

> Technology transfer passes machines, methods and skills to the host country.

Entry often brings **technology transfer**: the passing of machines, methods and skills from one firm or country to another. When a foreign maker installs a machine and trains local staff to run it, knowledge passes with the machine. How much passes depends on the terms agreed. If the foreign firm keeps its key skills inside its own staff, little remains when it leaves.

> Staff of a multinational are named by their nationality and place of work.

A multinational must also decide whom to employ in each subsidiary. Three names are used, each describing a person's nationality compared with the countries of the firm. The table shows them, using the firm of Country Z in Bangladesh.

:table Three kinds of staff in a multinational
| Name | Meaning | Example in Bangladesh |
| Parent-country national (PCN) | A person from the home country | A manager from Z |
| Host-country national (HCN) | A person from the host country | A Bangladeshi engineer |
| Third-country national (TCN) | A person from neither | A manager from Country Y |

> Each choice has merits: PCNs know the parent, and HCNs know the market.

Each choice has merits. PCNs know the parent's methods and are trusted by headquarters. HCNs know the language, the law and the customers, and cost less to employ, and their promotion encourages local goodwill. TCNs may bring wide experience of several markets. Many multinationals use PCNs for the first few years and then replace most of them with HCNs, keeping PCNs only for the top posts.

> Recap: what a multinational is and how it enters.

*Before moving on:* a multinational runs operations in several countries from a home country; it enters by direct investment, joint venture or franchise; and it staffs with PCNs, HCNs and TCNs. You should now be able to say why a firm that only exports is not a multinational.

## Importance and Advantages

> Firms go abroad for new markets, cheaper inputs and lower risk.

Why do firms become multinational? The reasons are mostly the same in every country. They seek new markets, because the home market may be full. They seek cheaper or better inputs: land, labour, materials or skill. They wish to avoid the cost of tariffs and transport that apply to goods sent across borders, by making the goods inside the market. They wish to spread risk, so that trouble in one country does not sink the firm. They gain size: a larger firm can spread its costs of research and advertising over more customers. And governments often offer tax relief to attract them.

> Host countries may gain capital, jobs, skills, exports and tax revenue.

The advantages to a host country are real. First, capital: a developing country may lack the funds to build a large plant, and a multinational brings them. Second, jobs, both in the plant and among suppliers and sellers. Third, skills: staff are trained and managers learn modern methods, and the technology transfer described above. Fourth, exports: the subsidiary may sell abroad through the parent's links with world markets. Fifth, revenue: the subsidiary pays taxes, including VAT. Sixth, competition: local firms must improve to keep their customers.

> The home country also gains from profits, markets and trade.

The home country gains too. Profits flow back to the parent and its shareholders. The parent buys machines and materials at home, which gives work to home firms. And the parent's growth strengthens the home country's share in world trade. The table below sets the gains of the three parties side by side.

:table Who gains from a multinational, and how
| Party | Main gains |
| The multinational | New markets, cheaper inputs, spread of risk, savings of scale, avoiding tariffs |
| The host country | Capital, jobs, skills, exports, taxes, better products and competition |
| The home country | Profits, orders for home firms, a stronger place in world trade |

> Recap: who gains from a multinational.

*Before moving on:* a multinational gains markets and savings; the host gains capital, jobs and skill; and the home country gains profit and orders. You should now be able to give two reasons for a firm to go abroad.

## Effects on Developing Countries Such as Bangladesh

> The same multinational may help in one way and harm in another.

A developing country welcomes capital and skill, but the same multinational may also cause harm. Both sides are true, and an honest account must give both. The table pairs seven areas, with a possible gain and a possible problem for each. Read each row across.

:table Gains and problems for a host country
| Area | A possible gain | A possible problem |
| Jobs | New jobs and training | Best posts held by outsiders; jobs lost in small local firms |
| Technology | Modern machines and methods | Key knowledge kept abroad; dependence on spare parts |
| Capital | Money for large projects | Profit sent home may exceed the money brought in |
| Local firms | Orders for local suppliers; better standards | Local firms driven out by a rich rival |
| Resources | Use of idle land and labour | Heavy use of scarce resources; pollution |
| Working life | Higher pay in some cases | Low pay, poor safety in others |
| Politics and culture | Wider links with the world | Pressure on governments; changed tastes |

> Profits that leave the country can, over the years, outweigh the capital that came in.

The question of money needs a worked case. *Given.* A foreign firm invests Tk 20,00,00,000 (Tk 20 crore) in a Bangladeshi plant. The plant earns Tk 4,00,00,000 a year in profit, of which 75 per cent is sent to the parent. *Find.* The yearly outflow and the time for the outflow to equal the investment. *Steps.* Outflow = 75 per cent × Tk 4,00,00,000 = Tk 3,00,00,000 a year. Time = Tk 20,00,00,000 ÷ Tk 3,00,00,000 = about 6.7 years. *Answer.* After about seven years the money sent home exceeds the money brought in. *Check.* Seven years at Tk 3 crore is Tk 21 crore, which is above Tk 20 crore.

> The sum is not the whole story: jobs, taxes and skills remain.

We must not conclude that the investment was a loss. The plant has also paid wages, bought local materials, paid taxes, and trained staff, and the remaining quarter of the profit, Tk 1 crore a year, stays and may be reinvested. The example shows only that capital flows run in both directions. A fair judgement counts the whole account.

> Governments use rules to keep the gains and reduce the harm.

A host government can reduce the harm by rules. It may require a share of ownership to be held by local partners, a minimum of local staff in senior posts, and a minimum of local materials. It may require the training of local staff, the transfer of technology, and compliance with its laws on pollution, safety and tax. It may bargain for conditions before it allows entry. Too many rules discourage firms from coming at all, so the government must balance its wish for investment against its wish for protection.

> The judgement depends on the firm, the terms and the host's strength.

Reasonable people disagree about multinationals. Some say they bring capital and skills that a poor country cannot find elsewhere; others say they take the profit and leave the host dependent. The facts differ from firm to firm. A multinational that trains local staff, buys from local suppliers and obeys the law serves the host well; one that avoids tax and pollutes serves it badly. The strength of the host's rules and officials largely decides which kind it meets.

> Recap: gains, problems and rules.

*Before moving on:* a multinational may bring capital, jobs and skill but may also cause outflows of profit, harm to local firms and pressure on resources, and host governments use rules to shape the result. You should now be able to argue both sides.

## The Worked Case: An Oven and an Offer

> The bakery's oven and franchise choices, August 2027.

:case Case file, illustrative. August 2027. The bakery, a private limited company, is considering two foreign links. (1) An oven from a multinational maker, at Tk 9,00,000 landed, against a locally made oven at Tk 6,00,000. The imported oven uses 20 per cent less gas; the bakery's gas costs Tk 25,000 a month. The maker will train two staff members, but supplies spare parts only itself. (2) A franchise offer from a foreign bakery brand: a fee of Tk 5,00,000 and a royalty of 5 per cent of all sales. Monthly sales are assumed to be Tk 6,00,000, with a profit of 25 per cent on any extra sales. All figures are invented.

> The case weighs a technology purchase and a brand offer.

*Situation.* Two multinational firms have made offers. *Question.* How should the bakery judge each?

> The oven's savings are small, but its other terms matter.

*Reasoning, oven.* The imported oven costs Tk 3,00,000 more. Gas saved is 20 per cent × Tk 25,000 = Tk 5,000 a month. Time to recover the extra cost is Tk 3,00,000 ÷ Tk 5,000 = 60 months, or five years. Savings alone are slow. But the staff training is a technology transfer, and the oven may bake more or better. On the other side, spare parts come only from the maker, which makes the bakery dependent. *Reasoning, franchise.* If extra sales are E, the profit on them is 25 per cent of E, and the royalty is 5 per cent of (Tk 6,00,000 + E). The royalty is covered when 0.25E = 0.05 × (6,00,000 + E). So 0.20E = Tk 30,000, and E = Tk 1,50,000, which is 25 per cent more sales, before the fee is counted at all.

> The conclusion separates a defensible purchase from an uncertain brand deal.

*Conclusion.* The oven is a defensible purchase if its capacity and quality, and a written promise on parts and service, are worth the extra cost. The franchise needs a rise of one-quarter in sales just to pay its royalty, and the fee comes on top; the owners should refuse unless they have strong evidence that the brand will bring such a rise. *Check.* With E = Tk 1,50,000, royalty = 5 per cent × Tk 7,50,000 = Tk 37,500, and profit on extra sales = 25 per cent × Tk 1,50,000 = Tk 37,500. They agree.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in a few lines.

A multinational corporation owns and runs operations in several countries, from a home country to host countries. It may enter by direct investment, a joint venture or a franchise, and it staffs with parent-country, host-country and third-country nationals. Firms go abroad for markets, cheaper inputs, lower risk and savings of scale. Host countries may gain capital, jobs, skills and revenue, but may also suffer an outflow of profit, harm to local firms, and pressure on resources. Rules and bargaining help to keep the gains and limit the harm.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five confusions, each stated and corrected.

Some say that any firm which exports is a multinational. It must run operations abroad. Others say that a franchisee is owned by the foreign brand, but the franchisee owns the shop and only rents the brand. A third error is to treat foreign direct investment as a loan; it buys ownership or control. Another is to say that multinationals always harm the host, or always help it; the evidence differs by firm and by rule. The last is to think that a host-country national is a foreigner: he or she is a citizen of the country where the subsidiary works.

## In History

> A trading company of 1600 shows how a firm may grow into a power.

On 31 December 1600, Queen Elizabeth I of England granted a charter to a company of London merchants to trade with the East Indies. It was not a multinational in the modern sense, but it was a firm based in one country that traded and kept stations in many others. Over the next 150 years it grew in India, and after the Battle of Plassey in 1757 and the grant of the right to collect revenue in Bengal in 1765, it came to rule much of the region. The story shows what can follow when a firm's power outgrows the control of any host government.
