:chapter IV | Responsibility to Society
:mood To whom does a business answer?
:desc A business sells to people, employs people, buys from people and lives beside people. This chapter asks what it owes to each of them, why it owes it, and how an observer can tell a responsible firm from one that only appears to be.

> A sweet shop could earn more by using an unsafe colouring that no one would notice.
Consider a small sweet shop in a busy market. Its owner learns that a cheap colouring, not approved for food, makes her sweets look brighter. Brighter sweets sell faster, and she would earn Tk 5,000 more profit each month. No customer would notice the colouring. This is a teaching example and is not part of the bakery case.

> Almost everyone says she should not, yet nothing forces her.
Almost everyone says that she should not use it. But ask why. The law does not stand in her shop to check each tray. Her customers cannot taste the difference. Her profit would rise, and profit is one of the aims of a business (Chapter II). So where does the *should not* come from?

> The answer lies in what a business owes to the people it touches.
The answer is the subject of this chapter. A business does not work alone. It sells to people, employs people, buys from people and lives beside people. What it does reaches all of them, and some of them cannot protect themselves. This chapter asks what a business owes to those people, why it owes it, and how an observer can tell which firms meet their duties.

> The earlier ideas this chapter uses.
*You will need* these ideas from earlier chapters: profit, cost, sales and customer (Chapter I); the aims of a business (Chapter II); and stakeholder, supplier, regulation, tax and VAT (Chapter III).

## Meaning and Importance

> A good neighbour has duties that no one pays for.
Think of a good neighbour. A good neighbour does not play loud music at midnight, does not throw rubbish into the lane and helps when someone is ill. Nobody pays the neighbour for this. People accept that living near others brings duties. A business lives among people in the same way.

> Social responsibility is the duty to act for the good of the people a firm affects.
> Word in use. Social: in daily life, about meeting friends. In business, about people and society as a whole.
@ Social responsibility | The duty of a business to act for the good of the people and society it affects, not only its owners.
The name for this duty is **social responsibility**. Social responsibility means the duty of a business to act in ways that are good for the people and the society it affects, and not only for its owners.^1 The sweet shop that uses safe colouring, even though no one would know, is acting with social responsibility.

> Ethics supplies the ideas of right and wrong behind the duty.
@ Ethics | Ideas of right and wrong that guide a person's choices.
Behind this duty lie ideas of right and wrong. **Ethics** means ideas of right and wrong that guide a person's choices. Social responsibility is ethics applied to business. An owner who weighs goods honestly, even when the customer cannot check the scale, is guided by ethics and not by fear of a fine.

> Duties come in three levels: what the law demands, what ethics asks, and what a firm freely gives.
Not every duty is of the same kind. Some are demanded by law. Some are asked by ethics, although no law demands them. Some are gifts that the firm chooses to give. The table names the three levels and gives a bakery example of each. Read the first column as how strong the duty is: must, should, could.

:table Three levels of duty, with a bakery example
| Level | Meaning | A bakery example |
| Must (legal duty) | A duty that law requires, with a penalty for failure | Follow food safety rules and pass on VAT collected |
| Should (ethical duty) | A duty that right and wrong ask, though no one may check | Pay staff on time and tell customers the truth about ingredients |
| Could (voluntary giving) | A gift beyond what law or ethics requires | Give unsold bread to a local shelter |

> The levels move over time and differ from place to place.
The levels are not fixed. Many duties began as ethical ideas and later became law, once enough people agreed that the harm was too great to leave to conscience. Law also differs from country to country. So a firm that asks only whether an act is legal has asked the smallest question. The larger questions are whether the act is honest, fair and safe for the people it reaches.

> Social responsibility has four features: it is wide, often voluntary, long-term and scaled to means.
Four features describe social responsibility. It is wide, because it reaches every group a firm affects. It is often voluntary, because law covers only the lowest level. It is long-term, because trust and harm both build up slowly. And it is scaled to means, since a small bakery and a large factory cannot give the same amount. Keeping these four features in mind prevents two common errors: thinking that duty is only law, and thinking that it is only generosity.

> Society lends a firm its workers, roads and peace, so the firm owes something back.
Why should a firm accept such duties? The first reason is that a firm takes from society. Its workers were raised and taught by families and schools. Its customers reach it by public roads. It trades in conditions of peace that others keep. A firm that takes all this and gives nothing back is like a guest who eats and never thanks the host.

> A firm's good name is worth money, and it is built by how the firm treats people.
@ Reputation | What people in general believe about a firm's honesty and quality.
The second reason is trust. **Reputation** means what people in general believe about a firm's honesty and quality. A bakery known for clean kitchens and fair weights keeps its customers even when a rival charges less. Reputation takes years to build and a day to lose. Staff also prefer to work for a firm they respect, and suppliers prefer to deal with a firm that keeps its word.

> Responsible conduct brings the firm loyal customers, steady staff and fewer disputes.
The firm itself gains in several ways, though gain is not the reason to be responsible. Customers who trust a firm return and tell others. Staff who feel fairly treated stay longer, so the firm spends less on finding and training new people. Suppliers who are paid on time offer better terms. A firm that follows the law avoids fines and sudden closure. Each gain is a saving of money or worry that an irresponsible firm never enjoys.

> Responsibility also protects weaker people and keeps the market fair.
Responsibility matters to society as well. It protects people who cannot protect themselves: customers who cannot test goods, and workers who need the job too much to refuse unsafe work. It also keeps the market fair. If honest firms must compete with firms that cheat, the honest firms lose. When all firms follow the same duties, honesty stops being a disadvantage.

> Some owners reply that duties cost money and belong to the government.
There are arguments on the other side, and they deserve a fair hearing. Owners may say that duties cost money that belongs to them. They may say that a rival who ignores the duties can sell cheaper. They may say that a firm's skill is business and not social work, and that looking after society is the task of government. These are serious points, and an honest owner feels them.

> The replies: many duties cost little, and the law binds all rivals.
Each point has a reply. Many duties, such as honest weights and clean tables, cost very little. Law that applies to all firms removes the advantage of the cheat. And government cannot be everywhere. It cannot watch every tray of sweets, so it relies on firms to behave well when no one is looking. The question is therefore not whether a firm has duties, but how large they are for a firm of its size and means.

> Profit and responsibility can pull apart today but often agree over time.
@! Go slowly here.
Now meet the hardest idea of the chapter. Chapter II said that a business has economic aims, such as profit, and social aims, such as service to people. Sometimes they seem to conflict. Use an everyday bridge. A farmer who never rests the land gets a big crop this year and poor soil next year. The short run and the long run give different answers. A business is in the same position.

> The sweet shop's shortcut gains Tk 60,000 in a year and may lose far more.
Return to the sweet shop with small numbers. The unsafe colouring gains Tk 5,000 a month, which is Tk 60,000 in a year. Suppose it is discovered. Customers leave, and profit falls by Tk 20,000 a month for six months, which is Tk 1,20,000. The shop also faces fines and perhaps closure. The shortcut loses more than it gained, even before the fines. These figures are assumed, but the pattern is common: harm to people tends to return as harm to the firm.

> If a duty is done only when it pays, it is not really a duty.
But take care. Do not conclude that responsibility is only a clever way to earn profit. Sometimes a duty costs money and brings nothing back that can be counted. A firm that is honest only when honesty pays is not honest. Responsibility means accepting a lower profit when the duty is real. The reconciliation is partial: many duties pay in the long run, and some only cost. A responsible owner accepts both.

> Rule of thumb: first obey the law, then avoid harm, then give within means.
When aims seem to conflict, an owner can use an order of questions. First, does the law require this? Then it must be done. Second, does the act harm someone who cannot protect themselves? Then it should be avoided or put right. Third, can the firm afford a gift or a kindness? Then it may give, in proportion to its means. *You should now be able to say:* a firm that harms people to gain profit today often loses more profit later, but a real duty must be kept even when it costs.

> Check the idea with one small calculation.
*Pause and check.* A shop spends Tk 2,000 a month on a safety check, and its monthly profit is Tk 40,000. What share of the profit does the check cost? (The answer is at the back of the book.)

> Recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* Social responsibility is the duty of a firm to act for the good of the people it affects. Duties are of three levels: must, should and could. Firms accept them because they take from society, need trust and protect weaker people. Profit and responsibility often agree over time, but a real duty does not depend on that.

## Duties to Customers, Employees, Community and Government

> Four groups come first: customers, employees, the community and government.
Chapter III defined a stakeholder as a person or group affected by a business or able to affect it. This section takes four stakeholders that every firm has: customers, employees, the people who live nearby and the government. The next section takes suppliers and those who put money into the firm.

### Duties to Customers

> Customers usually cannot test what they buy, so they must trust the seller.
Customers are the people the firm exists to serve. They usually cannot test what they buy. A buyer cannot see inside a loaf, weigh a packet to the gram or measure the cleanliness of a kitchen. So the customer must trust the seller, and trust creates a duty. The firm should sell goods that are safe, correctly described, correctly weighed and fairly priced.

> Adulteration harms customers in ways they cannot see.
@ Adulteration | Mixing something cheap, impure or harmful into food or goods.
The worst failure is adulteration. **Adulteration** means mixing something cheap, impure or harmful into food or goods. The unsafe colouring in the opening puzzle is an example. Adulteration is dangerous because the customer cannot detect it, and the harm may appear days or years later. For that reason it is punished by law in Bangladesh.^2

> Consumer protection is the shield for customers, built by law and by the firm's own rules.
@ Consumer protection | Protecting customers from unsafe goods, false claims and unfair practices, by law and by firms' own rules.
The shield that guards customers has a name. **Consumer protection** means protecting customers from unsafe goods, false claims and unfair practices, by law and by the firm's own rules. A firm contributes to it in everyday ways: it labels ingredients, shows prices in plain sight, states the date on fresh goods and handles complaints politely and quickly.

> Honest weights and honest claims are the commonest tests.
Two tests come up most often. The first is honest quantity. Suppose a packet sold as 500 grams holds 450 grams. At Tk 100 a packet, the customer pays Tk 100 for goods worth Tk 90, a loss of Tk 10 on each packet. Over 200 packets, the seller gains Tk 2,000 from a cheat that no customer will notice. The second test is honest claims. A bakery that advertises *baked fresh daily* must bake fresh daily. A false claim deceives customers who rely on it.

> A fair price excludes hoarding and overcharging in times of shortage.
Price raises a further duty. A fair price covers the seller's cost and a reasonable profit. It does not take advantage of a shortage. Suppose rain floods the roads and bread is scarce. A seller who holds back stock to raise the price later, or who doubles the price on the same day, takes advantage of customers who have no other choice. Law may forbid such acts, but ethics forbids them even where law is silent.

> After the sale, a responsible firm still listens to the customer.
The duty does not end at the counter. A responsible firm replaces goods that are faulty, answers complaints without anger and tells customers about any danger it later discovers. Customers also have a part to play. They should check goods, keep receipts and report cheating. But since the seller knows more than the buyer, most of the duty falls on the seller.

### Duties to Employees

> Employees depend on the firm for their living, so the firm owes them fair terms.
Employees give their time and effort to the firm, and many depend on the wage for their living. This creates duties. The firm should pay fair wages on time, provide a safe place to work, keep hours reasonable, treat people with respect and give them the chance to learn. It should never make children do harmful work. Employees should also be able to raise a problem without fear of losing the job.

> Late wages and unsafe work harm people who cannot easily leave.
Look at this from the employee's seat. A worker paid every day or month often spends the money before it arrives, on food and rent. A wage that comes two weeks late forces a loan or an empty plate. A bakery oven can burn, and flour dust and heat tire the body. Gloves, a fire extinguisher and a clear exit cost little. A worker who must choose between a safe place and a job needs the firm to make the safe place certain.

> Fair treatment and training are further duties to employees.
Fair treatment also means that people are judged by their work, and not by their family, religion, sex or place of birth. It means that a worker who makes a mistake is taught and not insulted. And it means that the firm helps people to improve. A young baker taught to make cakes becomes more useful to the firm and more secure in life. The firm gains skill, and the employee gains a future.

> Some duties scale with the firm's means; basic safety does not.
Here is an important exception. Some duties depend on what a firm can afford. A very small bakery cannot offer the training course that a large firm can. But some duties do not scale. Basic safety, wages that are paid and honest treatment cannot be reduced because a firm is small. A firm that cannot meet these should not employ people until it can.

### Duties to the Community

> The community is the people living and working near the firm.
@ Community | The people who live and work near a business.
The people nearest to a firm are its community. A **community** means the people who live and work near a business. A bakery gives jobs to local people, buys from local shops and brings customers to the street. But it also makes noise at dawn, sends out smoke from its oven and produces waste. The community feels all of this, whether or not it is a customer.

> A cost that falls on neighbours is still a cost of the firm's activity.
Take a new setting, a small dyeing workshop beside a canal. It is a teaching example and is not part of the bakery case. The owner lets dirty water run straight into the canal and saves the cost of treating it. The saving is the owner's. But fish die, and families downstream can no longer wash or bathe in the water. The cost has not vanished. It has moved from the firm to the neighbours, who did not choose it and cannot refuse it.

> Pollution and sustainability name the harm and the remedy.
@ Pollution | Harm to air, water or land caused by waste or smoke.
@ Sustainability | Using resources in a way that leaves enough for people in the future.
The harm in the canal is **pollution**, which means harm to air, water or land caused by waste or smoke. The remedy is a way of working called **sustainability**, which means using resources in a way that leaves enough for people in the future. A bakery can practise it by wasting less flour, using energy carefully and disposing of rubbish properly. The workshop should treat its water before it leaves the premises.

> The firm that causes harm should bear the cost of preventing it.
Who should pay for clean water? The simple answer is the firm that dirtied it. Suppose treating the workshop's water costs Tk 4,000 a month. If the owner refuses, the neighbours pay in sickness and lost fish, and their cost is far greater than Tk 4,000. If the firm pays, the price of its cloth rises slightly, and the people who buy the cloth share the cost. This is fairer than leaving the burden on neighbours who never chose it.

> Communities also benefit when firms give back in useful ways.
A firm may also give something back in a positive way. It can hire local people, buy from local suppliers, support a school's needs or give unsold bread to people without food. These are mostly voluntary gifts, at the *could* level of the table. They are valued most when they come from a firm that has already met its duties at the *must* and *should* levels.

### Duties to Government

> A firm must obey laws, hold its licences and keep honest records.
The government makes the rules under which all firms work, so a firm owes it obedience and honesty. The firm should obey the law, hold the licences its trade needs, keep honest records and pay the taxes it owes. The tax duty is exact. In Chapter III, a bakery that adds Tk 15 of VAT to a Tk 100 loaf must pass the Tk 15 to government. That money never belonged to the bakery.

> Honest records mean the taxes are counted truthfully.
Honest records matter as much as honest payment. Suppose the bakery sells 200 loaves in a day at Tk 100 before VAT. At 15 per cent, the VAT is 200 × Tk 15, which is Tk 3,000. If the owner writes down only 150 loaves, the VAT passed on is Tk 2,250, and Tk 750 is kept unlawfully. The Tk 750 was never the bakery's. Keeping a second, hidden set of accounts is a common form of this cheating.

> Refusing a bribe protects honest firms and the public.
@ Bribe | Money or a gift offered to an official to win an unfair favour.
A second duty is to refuse bribes. A **bribe** means money or a gift offered to an official to win an unfair favour. Suppose a clerk hints that a payment would speed up a licence. If the firm pays, it cheats honest firms that wait their turn, and it teaches officials that favours are for sale. The firm that refuses may wait longer, and that cost is part of the duty.

> Firms may speak to government, but through honest channels.
This duty does not silence the firm. A firm may disagree with a rule and may say so, for example through a trade body (Chapter XII). It may ask for fair treatment. What it may not do is evade the rule in secret. Open argument is part of a healthy relation with government. Secret evasion is not.

> Recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* Customers are owed safe, honest, fairly priced goods, and consumer protection is the shield for them. Employees are owed fair pay, safe work and respect. The community is owed freedom from pollution and a sustainable use of resources. Government is owed obedience, honest records, taxes in full and refusal of bribes.

## Duties to Suppliers, Investors and Others

> Suppliers are small businesses too, and late payment can break them.
A supplier is a person or business that sells goods or services to another business (Chapter III). The first duty to a supplier is to pay what is agreed, on the day it is agreed. Consider the flour supplier of the case file. The bakery buys flour worth Tk 32,400 a month. If the bakery pays after 30 days, the supplier has Tk 32,400 of bills unpaid at any time. If it pays after 90 days, the supplier waits for three months of bills, which is Tk 97,200. The extra Tk 64,800 is money the supplier needs to buy more flour.

> Other duties to suppliers: honest orders, fair prices and kept promises.
A small supplier often cannot afford to wait or to argue. Other duties follow from this. The buyer should order honestly and not cancel orders without reason. It should pay a fair price and not squeeze the supplier below its cost. It should keep its promises. In return, the supplier owes the buyer goods of the quality agreed. The relation is one of mutual duty, though the stronger party carries more of the burden.

> Investors put money into a firm and trust it to use it well.
@ Investor | A person who puts money into a business in the hope of a return.
Now the people who put money in. An **investor** is a person who puts money into a business in the hope of a return. Owners are investors, and so are lenders such as banks. The firm owes investors honest accounts, careful use of their money, repayment of loans with interest on time and a fair share of the profit. At present the bakery has one investor, its owner, but later chapters introduce partners and shareholders, who need the same honesty.

> Honest accounts let lenders and owners judge the firm correctly.
Suppose a bank lends a firm Tk 5,00,000 at 10 per cent interest a year. The yearly interest is Tk 50,000. The bank decides to lend by reading the firm's accounts. If the accounts overstate the firm's profit, the bank is misled and may lend more than the firm can repay. The same is true of a partner or shareholder who buys a share on the strength of false accounts. Honest accounts are therefore a duty to everyone who relies on them.

> Competitors are owed fair play, and the public is owed truth.
Other stakeholders matter too. Competitors are owed fair play. A firm should not tell false stories about a rival's goods, copy its trademark or spread rumours about its kitchen. The wider public is owed truth. A firm should not mislead people about its goods or its record, and should be ready to answer questions from those who ask.

> The table gathers all the stakeholders and what the firm owes each.
The table below gathers the stakeholders met in this chapter. Read each row as a pair: what the stakeholder wants and what the firm owes in return. Later in this chapter the table is also a tool for checking a firm.

:table Who a firm answers to
| Stakeholder | What it wants | What the firm owes |
| Owners and investors | A fair return and safe money | Honest accounts, careful use, fair profit share |
| Employees | Fair pay, safe work, respect | Wages on time, safe conditions, training |
| Customers | Safe, honest goods at a fair price | True claims, correct weights, complaint handling |
| Suppliers | Prompt payment, steady orders | Payment on agreed days, fair prices |
| Community | Quiet, clean surroundings; local benefit | No pollution, careful waste, local jobs |
| Government | Obedience, honest taxes | Licences, accurate records, no bribes |
| Competitors | Fair competition | No false claims about rivals |

> Duties can pull against each other, and no formula settles every case.
The duties can pull in different directions. A wage rise helps staff but may lead to higher prices for customers. Paying suppliers early helps them but reduces the firm's cash. No formula settles every case. The order of questions from Section 1 helps: law and safety first, promises made second, gifts last. After that, the owner must use judgement, and two honest owners may judge differently.

> Recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* Suppliers are owed payment on agreed days and fair dealing. Investors are owed honest accounts and careful use of their money. Competitors are owed fair play, and the public is owed truth. Duties sometimes conflict, and judgement is needed.

## Recognising a Responsible Firm

> Talk is cheap, so an observer needs signs that cannot be faked easily.
How can an outsider tell whether a firm is responsible? A firm may say that it cares. Talk costs nothing. The observer needs signs that are hard to fake: what the firm does every day, how it treats those who cannot complain, and whether it keeps to its promises when keeping them is costly.

> A code of conduct states in writing how people in the firm must behave.
@ Code of conduct | A written set of rules about how people in a firm must behave.
One sign is a written standard. A **code of conduct** means a written set of rules about how people in a firm must behave. It may say that staff must wash hands before handling food, must not accept gifts from suppliers and must treat customers politely. A code helps only if people know it, follow it and see the owner follow it. A code in a drawer is not a sign of anything.

> An audit checks whether the firm's work meets its standards.
> Word in use. Audit: in daily life, a check. In accounting, an examination of accounts by an independent person.
@ Audit | A careful check of whether work, goods or records meet agreed standards.
A second sign is regular checking. An **audit** means a careful check of whether work, goods or records meet agreed standards. A hygiene audit, for example, inspects the kitchen against a list of rules. Audits are strongest when done by someone outside the firm, who has no reason to hide a fault. In later accounting chapters, you will meet the audit of financial accounts.

> A checklist of six questions helps an observer judge a firm.
The table offers a checklist of six questions. Each row gives the sign of a responsible firm and the sign of an irresponsible one. No firm is perfect, so the question to ask is which column describes the firm more often.

:table A checklist for judging a firm
| Question | Sign of a responsible firm | Sign of an irresponsible firm |
| Does it obey the law? | Holds licences, pays taxes in full | Hides sales, avoids rules |
| Is it honest with customers? | True claims, correct weights | Short weight, false claims |
| How does it treat staff? | Wages on time, safe work | Late wages, unsafe work |
| How does it treat suppliers? | Pays on agreed days | Delays payment without reason |
| Does it care for the local area? | Handles waste, limits noise and smoke | Dumps waste, ignores complaints |
| Is it open? | Answers questions, admits and corrects faults | Hides faults, punishes those who report them |

> Many different people can check a firm, not only inspectors.
Several people are in a position to check. Customers see the goods and the shop. Staff see the daily treatment of workers. Neighbours see the smoke and the noise. Government inspectors check licences, hygiene and weights. The press can report a failure. Trade bodies (Chapter XII) may require members to follow rules. A firm that is open to such checking, and answers complaints, is more likely to be honest than one that hides.

> A gift without daily honesty is show, not substance.
Be careful with showy signs. A firm may give a large gift to a school and advertise it loudly, while paying staff late and burning waste near homes. This is show without substance. A large gift does not cancel a daily harm. The test is the daily routine, not the occasional display. It follows that the checklist should be applied to ordinary days.

> Judge a firm by its means, and never excuse a failure of safety or honesty.
Judge a firm in proportion to its means. A bakery with four staff cannot sponsor a hospital, and no one should expect it to. But it can pay on time, keep its kitchen clean and refuse to cheat. Small size excuses small gifts. It does not excuse harm or dishonesty. *You should now be able to say:* a responsible firm is one whose daily conduct, checked by someone able to see it, meets its duties to the people it affects.

> Recap of the section.
*Before moving on.* A responsible firm shows signs in daily conduct: a code that is followed, regular checks, and honest treatment of every stakeholder. Large gifts do not replace daily honesty. A firm is judged in proportion to its means, but never excused on safety or honesty.

## A Worked Case: The Bakery's Policy in April 2026

> The owner adopts three actions on 6 April 2026.
:case *Case file, illustrative.* On 6 April 2026, the owner of the bakery adopts three actions. First, pay every supplier within 30 days. Second, pay for a hygiene audit of the kitchen each month, at Tk 3,000 a month. Third, give 20 unsold loaves each week to a local shelter for people without homes. The ingredients in each loaf cost Tk 45, and they are already paid for. Packing and carrying the loaves cost Tk 200 a week. The flour bill is Tk 32,400 a month (Chapter III), and the profit aim is Tk 60,000 a month.

### Question

> The case asks who each action serves, what it costs and whether the plan fits the profit aim.
Whom does each action serve? Which level of duty is each: must, should or could? What do the three actions cost each month? Does the plan fit the profit aim?

### Reasoning

> Step 1: each action serves a different stakeholder.
Step 1 is to match each action to a stakeholder. Paying within 30 days serves the suppliers. The hygiene audit serves the customers. The weekly gift serves the community. Each of the three serves a different one, so the plan does not leave one group out.

> Step 2: place each action on the three levels of duty.
Step 2 is to place each action on the three levels. The hygiene audit is partly *must*, because food safety rules apply, and partly *should*, because the monthly check goes beyond what any inspector will ask. Paying within 30 days is *should*, because it follows from a promise of fair dealing. The weekly gift is *could*, because no law or ethical rule demands it.

> Step 3: add up the monthly costs.
Step 3 is cost. The audit costs Tk 3,000 a month. Paying suppliers on time has no direct cost, although the firm needs enough cash on the due date. For the gift, the ingredients are already paid for, and the loaves would otherwise be thrown away, so the extra cost is only Tk 200 a week. Over four weeks, that is Tk 800. The table collects the figures.

:table Monthly cost of the three actions
| Action | Stakeholder served | Level of duty | Monthly cost |
| Hygiene audit | Customers | Must and should | Tk 3,000 |
| Pay suppliers in 30 days | Suppliers | Should | Nil (cash needed) |
| Weekly gift of 20 loaves | Community | Could | Tk 800 |
| Total | | | Tk 3,800 |

> Step 4: the extra cost raises the sales needed to reach the profit aim.
Step 4 is the effect on the profit aim. Chapter III gave the sales test: rent, flour and the profit target. With the flour rise, sales needed were Tk 1,37,400. Adding Tk 3,800 of new cost gives Tk 1,41,200. The test rises by 2.8 per cent, because Tk 3,800 divided by Tk 1,37,400 is about 0.028. As before, other costs such as staff and gas are left out to keep the test simple.

### Conclusion

> Adopt all three, in an order of priority if money becomes short.
The owner should adopt all three actions. If cash becomes short, the order of priority is the order of the levels. The hygiene audit comes first, because it protects customers from harm. Paying suppliers on time comes second, because it keeps a promise to small businesses. The gift comes last, because it is voluntary and can be reduced without breaking any duty.

### Check

> Test the plan against the profit aim of Tk 60,000.
If sales and other costs stay as they are, profit falls from Tk 60,000 to Tk 56,200, which is Tk 3,800 less. That is 6.3 per cent below the aim. If the flour rise of Tk 2,400 also remains, profit falls to Tk 53,800. The shortfall is Tk 6,200, which is about 10.3 per cent of the aim. These costs are real. The benefit is trust, which cannot be counted exactly, but which Section 1 showed to be worth money over time. A different owner might weigh the figures differently.

## Summary

> The chapter in a few lines.
Social responsibility is the duty of a business to act for the good of the people and society it affects. Duties are of three levels: must, should and could. Firms accept them because they take from society, need a good reputation and protect weaker people. Profit and responsibility often agree over time, but a real duty must be kept even when it costs.

> Whom the firm owes and how to recognise it.
A firm owes safe, honest goods to customers, fair pay and safe work to employees, clean surroundings to the community, obedience and honest taxes to government, prompt payment to suppliers, honest accounts to investors and fair play to competitors. A responsible firm can be recognised by its daily conduct, its written code and its regular checks, and not by one large gift.

## Think It Through

> Problem 1: a printing press with noisy night shifts.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake: thinking responsibility means giving to charity.
*Social responsibility means giving money to charity.* Giving is only the *could* level. A firm that gives generously but cheats customers or pays staff late is not responsible. Honest daily conduct comes before gifts.

> Mistake: thinking that what is legal is enough.
*If it is legal, it is responsible.* Law is the smallest level of duty. Many harmful acts are not illegal, and law differs from place to place. Ethics asks whether the act is honest, fair and safe.

> Mistake: believing responsibility is only a way to earn profit, or that it only costs.
*Responsibility is only a way to earn profit,* or *responsibility is only a cost.* Neither is true. Many duties pay in the long run, and some only cost. A real duty is kept in both cases.

> Mistake: thinking that only large firms have duties.
*Only large firms have duties.* A small firm has the same duties of honesty and safety as a large one. Only its gifts and extras are smaller.

> Mistake: forgetting that duties extend beyond customers and owners.
*A firm owes duties only to customers and owners.* A firm also owes duties to employees, suppliers, the community, government and competitors. Overlooking any one of them is a common source of harm.

## In History

> A true story of a failure of duty and what followed.
On the morning of 24 April 2013, an eight-storey building called Rana Plaza collapsed in Savar, near Dhaka. It housed five garment factories. More than 1,100 workers were killed and about 2,500 were injured.^3 Deep cracks had been found in the walls the day before. Other businesses in the building sent their people out. The garment workers, who were afraid to enter, were told to return to work. It was a failure of duty to employees, and of the building and safety rules that were meant to protect them.

> The response showed that duties can reach beyond one firm.
In May 2013, the month after the collapse, many foreign brands and retailers, together with trade unions, signed a legally binding agreement called the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh. It required independent safety inspections with public reports and repairs where factories were unsafe. More than 190 brands had signed by its 2018 update. In 2021 the Accord became a wider agreement, the International Accord for Health and Safety in the Textile and Garment Industry. The story shows that the duty for workers' safety lies with owners, with those who buy from them and with government. Safety work did not end in 2013, and it still needs watching.
