:chapter XIII | Business Combination
:mood When two firms become one, who is stronger, and who is at risk?
:desc Firms do not only grow by selling more. They also grow by joining with other firms. This chapter explains why firms combine, the forms that a combination can take, and the gains and dangers that follow for firms, buyers, workers and the nation.

> A bakery owner is offered a deal by her nearest rival, and must decide.

Two bakeries stand three hundred metres apart. They bake the same bread, hire from the same neighbourhood, and fight each other for the same customers. One morning the owner of the second bakery visits the first and says: "Why do we not join?" The first owner sees at once what she might gain: fewer rivals, shared costs, a larger name. She also sees what she might lose: her full control, and perhaps her customers' goodwill. How should she think about the offer? This chapter gives her the words and the tools.

> This chapter needs the idea of a company and of shares, as raised in Chapter XII.

*You will need:* the company, shares and control (Chapters VII and XII), and the competitive force on a firm (Chapter III). The share figures at the end of Chapter XII are carried forward in the worked case.

## Meaning and Causes of Combination

@ Business combination | The joining of two or more firms, in a way that gives them common control or common policy.

> A combination is any joining of firms for common control or policy.

A **business combination** is the joining of two or more firms, in a way that gives them common control or a common policy. The joining may be loose, as when firms only agree to fix a price. It may be complete, as when one firm absorbs another. In every case the aim is to act together where acting alone would be weaker. The word covers many forms, which the next section sorts one by one.

> Firms combine to grow, to cut costs, to meet competition, and to secure supply.

Why do firms combine? Several causes recur. The first is competition: two rivals who fight on price may both do better by joining, since they no longer undercut each other. The second is growth: buying another firm is faster than building one's own. The third is lower cost, which we explain below. The fourth is security: a bread maker who buys a flour mill is sure of flour, and a flour mill that buys bakeries is sure of buyers. The fifth is spread of risk: a firm in several lines is not ruined by trouble in one. The sixth is cost of new methods: research and machines are costly, and large firms can bear the cost. Last, a weak firm may join a strong one to survive.

@ Economies of scale | The fall in cost per unit that comes when a firm produces more.

@! Go slowly here.

> Larger output can spread fixed costs over more units and cut the cost of each.

The third cause needs care, because it rests on an idea. **Economies of scale** means the fall in cost per unit that comes when a firm produces more. Start with an everyday case: a household that cooks for four spends little more on gas and fuel than one that cooks for two, so the fuel cost *per person* falls. Now a business case. *Given.* Two bakeries each pay fixed costs of Tk 60,000 a month, such as rent and oven costs, and each bakes 6,000 loaves. If they join and need fixed costs of only Tk 90,000 in all, the joined firm bakes 12,000 loaves. *Find.* The fixed cost per loaf, before and after. *Steps.* Before: Tk 60,000 ÷ 6,000 = Tk 10 a loaf. After: Tk 90,000 ÷ 12,000 = Tk 7.50 a loaf. *Answer.* The fixed cost per loaf falls by Tk 2.50. *Check.* 12,000 loaves × Tk 7.50 = Tk 90,000, which agrees.

> Size has limits: very large firms may become slow and costly to manage.

Scale has a limit. A firm may grow so large that managing it becomes costly and slow. Orders pass through many hands, staff lose touch with customers, and a mistake at the top spreads far. When cost per unit rises with size, the firm suffers *diseconomies of scale*. A combination does not lower costs automatically; it does so only when the joined firm is managed well.

Two firms each pay fixed costs of Tk 50,000 and each makes 5,000 units. After joining, fixed costs are Tk 70,000 and output is 10,000 units. Find the fixed cost per unit before and after. The answer is at the back of the book.

*Before moving on:* a combination is a joining of firms for common control or policy, caused by competition, growth, cost, security, risk and weakness. You should now be able to explain economies of scale in a sentence.

## Forms of Combination

> Combinations differ in how closely firms are tied, and in whether they survive.

Combinations come in several forms, and the names are easy to confuse. Two questions sort them. First, how tightly are the firms tied? Some stay separate and merely agree; some give up part of their control; some disappear into one. Second, which stages of business do the firms occupy: the same, or different? This section answers the first question and then the second. Take it slowly, and keep the table in view.

@ Pool | An agreement among firms to share the market or to put their profits together and divide them by rule.

@ Cartel | An agreement among independent firms to fix prices or limit output, so as to avoid competing.

> Loose forms: pools and cartels are agreements, and the firms stay independent.

The loosest forms are agreements. In a **pool**, firms agree to share the market among themselves, or to put their profits into one fund and divide it by a fixed rule. In a **cartel**, independent firms agree to fix prices or limit output, so that they do not compete.^1 In both, each firm keeps its own owners and its own name. Because such agreements may raise prices against buyers, many countries forbid cartels, or limit them by law.

^1: Books vary a little in how they use "pool" and "cartel", and some treat them as the same. This book keeps them apart as above.

@ Trust | A combination in which the owners of several firms hand their shares to trustees, who then control the firms together.

@ Holding company | A company that owns enough shares in other companies to control them.

> Closer forms: a trust or a holding company controls firms by shares.

A tighter form uses shares. In a **trust**, the owners of several firms hand their shares to a small group of trustees, who then control all the firms together; the owners receive certificates and a share of the joint profit. In a **holding company**, one company buys enough shares in others to control them. The controlled firms, called *subsidiaries*, stay separate in law and keep their names, but their policy is set by the holder. This form has two merits: the holder needs to buy only a majority, not everything, and the firms keep their local goodwill.

@ Merger | A combination in which one firm takes over another, which then ceases to exist as a separate firm.

@ Amalgamation | A combination in which two or more firms are dissolved and a new firm takes over all of their business.

> Complete forms: in a merger one firm absorbs another; in an amalgamation a new firm is born.

The tightest forms make the firms one. In a **merger**, one firm takes over another, which then ceases to exist as a separate firm. If firm A takes over firm B, then A continues and B disappears. In an **amalgamation**, two or more firms are dissolved and a new firm takes over all of their business. If A and B amalgamate, both disappear and a new firm C appears.^2 The shareholders of the old firms receive shares in the continuing or the new firm, and the old firms' staff and assets pass to it.

^2: In daily speech "merger" is often used for both. The difference is whether an existing firm continues or a new one is formed.

:table Forms of business combination
| Form | How the firms join | Do the firms stay separate? |
| Pool | Agree to share market or profits | Yes |
| Cartel | Agree on prices or output | Yes |
| Trust | Owners hand shares to trustees | Yes in law; one control |
| Holding company | One company owns a controlling share of others | Yes in law; one control |
| Merger | One firm absorbs another | No; the absorbed firm ends |
| Amalgamation | Old firms dissolve; a new firm is formed | No; all old firms end |

@ Horizontal combination | A joining of firms that work at the same stage of making and selling a product.

@ Vertical combination | A joining of firms that work at different stages of making and selling a product.

> The second question: are the firms at the same stage or at different stages?

Now the second question, which cuts across the first. A **horizontal combination** joins firms that work at the same stage of making and selling a product, for instance two bakeries. A **vertical combination**, also called *vertical integration*, joins firms that work at different stages, for instance a bakery and a flour mill. If the bakery buys its supplier, the move is *backward*; if it buys the shops that sell its bread, the move is *forward*. A third kind joins firms in unrelated lines, such as a bank and a biscuit maker; it is called a *conglomerate*, and it chiefly spreads risk.

:table Horizontal and vertical combination
| Kind | Stage | Example from the bakery | Main aim |
| Horizontal | Same stage | Bakery joins another bakery | Fewer rivals, shared cost |
| Vertical, backward | Earlier stage | Bakery buys a flour mill | Secure supply |
| Vertical, forward | Later stage | Bakery buys a chain of cafés | Secure sales |
| Conglomerate | Unrelated | Bakery joins a transport firm | Spread risk |

> Remember that the two questions combine: a merger may be horizontal or vertical.

Keep the two questions apart. A merger of two bakeries is a merger by form and horizontal by stage. A holding company that owns a flour mill and a bakery is a holding company by form and vertical by stage. When you meet a combination, name it twice: say what form it takes, and say what stage it joins.

## Opportunities and Threats

> Combinations offer gains to firms, and some to buyers and workers.

A combination can bring gains. The firms may lower their cost per unit, as in our example. They may gain a larger share of the market and a stronger voice with suppliers, who will cut prices to win a large customer. They may share research, machines and skilled staff. They may reach new markets. Buyers may gain too, if lower costs become lower prices, or if the firm can afford better quality. And a weak firm may be saved, with its jobs.

> Combinations also bring dangers, chiefly less competition and less choice.

@ Monopoly | A market in which one seller supplies all or nearly all of a good.

The dangers are as real. The chief one is less competition. If rivals combine until few remain, the firm may fix high prices, since buyers have nowhere else to go. A firm that is the only seller of a good is a **monopoly**. Buyers may also lose choice and service. Workers may lose jobs, since two firms joined need fewer staff than before. Management may fail: two different ways of working may clash, and a very large firm may be slow. And a firm that pays too much for another, or borrows heavily to buy it, may be left with a burden. The table sets out the main points by party.

:table Opportunities and threats of combination
| Party | A possible opportunity | A possible threat |
| The firms | Lower cost, larger share, shared research | Clash of methods, heavy debt, slow management |
| Buyers | Lower prices, better quality | Higher prices and less choice if rivals vanish |
| Workers | Safer jobs in a stronger firm | Job losses where work is duplicated |
| The nation | Stronger firms that can sell abroad | Power in few hands; weaker small firms |

> Governments therefore make rules to keep competition alive.

Because of the dangers, governments make rules about combinations. A competition law may forbid cartels, forbid a firm from abusing a monopoly, and require large mergers to be reported and approved. Bangladesh has a competition law and a Competition Commission to enforce it.^3 The aim is not to forbid all combinations, which can serve the public, but to stop those that harm buyers.

^3: The Bangladesh Competition Act was passed in 2012. Check the present law and the rules on which mergers must be reported.

> Recap: gains, dangers and rules.

*Before moving on:* a combination may lower costs and strengthen firms, but it may also reduce competition and jobs, so governments make rules. You should now be able to give one gain and one danger of a merger.

## The Worked Case: The Rival Bakery

> The bakery's merger with its rival, 2030.

:case Case file, illustrative. 2030. After the public issue of Chapter XII, the bakery company has 3,00,000 shares of Tk 10 issued: 1,20,000 held by the first owner, 80,000 by the second partner, and 1,00,000 by the public. Authorised capital is 5,00,000 shares. The rival bakery is valued at Tk 15,00,000. The bakery proposes to take over the rival's business and to pay the rival's owners with new shares of Tk 10, valued at par. All figures are invented.

> The case asks what kind of combination this is and who controls the result.

*Situation.* The bakery will take over its nearest rival and pay in shares. *Question.* What kind of combination is this, how many shares are issued, and who controls the firm afterward?

> We name the form, count the shares, and find each group's percentage.

*Reasoning.* The two firms bake the same product, so the combination is horizontal. The bakery continues and the rival's business is absorbed, so the form is a merger, not an amalgamation. The rival is worth Tk 15,00,000, and each new share is Tk 10, so the bakery issues 15,00,000 ÷ 10 = 1,50,000 shares. Total issued shares become 3,00,000 + 1,50,000 = 4,50,000, still below the authorised 5,00,000. Holdings: partners 2,00,000 ÷ 4,50,000 = 44.4 per cent (the first owner 26.7, the second partner 17.8); the rival's owners 1,50,000 ÷ 4,50,000 = 33.3 per cent; the public 1,00,000 ÷ 4,50,000 = 22.2 per cent.

> The merger adds size but costs the original owners their majority.

*Conclusion.* The merger removes the nearest rival and enlarges the firm, but the partners fall from 66.7 per cent (Chapter XII) to 44.4 per cent and lose their majority. No single group holds more than half. The partners must decide whether the gain in size and sales is worth the loss of control, and whether the rival's owners will vote with them. They should also check the competition law if the combined firm becomes large in its market. *Check.* 44.4 + 33.3 + 22.2 = 99.9, which is 100 after rounding. And 4,50,000 shares at Tk 10 is Tk 45,00,000 of issued capital.

## Summary

> The chapter's main points in a few lines.

A business combination is a joining of firms for common control or policy. Firms combine for growth, lower cost, security of supply and sales, spread of risk, and survival. The forms are the pool and cartel (agreements), the trust and holding company (control by shares), and the merger and amalgamation (one firm). Combinations are horizontal, vertical or conglomerate according to the stage they join. They offer lower cost and strength but threaten competition and jobs, so governments make rules.

## Where People Go Wrong

> Five confusions, each stated and corrected.

Some treat a merger and an amalgamation as the same, though one firm continues in the first and a new firm is born in the second. Others say that every combination is a monopoly; most are not. A third error is to take a cartel for a holding company: the cartel is an agreement among independent firms, and the holding company controls through shares. Another is to say that a larger firm always has lower cost, when very large firms may suffer diseconomies. The last is to say that vertical means larger; it means a different stage.

## In History

> A trust of 1882 gave its name to the fear of combined power.

In 1882 the shareholders of the oil companies run by John D. Rockefeller in the United States handed their shares to a board of trustees, who controlled the whole refining business as one. The arrangement, called the Standard Oil Trust, gave its name to the whole class of combination, and the public came to fear "trusts" as power in a few hands. In 1890 the American Congress passed the Sherman Antitrust Act against such combinations, and in 1911 the Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil to be broken into separate companies.
