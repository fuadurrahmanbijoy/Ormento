:chapter V | Choosing a Form of Ownership; The Sole Proprietorship
:mood Who owns the business, and who pays when it fails?
:desc Every business belongs to someone, and the way it belongs decides who has power, who takes profit and who answers for debt. This chapter surveys the main forms of ownership and then studies the simplest form in full: ownership by one person.

> A failed shop owes more than it owns, and someone must pay the difference.
Picture a small bakery that has failed, in a town far from Dhaka. Its oven, tables and trays are sold, and together they bring Tk 3,00,000. The bakery owes Tk 5,00,000 to flour sellers and to a bank. Two lakh taka remain unpaid. Who must find that money? The flour sellers may have to accept a loss. The bank may have to accept a loss. Or the baker may have to pay from savings, from a plot of land, even from the family home.

> The answer lies in a choice made on the first day, not on the day of failure.
Nothing in the shop itself tells us which answer is right. The answer lies in a decision the baker made long before: how the business would be owned. One business can be owned in several ways, and each way shares out profit, power and debt differently. This chapter surveys the main ways and then studies the simplest one. By the end you will be able to say who must pay the missing Tk 2,00,000, and why.

> This chapter uses ideas from Chapters I and II.
*You will need.* From Chapter I: owner, profit, loss, risk and entrepreneur. From Chapter II: capital and the four factors of production. Chapter III and Chapter IV are not needed, though the bakery's figures from them appear once in the worked case.

## Why the Form of Ownership Matters

> The same shop can be owned in different ways, and the way changes the rules.
@ Form of ownership | The legal way a business is owned and controlled.
Our bakery from Chapter I could be owned in three different ways. One person could own it alone. Two friends could own it together. Or a hundred people could each own a part. The oven, the recipes and the customers would be the same in all three cases. What would change is the set of rights and duties that the owners hold. We call this set the **form of ownership**: the legal way a business is owned and controlled.

> Six questions reveal the difference between any two forms.
To compare forms fairly, we ask the same six questions of each one. First, who owns the business? Second, who takes decisions? Third, who receives the profit and who bears the loss? Fourth, who must pay what the business owes? Fifth, how can the business raise more capital? Sixth, what happens to the business when an owner leaves or dies? The fourth question is the one that troubled our failed baker, and Section 2 teaches it with care.

> Five forms exist; this table is only a map to the chapters that teach them.
The table below lists the five main forms found in Bangladesh. Read each row from left to right: the form, the people who own it, and the chapter where it is taught. The table is a map, not a lesson. Do not try to learn the later forms now.

> No form is best for every business; the right one depends on needs.
No form is best for every business. A tea stall that needs Tk 5,000 of capital has different needs from a factory that needs Tk 5,00,00,000. A person who wants full control will choose differently from one who wants to share risk. The first choice is also not final. Firms change form as they grow, and our running bakery will change form twice in later chapters. Chapter VIII, Section 5 sets all five forms side by side once you know each of them.

## The Sole Proprietorship

### Meaning

> The simplest way to own a business is to own it alone.
@ Sole proprietorship | A business that one person owns and controls.
The simplest way to own a business is to own it alone. A **sole proprietorship** means a business that one person owns and controls.^1 The word *sole* means *only*. The tea stall of Chapter I was a sole proprietorship. So is a tailor who works from a rented room, and so is our bakery from the day it opens on 1 March 2026.

> Sole describes the owner, not the staff; many such firms have employees.
The word *sole* describes ownership, not staffing. A sole proprietorship may have no helpers, or it may have many. Our bakery has four staff and one owner. The owner is the entrepreneur of Chapter I: the person who brings together land, labour and capital, and who accepts the risk. Often the owner also does part of the work, such as baking or serving.

> In law, the firm and its owner are one and the same person.
The law treats the firm and its owner as one person. A sole proprietorship has no separate legal existence apart from the person who owns it.^2 If the firm signs a contract, the owner signs it. If the firm owes money, the owner owes it. This single fact explains the most important feature of the form, which we meet below under the heading of liability.

### All the Profit and All the Loss

> The owner keeps every taka of profit and also bears every taka of loss.
No one else shares in the business, so no one else shares in its results. Recall the tea stall of Chapter I. On a good day it earned a profit of Tk 300, and on a poor day it made a loss of Tk 100. Both numbers belonged to the owner alone. This gives the owner a strong reason to work hard, because every extra taka of profit is the owner's. It also leaves the owner with no one to share a bad month.

### Unlimited Liability

> We begin with ordinary life: a small loan from a neighbour.
@! Go slowly here.
@ Debt | Money that one person must pay to another.
@ Creditor | A person to whom money is owed.
This idea is new and important, so we take it one step at a time. Begin at home, not in business. Suppose you borrow Tk 1,000 from a neighbour. The money you must pay back is a **debt**, and the neighbour who lent it is your **creditor**. A debt means money that one person must pay to another. A creditor means a person to whom money is owed.

> You must repay a debt even if the plan for the money fails.
Now suppose you spend the Tk 1,000 on goods for a small stall, but the goods sell for only Tk 600. Your plan has failed, yet the debt is still Tk 1,000. You pay Tk 600 from the sales. You must pay the other Tk 400 from your own pocket. The neighbour does not care that the stall failed. The neighbour cares only that the debt was agreed.

> Liability is the legal duty to pay a debt.
@ Liability | A legal duty to pay a debt. Word in use: in daily life it can mean blame; here it means only the duty to pay.
The law has a word for this duty to pay. **Liability** means the legal duty to pay a debt. A person who has liability for a debt can be made to pay it, by a court if necessary. In this book the word never means blame. It means only the duty to pay.

@ Unlimited liability | A duty to pay all the firm's debts, using the owner's own property if the firm's things are not enough.
> The sole proprietor's duty covers all the firm's debts and reaches the owner's own property.
Now return to business. In a sole proprietorship the firm and the owner are one person. So the firm's debts are the owner's debts. The creditors are paid first from the things the business owns. If that is not enough, they may claim the owner's own property as well. We call this **unlimited liability**: the owner's duty to pay all the firm's debts, with no limit set by the size of the business.

> In the opening puzzle, the baker must pay the missing Tk 2,00,000.
Now we can answer the puzzle that opened this chapter. The failed bakery owned things worth Tk 3,00,000 and owed Tk 5,00,000. The shortfall was Tk 2,00,000. If the bakery was a sole proprietorship, the baker must pay that shortfall from personal property, such as the plot of land. Some other forms of ownership protect the owner's personal property from business debts. Chapter VII teaches this protection, which is called limited liability. A sole proprietorship has no such protection.

> Unlimited does not mean the debt is unlimited; it means the reach is.
There is a common confusion here. Unlimited liability does *not* mean that the owner owes more than the debts. The debts stay exactly what they were: Tk 5,00,000 in our example, no more. What is unlimited is how far the creditors may reach to collect it. Which personal property can be taken is set by law and by a court, so a real owner in trouble should seek advice from a lawyer.

> Pause and check: apply the shortfall rule to new numbers.
*Pause and check.* A grocer's sole proprietorship owes Tk 60,000. The shop's things can be sold for Tk 40,000. The grocer owns other personal property worth Tk 50,000. How much must the grocer pay from personal property, and how much personal property remains? *(Answer at the back of the book.)*

> You should now be able to state the rule in one sentence.
*You should now be able to say:* "If a sole proprietorship cannot pay its debts from the business's own things, the creditors may claim the owner's other property."

### Control, Size and Life

> One owner means quick decisions, but also no one to share the work.
The owner decides alone. A grocer who sees that rice has become cheaper can lower the price within minutes, with no meeting and no vote. The same freedom means that every decision, good or bad, is the owner's own. There is also no second person to ask.

> The firm can grow only as far as the owner's money and skill allow.
The firm's size is limited by what one person can supply. The capital comes mainly from the owner's own savings, with perhaps some borrowing. The skill and the hours come from the owner too. Large amounts of capital and large teams are therefore rare in this form.

> The firm has no life apart from its owner.
The firm's life is tied to the owner's. If a tailor falls ill for six months and has no one to run the shop, the business stops earning. If the owner dies, the firm in law ends, though family members may start a new firm with the same shop and tools. A form with more owners can continue when one of them leaves, as Chapter VI shows.

:box IN SHORT
A sole proprietorship is a business with one owner. The owner decides alone, keeps all the profit, bears all the loss and answers for all the debts with everything the owner has.

> Recap: one owner, all profit and loss, unlimited liability, and a short life.
*Before moving on.* Remember four facts: one owner; the owner takes all profit and all loss; the owner has unlimited liability; the firm's life is tied to the owner.

## Merits, Limits and Suitable Fields

### Merits

> The form is cheap and quick to start.
This form is the easiest to start. The owner needs money, a place and a trade licence, which Section 4 describes. No one else must agree, and no long agreement must be written. A person with Tk 50,000 and a skill can open within days. For this reason it is the form that most new traders choose first.

> One owner means fast and flexible decisions.
Decisions are fast and flexible. The owner can change the menu, the price or the opening hours at once. A bakery owner who sees that customers want brown bread can bake it tomorrow. Larger forms often need meetings and agreement before such changes.

> The owner keeps all the profit, which gives a strong reason to work well.
The owner keeps all the profit. This gives a strong reason to work carefully, to waste little and to serve customers well. A paid manager may feel less urgency, because the profit is not the manager's.

> Close contact with customers builds a good name.
@ Goodwill | The value of a firm's good name and loyal customers. Word in use: in daily life, a kind feeling; in business, a good name that has a money value.
The owner often serves customers in person. A shopkeeper who knows each customer's taste and treats each fairly earns trust. That trust is called **goodwill**: the value of a firm's good name and its loyal customers. Goodwill belongs entirely to the owner. If the owner sells the shop, a buyer may pay extra for the goodwill.

> Plans stay private to the owner.
A sole owner need not share plans or results with co-owners, because there are none. The business can try a new idea quietly. The owner must still give the tax office what the law requires, but no partner or shareholder has a right to see the books.

### Limits

> Capital is limited to the owner's savings plus what lenders will give.
The main limit is capital. A firm that wants to grow needs more money, and one person's savings run out. Lenders also hesitate, because a small firm with one owner has little to offer as proof that it will repay. Our bakery began with Tk 12,00,000 of the owner's own savings. That is enough for one shop. It does not allow a second shop without more money from somewhere else.

> A failed firm can cost the owner personal property.
Unlimited liability is the second limit. As Section 2 showed, a firm that fails can take the owner's land, savings and other property with it. This risk makes careful owners cautious. It also prevents many people from starting at all.

> One person's skill, time and judgement are not enough for every job.
A third limit is the owner's own ability. One person cannot be an expert baker, buyer, accountant and seller at the same time. The owner works long hours with little rest. Mistakes in areas where the owner is weak can harm the whole firm.

> The table sets each merit beside the limit it brings with it.
Many merits and limits are two sides of the same fact. The table below pairs them. Read each row from the left: the feature, the merit it gives, and the limit that comes with it.

:table Merits and limits of the sole proprietorship
| Feature | Merit | Limit |
| Control | Quick decisions, no meeting needed | No second opinion to check mistakes |
| Profit and loss | Owner keeps every taka of profit | Owner bears every taka of loss |
| Capital | Easy to begin with small savings | Hard to raise large amounts later |
| Liability | Full responsibility builds trust | Personal property is at risk |
| Life | Simple to start and to close | Firm depends on owner's health and presence |

### Suitable Fields

> One-owner firms suit small, personal and low-capital trades.
A sole proprietorship suits work that one person can manage and that needs little capital. Examples are a neighbourhood grocery, a tailor, a barber, a vegetable seller, a bicycle repairer, a private tutor and a small bakery like ours. These trades share four features: they need small capital, they depend on personal service, they serve local customers, and a single failure rarely causes huge debts. The form is a poor fit for work that needs very large capital, such as a steel mill or an airline.

### Why Small Owners Survive Beside Large Firms

> A puzzle: why do small shops survive when large firms are so strong?
Large firms can buy in bulk and use costly machines. We might expect small owners to disappear. Yet in every neighbourhood we still find small shops, and they survive. Several reasons explain this.

> Small owners win on nearness, service and flexibility.
The first reason is nearness. A neighbourhood grocery is open early and late and a short walk away. The second is service. The owner knows the customers and may let a known neighbour pay at the month's end. The third is flexibility. A tailor can make one garment to the customer's exact size, which a factory making thousands of garments will not do. The fourth is low cost. The owner's own work and family help cost little, and a small rented room costs less than a large building.

> Large firms often depend on small owners to reach customers.
Large firms also leave room for small ones. Many small shops sell the goods that large firms make, and carry them to places a factory cannot reach. In this way small and large firms do not only compete; they also need each other.

> Our firm sees the capital limit; a new setting shows it more sharply.
We now test the capital limit in a new setting. A furniture maker in Narayanganj owns a workshop alone and has Tk 4,00,000 in savings. A new machine would double the output, but it costs Tk 15,00,000. The savings cover little more than a quarter of the cost. No matter how skilled the owner is, one person's savings cannot buy it. This is the point where many sole proprietors look for a second owner or other sources of capital. Chapter VI takes up the next step.

> Recap: merits and limits come in pairs; small owners survive by nearness and service.
*Before moving on.* The merits are easy start, quick decisions, all the profit and goodwill. The limits are small capital, unlimited liability and one person's skills. Small owners survive by nearness, service and flexibility.

## Starting and Running the Business

> A trade licence is the basic permission needed to open.
@ Trade licence | Written permission from a local government to run a trade in its area.
The first requirement in Bangladesh is a **trade licence**: written permission from local government to carry on a trade in its area.^3 In a city the licence comes from the city corporation. Outside the cities it comes from the pourashava (the municipality) or from the union parishad. The licence is issued in the owner's name, and it is valid for one year, so it must be renewed every year. A business that trades without a licence may be penalised or stopped.

> Other steps follow the licence: a bank account, tax registration and special permits.
Other steps follow. The owner usually opens a bank account for the business and obtains a tax number from the National Board of Revenue. If the sales are large enough, the owner must also register for the VAT of Chapter III. Some trades need further permits, such as those that sell food, which the owner should check with the authority in charge of that trade. A firm that imports or exports needs other registrations, which Chapter X explains.

> The owner runs every part of the firm and should keep clear records.
The owner manages every part of the firm: buying, making, selling, paying staff and keeping records. As the firm grows, the owner gives some jobs to staff but remains responsible for the whole. One good habit protects the owner from confusion. The owner should keep the business's money apart from the household's money. When the two are mixed, the owner cannot tell what the business earns. In law the two are not separate when a debt must be paid, but in records they should always be kept apart.

> Recap: licence, bank, tax registration, then daily management and clear records.
*Before moving on.* Start with the trade licence. Then open a bank account and register for tax. Run every part of the firm, and keep business money apart from household money.

## Worked Case: The Bakery Opens

> The case file shows the choice of form and the first month.
:case Case file, illustrative. On 1 March 2026 the bakery opens as a sole proprietorship. The owner has obtained a trade licence from the city corporation and has put in Tk 12,00,000 of the owner's own savings as capital. Sales in the first month, to 31 March 2026, are Tk 2,40,000. For a test only, we then suppose that, after some time, the bakery failed with business things that sell for Tk 3,00,000 and debts of Tk 5,00,000, while the owner holds land worth Tk 6,00,000 in the owner's own name.^4

> The question has three parts: form, first month, and the failure test.
*Question.* Why is the sole proprietorship a suitable form for the bakery on 1 March 2026? Do the first month's sales look healthy? If the bakery failed as supposed, how much would the owner pay, and how much would remain?

> The form fits one owner with own capital and a small, personal trade.
*Reasoning, step 1: the form.* The bakery has one owner. The owner supplies all Tk 12,00,000 of capital from savings. The trade is personal, local and small. These are the features that suit a sole proprietorship. A second owner joins later, on 1 July 2026, which Chapter VI follows.

> The first month's sales are above the target set in Chapter III.
*Step 2: the first month.* Chapter III tested the bakery's sales against rent, flour and the owner's profit target, and found that Tk 1,37,400 was needed. The first month's sales were Tk 2,40,000. The difference is Tk 2,40,000 minus Tk 1,37,400, which is Tk 1,02,600. That amount is available for the costs the test ignored, such as staff wages. It is not all profit. Whatever is left after every cost belongs to the owner alone.

> Business things are sold first; the owner's property covers the rest.
*Step 3: the failure test.* The creditors are paid first from the business things. Debts are Tk 5,00,000 and the things bring Tk 3,00,000, so the shortfall is Tk 5,00,000 minus Tk 3,00,000, which is Tk 2,00,000. Under unlimited liability the owner must pay this from other property. The land is worth Tk 6,00,000. The owner sells part of it and pays Tk 2,00,000. The land left is Tk 6,00,000 minus Tk 2,00,000, which is Tk 4,00,000.

> The form suits the bakery today, with a clear risk for the owner.
*Conclusion.* The sole proprietorship suits the bakery on opening day: one owner, own capital, a licence, and a healthy first month. The risk is also clear. In the failure test the owner would lose the business things and Tk 2,00,000 of personal property.

> A check adds the parts back to the original debt.
*Check.* Business things Tk 3,00,000 plus the owner's payment Tk 2,00,000 equals Tk 5,00,000, which is the full debt. The creditors are paid in full. No debt remains.

### Where People Go Wrong

> Mistake one: thinking unlimited liability means unlimited debt.
Many students write that unlimited liability means the owner owes an unlimited amount. This is wrong. The debts are whatever the firm owes. What is unlimited is the reach of the creditors, who may use the owner's other property.

> Mistake two: thinking sole means alone.
Another common error is to believe that a sole proprietorship has no employees. It may have many. *Sole* describes the owner, of whom there is only one.

> Mistake three: thinking a licence gives separate legal status or protection.
A third error is to think that holding a trade licence protects the owner's personal property. It does not. The licence only gives permission to trade. In a sole proprietorship the owner remains liable for all debts.

### In History

> Medieval craft masters owned their own workshops, in a form like the sole proprietorship.
Long before modern companies, much trade was done by owners of small workshops. In medieval Europe, from about the twelfth century in France and Italy, craftsmen formed guilds. A worker rose from apprentice to journeyman, then to master. To become a master he had to show a piece of work, called a masterpiece, to the guild, and he had to be able to fund his own workshop and tools.^5 The master then owned the shop, trained apprentices and answered for the quality of what it made. The guild checked standards, which protected customers. This early form shows why one-owner firms have lasted: they suit crafts where skill, trust and personal responsibility matter more than large capital.
